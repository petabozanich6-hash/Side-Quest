Rounding numbers: which landmark is nearer?

S2 · Mathematics

Your mission

Use distances on a number line to round numbers to a ten or a hundred, and explain a midpoint decision.

Learning intention

We are learning to choose the nearest multiple of ten or hundred using endpoints, distances and midpoints.

Success criteria

  • I can name the two neighbouring tens or hundreds.
  • I can compare the distances to both endpoints.
  • I can round a midpoint upwards using the agreed rule.
  • I can explain why the rounding place changes my answer.

1. Find the neighbouring tens

Rounding to the nearest ten means choosing the closest multiple of ten.

A rounded number is a useful approximate value, not a new exact count.

Multiples of ten end in zero: 210, 220, 230. Locate your number between two neighbours. For 224, these are 220 and 230. The word nearest asks which endpoint has the shorter distance. We are not always making a number bigger. We are making it close to a useful landmark. When someone needs an exact count, keep the original number instead of replacing it with the rounded value.

Example

WATCH MY THINKING
For 224, I choose 220 and 230 as my endpoints. I count four units back to 220 and six units forward to 230. Four is the shorter distance, so 224 rounds to 220. The actual number remains 224.

YOUR SMALL TRY
Which two neighbouring tens surround 237? Write both before comparing with the suggestion.

COMPARE AFTER TRYING
230 and 240 are consecutive multiples of ten and 237 is between them. 220 and 240 would span two tens, so they are not neighbours.

Notice

A common mistake is choosing any two numbers ending in zero, such as 200 and 230. Use consecutive multiples with only one ten between them, and make sure the original number sits between them.

2. Compare distances to both tens

The nearest endpoint is the one with the smaller distance from the starting number.

Counting both distances explains the choice instead of relying on a guessed direction.

Subtract the lower endpoint from the number to find one distance. Subtract the number from the upper endpoint for the other. The two distances add to ten because there are ten units between the neighbouring tens. A number left of the midpoint is closer to the lower endpoint; a number right of it is closer to the upper one. Do not compare how large the endpoints are. Compare how far away each is.

Example

WATCH MY THINKING
For 368, the endpoints are 360 and 370. The distances are 368 minus 360 = 8, and 370 minus 368 = 2. Since 2 is smaller than 8, I round to 370. The distances add to 10, which confirms I used neighbouring tens.

YOUR SMALL TRY
Round 432 to the nearest ten using both distances. Make your choice before reading on.

COMPARE AFTER TRYING
432 is 2 from 430 and 8 from 440. It rounds to 430 because the lower endpoint is closer, not just because it is lower.

Notice

A common mistake is choosing the lower endpoint simply because its value is smaller. Compare the two distances, not the sizes of the endpoints themselves.

3. Use hundreds and recognise a tie

For the nearest hundred, use neighbouring multiples of one hundred; at an exact midpoint we round upwards.

The midpoint rule gives everyone the same answer when both endpoints are equally near.

Hundred landmarks end in two zeros. There are one hundred units between neighbours, so their midpoint is fifty units from either end. For 450 the endpoints are 400 and 500, not 440 and 460. Both distances are 50. Neither endpoint is nearer, so we use the agreed upward rounding rule. This is a convention for a tie, not proof that the upper endpoint is closer. Below the midpoint choose the lower hundred, and above it choose the upper hundred.

What happens at the midpoint?

Pip: I have 450 on a line from 400 to 500. Is 500 closer?
Juno: Calculate both distances. Each is fifty, so neither endpoint is closer.
Pip: Then I name the tie and use our agreed upward midpoint rule.
Juno: Pause and explain why a midpoint rule is different from choosing a shorter distance.

Example

WATCH MY THINKING
For 450, I find 400 and 500. I calculate 450 minus 400 = 50 and 500 minus 450 = 50. I name the tie, then use our upward midpoint rule to round to 500.

YOUR SMALL TRY
Round 550 to the nearest hundred. Is either endpoint actually nearer?

COMPARE AFTER TRYING
550 is 50 from both 500 and 600. It rounds to 600 by the midpoint rule; neither endpoint has a shorter distance.

Notice

A common mistake is saying the upper endpoint is nearer at the midpoint. State that the distances are equal, then name the upward rounding convention.

4. Choose the scale before calculating

The requested place determines the landmarks: tens are ten apart and hundreds are one hundred apart.

The same starting number can have different rounded values because the useful scale has changed.

Read the instruction before drawing your line. For 726 to the nearest ten, use 720 and 730; its distances are 6 and 4. For the nearest hundred, use 700 and 800; its distances are 26 and 74. Both answers are reasonable approximations for different purposes. Write the original number, the rounding place and your chosen value so a reader knows what you did. Do not round to tens first and then to hundreds; make each decision directly from the original number.

Example

WATCH MY THINKING
I round 726 directly to a ten: 730 is 4 away, closer than 720 at 6 away. I start again with the original 726 for a hundred: 700 is 26 away, closer than 800 at 74 away. My two answers use different scales.

YOUR SMALL TRY
Round 584 directly to the nearest ten and directly to the nearest hundred. Record each pair of endpoints.

COMPARE AFTER TRYING
For tens, 580 and 590 give distances 4 and 6, so choose 580. For hundreds, 500 and 600 give distances 84 and 16, so choose 600. Both decisions start with 584.

Notice

A common mistake is rounding to a ten first and then rounding that result to a hundred. Return to the original number for each place. For 449, direct hundred rounding gives 400; rounding through 450 would give the wrong hundred.

Worked example

I DO: Round 286 to the nearest ten, and separately to the nearest hundred.
Tens: 280 and 290 bracket 286. Distances are 6 and 4; choose 290.
Hundreds: 200 and 300 bracket the original 286. Distances are 86 and 14; choose 300.
I label the rounding place beside each answer. Neither calculation changes the exact starting number.
For a tie example, round 735 to a ten: 730 and 740 are each 5 away. The upward rule selects 740.

Guided practice

WE DO: Round 314 to the nearest ten. Write the endpoints and both distances before continuing.
Compare after trying: 310 and 320; distances 4 and 6. Choose 310 because its distance is smaller. If you chose 320, draw ten equally spaced units and locate 314 before the midpoint.

Now round 850 to the nearest hundred. Name the tie before choosing.
Compare after trying: 800 and 900; distances 50 and 50. Neither is closer. Use the upward tie rule to select 900. If you said 900 was closer, correct the reason to equal distances and the agreed rule.
