# Stage 2 / Year 3 Mathematics — Add across a hundred without losing place value
**Status:** individually authored curriculum draft; not yet connected to a verified queue seed, not validator-certified, browser-tested, NESA-signed-off or deployed. **Reference:** [NESA Mathematics K–10 (2022) outcomes](https://curriculum.nsw.edu.au/learning-areas/mathematics/mathematics-k-10-2022/outcomes). Outcome mapping is provisional pending content-point review. Child-facing material appears before the parent-only section.

**Proposed outcome:** MA2-AR-01 (mental and written strategies for adding and subtracting two- and three-digit numbers); MAO-WM-01 (reasoning and communication). **Time:** 50–60 minutes.

## Child mission
A library has 178 books and receives 46 more. Can you find the new total using a number line, not guesswork? You'll learn to split numbers into useful jumps and explain why the answer makes sense.

**Success checks:** I can split an addend into tens and ones; bridge the next hundred accurately; record jumps; check an answer with estimation and an inverse calculation.

### Teach 1 — Number lines show addition as forward movement
**Rule:** Start from one number and move forward by the value of the second. **Why:** Addition combines quantities without changing the size of either quantity.
**I do:** 143 + 25: jump +20 to 163, then +5 to 168. The two jumps total 25; I did not add 20 *and* 25.
**Common error:** Starting the +5 jump at 143 rather than 163. **Fix:** Every jump starts at the last landing point.
**Your small try:** 152 + 24. Write 152 → 172 → 176. Compare your working: +20 then +4 ends at 176.
**Quick check:** Why isn't 152 + 24 equal to 196? Because counting the 20 twice makes too large a total.

### Teach 2 — Bridge to a friendly hundred
**Rule:** Split the second number so the first jump lands exactly at 200, 300 or another helpful hundred. **Why:** Hundreds are easier landmarks for mental arithmetic.
**I do:** 178 + 46: 178 needs 22 to reach 200. Split 46 into 22 + 24. So 178 + 22 = 200; 200 + 24 = **224**.
**Common error:** Using 46 after the first jump instead of the remaining 24; this double-counts 22. **Fix:** Label the whole addend as 22 + 24 first.
**Your small try:** 186 + 27. Need 14 to reach 200, with 13 remaining: 200 + 13 = **213**.
**Quick check:** Explain why a split into 14 + 27 would be invalid for 186 + 27.

### Teach 3 — Adding tens first and bridging are equivalent
**Rule:** You may add 40 then 6, or 22 then 24; both decompositions total 46.
**I do:** 178 + 40 = 218; +6 = 224. Check against 178 + 22 + 24 = 224. The path changes but the destination does not.
**Why it matters:** Different strategies suit different starting numbers. A bridge might be easier near a hundred; tens first can be easier elsewhere.
**Common error:** Moving place values across columns, e.g. calling 178 + 40 = 182. **Fix:** Four tens increase the tens place by four; they do not add four ones.
**Your small try:** 265 + 38: 265 + 30 + 8 = 303; or 265 + 35 + 3 = 303.
**Quick check:** Which strategy would you choose for 199 + 26, and why? 199 + 1 + 25 = 225 is efficient.

### Teach 4 — Check that the answer is reasonable
**Rule:** Estimate first; verify by subtraction afterward.
**I do:** For 178 + 46, estimate 180 + 50 ≈ 230. Exact answer 224 is nearby. Verify 224 − 46 = 178. If the subtraction failed, revisit each jump.
**Common error:** Treating a rounded estimate as the exact answer. **Fix:** Label estimates with ≈ and exact answers with =.
**Your small try:** 289 + 36 = 325. Estimate 290 + 40 ≈ 330; check 325 − 36 = 289.
**Quick check:** Why would 289 + 36 = 425 be unreasonable? It's about 100 above a sensible estimate.

## Worked example — watch
A fundraiser collected 167 tickets on Monday and 58 on Tuesday. Split 58 into 33 + 25. From 167 jump +33 to 200, then +25 to **225**. Verify 225 − 58 = 167.

## Guided practice — try before comparing
1. **194 + 37.** How much reaches 200? 6. How much of 37 is left? 31. **Compare:** 194 → 200 (+6) → **231** (+31). If you reached 237, you probably added all 37 again.
2. **258 + 67.** Tens first: +60 → 318, then +7 → **325**. **Compare:** You can alternatively add +42 to 300, then +25 to 325.

## Independent evidence — new numbers, no model answers here
1. **176 + 59:** Show a split that crosses 200 and explain the two jumps.
2. **287 + 48:** Show tens-first and bridging strategies; explain why both give the same total.
3. **395 + 27:** Choose the most efficient strategy and justify it.
4. A club has **168** members and enrols **75** more. Write a number sentence, estimate, calculate, and check by subtraction.
Type your solutions or write them on paper and submit a photo/brief typed explanation. Label any estimate.

## Knowledge check — eight multiple-choice questions
1. 148 + 20 = ? A 150; **B 168**; C 348; D 128. *Feedback:* Twenty is two tens, so 148 becomes 168.
2. What bridges 189 to 200? A 9; **B 11**; C 21; D 111. *Feedback:* The difference 200 − 189 equals 11.
3. Split 35 after adding 12. Remaining? A 12; B 35; **C 23**; D 47. *Feedback:* 12 + 23 = 35.
4. 196 + 28 = ? A 204; B 218; **C 224**; D 234. *Feedback:* +4 reaches 200, leaving +24.
5. 274 + 40 = ? A 278; **B 314**; C 674; D 234. *Feedback:* Four tens increase 274 by 40.
6. 157 + 65 = ? A 212; **B 222**; C 322; D 192. *Feedback:* 157 + 43 = 200, then +22 = 222.
7. Which checks 326 + 28 = 354? **A 354 − 28 = 326**; B 354 + 28 = 326; C 326 − 354 = 28; D 28 − 326 = 354. *Feedback:* Subtraction reverses addition.
8. A sensible estimate for 288 + 41 is? A 120; B 230; **C 330**; D 530. *Feedback:* 290 + 40 is approximately 330.

**Written check A:** Explain two different ways to calculate 189 + 47 and why they agree.
**Written check B:** Correct a learner who writes 197 + 36 = 233 by adding +3 to 200, then mistakenly adding +36 again.

## Hints — three increasingly specific supports for every learner stage
- **Mission:** Focus on crossing the next hundred → identify a starting number and addend → ask what gets you from 178 to 200.
- **Teach 1:** Addition moves forward → split into tens and ones → for 143 + 25 use +20 and +5.
- **Teach 2:** Look ahead to the next hundred → subtract the starting number from that hundred → 200 − 178 = 22, leaving 24 of the 46.
- **Teach 3:** Try two splits of the same addend → each split must total 46 → 40 + 6 and 22 + 24 both total 46.
- **Teach 4:** Approximate the size first → undo addition by subtraction → 224 − 46 should equal 178.
- **Worked:** Follow each labelled jump → account for every part exactly once → 33 + 25 must total 58.
- **Guided:** Compare your first jump with 200 → find the remaining addend → for 194 + 37, 6 + 31 = 37.
- **Independent:** Label your chosen strategy → record split and each landing point → check the last answer by subtracting the second addend.
- **Quiz:** Rule out impossible scales → check tens versus ones → 196 + 28 should be just above 220.
- **Submit:** Include an estimate and a check → label each independent number → make your explanation understandable without your verbal help.

## Parent-only answer key and assessment
1. 176 + 59 = **235**: +24 to 200, +35 to 235.
2. 287 + 48 = **335**: +40 → 327 → +8 → 335; or +13 → 300 → +35 → 335.
3. 395 + 27 = **422**: +5 to 400, +22 to 422.
4. 168 + 75 = **243** members; estimate about 170 + 70 = 240; check 243 − 75 = 168.
Written A: 189 + 11 + 36 = 236, or 189 + 40 + 7 = 236. Written B: 197 + 3 + 33 = 233; 233 is actually correct, but the stated *method* of adding 36 again would yield 236, so ask the learner to explain the discrepancy and correct the method rather than rejecting a correct total.
**Evidence:** Compare the child's initial and corrected reasoning, independent decomposition and inverse checks. Strong work explains *why* a split preserves the addend. **Extension:** Design a three-digit addition where tens-first seems harder than bridging, then defend your choice. **Screen-free:** Draw jumps on scrap paper and talk through them before entering the result.
