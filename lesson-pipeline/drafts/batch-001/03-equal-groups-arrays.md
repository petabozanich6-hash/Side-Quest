## Child mission
You are arranging 24 seedlings in neat rows. Instead of counting every seedling one by one, use equal groups and multiplication. Show why 4 rows of 6 equals 24.

**Success:** I can distinguish equal from unequal groups, read an array as rows × columns, connect repeated addition to multiplication, turn an array and explain what changes or stays the same, and solve unfamiliar group problems.

### Teach 1 — Multiplication describes equal-sized groups
**Rule:** 4 groups of 6 means 6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24, written 4 × 6 = 24.
**Why:** Instead of counting 24 objects one by one, group structure lets us count more efficiently.
**Model:** Four trays each hold six seedlings. Count six for each tray: 6, 12, 18, 24. Four is the number of groups; six is the size of each group.
**Common error:** Calling groups of 3, 4 and 5 an equal-group multiplication. **Fix:** Each group must have exactly the same number of items.
**Try:** Three bags contain five marbles each. Add 5 + 5 + 5 = 15, so 3 × 5 = **15**.
**Check:** How would you show 2 × 7 with repeated addition? 7 + 7.

### Teach 2 — Arrays organise equal groups into rows and columns
**Rule:** In an array, rows run across and columns run down. Three rows of four has 3 × 4 = 12 items.
**Why:** Rows and columns display the structure without hiding any items.
**Model:** Draw three horizontal lines with four dots on each. Count 4, 8, 12. The first factor identifies the row count in this sentence.
**Common error:** Drawing four in the first row but only three in the next. **Fix:** Check that each row has the same number of objects.
**Try:** Draw two rows of five and write 2 × 5 = **10**.
**Check:** How many dots are needed in four rows of three? 12.

### Teach 3 — Turn an array; the total stays equal
**Rule:** A 3 × 4 array can be turned into a 4 × 3 array, with twelve objects in both.
**Why:** Turning a rectangle moves the same objects without adding or removing any. This shows the commutative property.
**Model:** 3 rows of 4 = 12; 4 rows of 3 = 12. The descriptions change, but the total is unchanged.
**Common error:** Thinking 4 × 3 must be different from 3 × 4 because the words are reversed. **Fix:** Count the actual objects in each orientation.
**Try:** Draw 2 rows of 6, turn the drawing (or redraw 6 rows of 2), then explain why both give **12**.
**Check:** Does turning two rows of seven create new items? No.

### Teach 4 — Solve problems with the right group size
**Rule:** Identify the number of groups and items in each before multiplying.
**Why:** Different situations can use the same multiplication facts, but an incorrect model can give an incorrect answer.
**Model:** Five tables seat four people each. Draw five groups of four: 5 × 4 = 20 seats. Do not add 5 + 4 = 9.
**Common error:** Using multiplication when the groups are not all equal; one table with two seats would change the total. **Fix:** First check whether the situation really says 'each'.
**Try:** Seven packs contain three stickers each: 7 × 3 = **21**.
**Check:** If two packs have different amounts, what information is missing? The actual size of each pack.

## Worked example
A garden has six rows with four seedlings per row. Each row has four, so repeated addition is 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 24. The multiplication equation is **6 × 4 = 24**. Turning the array shows 4 × 6 = 24 without changing the number of seedlings.

## Guided practice
1. Make **five groups of three** counters (or pencil dots). There are 15 counters. Your equation: 5 × 3 = **15**. If your count differs, inspect each group's size.
2. Draw a **3-row by 6-column** array. Total **18**. Write a second equation by turning it: 6 × 3 = **18**. If the total changes, check that no dot was left out.

## Independent evidence
1. Draw **4 rows of 7** and give its repeated addition and multiplication equations.
2. Explain whether **3 × 8** and **8 × 3** have the same total, using labelled diagrams.
3. Six packets each hold five seeds. How many seeds? Explain why addition of just 6 + 5 is incorrect.
4. A display has three shelves with four objects each, but another shelf contains only two objects. Explain why **4 × 4** is not the total and calculate the actual number.

## Eight multiple-choice checks
1. 3 groups of 4 = ? A 7; **B 12**; C 34; D 9. *Feedback:* Count four three times to make twelve.
2. Which shows 4 × 5? **A 5 + 5 + 5 + 5**; B 4 + 5; C 4 + 4 + 4 + 4; D 5 × 5. *Feedback:* Four groups contain five objects each.
3. Two rows of six contain? A 8; **B 12**; C 26; D 16. *Feedback:* Six plus six equals twelve.
4. Which arrangement is an equal-group model? A groups of 2,3,4; **B three groups of 5**; C groups of 1,2,3; D one group of 3 and one of 8. *Feedback:* Every group must contain the same number.
5. 7 × 3 = ? A 10; **B 21**; C 73; D 27. *Feedback:* Seven equal groups of three contain twenty-one.
6. Turning an array will? A double the total; B halve it; **C keep the same total**; D remove a row. *Feedback:* Turning rearranges the same objects without altering their count.
7. Four rows of seven = ? A 11; B 21; **C 28**; D 47. *Feedback:* Seven added four times totals twenty-eight.
8. Why is 5 + 4 not the total seats at five four-seat tables? A addition is never used in multiplication; **B it combines two numbers rather than five groups of four**; C five tables have five people each; D four is larger than five. *Feedback:* Model all groups to find the total rather than adding the two factors.

**Written A:** Draw two different arrays for 18 dots. Name the rows and columns and explain why both contain 18.
**Written B:** A learner says 3 packets of 6 and 6 packets of 3 have different totals. Evaluate the statement using diagrams.

## Hint ladders — three distinct supports for each screen
- **Mission:** Think equal packets → count groups and per-group size → four trays of six is 4 × 6.
- **Teach 1:** Repeated addition matters → use the same addend for every group → 5 + 5 + 5 represents three bags of five.
- **Teach 2:** Rows run across → inspect the number of dots in each row → three rows each containing four is 3 × 4.
- **Teach 3:** Turn without changing items → compare rows and columns after rotation → 3 × 4 and 4 × 3 both count 12 dots.
- **Teach 4:** Read the word 'each' → identify groups and items per group → seven packs of three is 7 × 3.
- **Worked:** Trace one row at a time → count six groups of four → 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4 + 4.
- **Guided:** Draw the array before writing an answer → count dots per row and rows → for 3 by 6, use 6 + 6 + 6.
- **Independent:** Label both dimensions → count in equal-sized jumps → compare your total to a turned or repeated-addition model.
- **Quiz:** Look for equal group sizes → eliminate equations that add just the factors → four groups of seven requires four sevens.
- **Submit:** Attach or describe your array → include group size and group count → explain at least one answer using both addition and multiplication.

## Optional story episode
**Pip:** “I have five tables with four seats each. Five plus four means nine seats!”
**Juno:** “Draw all five tables. How many chairs did you put at each one?”
**Pip:** “Four. I can count four, eight, twelve, sixteen, twenty. Oh—twenty seats!”

**Pause:** Say what each factor means in 5 × 4.

## Parent-only answer key
1. 4 × 7 = **28**: 7 + 7 + 7 + 7 = 28.
2. 3 × 8 = 8 × 3 = **24**; rotation changes orientation, not count.
3. 6 × 5 = **30**; 6 + 5 gives 11 and ignores the repeated groups.
4. 3 × 4 + 2 = **14**, not 4 × 4 = 16.
Written A: examples 3 × 6 and 2 × 9; both contain 18. Written B: 3 × 6 and 6 × 3 both equal 18; a rotated dot array demonstrates why.
Assess whether the learner can build and explain equal groups, label dimensions and identify mixed-size cases. **Extension:** Show all rectangular arrays for 24 without repeating rotations; explain why some numbers make more arrays than others. **Offline:** Use dried beans or drawn circles to build and rotate arrays. **Safety:** Small counters require supervision where younger children are present.
