# Year 5: Plan and draft a connected narrative

Stage 3 · Year 5 · English · Week 1 · Lesson 2 · 60 minutes

Seed key: `s3-eng-y5-w01-l2`. Review status: unreviewed. Original text. Exact outcome codes remain empty pending verified mapping.

## Accept the quest

Become a Story Architect. Build a character's goal, test it with an obstacle and shape choices that lead to a believable ending.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to plan and draft a narrative whose events connect through a character's choices.

- I can introduce a character, setting and goal.
- I can develop a problem with connected attempts and consequences.
- I can write an ending that follows from an earlier choice.
- I can revise one weak connection in my draft.

**Materials:** This lesson contains the texts and examples you need; Optional: paper and a pencil

### Help with this step

1. Read the mission and choose a small story problem you can picture.
2. The lesson will help you plan before drafting; begin with the opening step.
3. Keep notes about a character wanting to do something. You can choose your setting after the examples.

## Teaching 1: 🏗️ Build an opening with a purpose

**Give the reader somewhere to begin.**
A narrative is a story. Its opening, or orientation, introduces a character and useful information about the place and situation. A detailed opening does not need to describe everything the character owns.

**Introduce a goal.**
A goal is something the character wants to achieve. It gives the reader a reason to follow the next events. “Eli wanted to finish a drawing” is clearer than “Eli was a person.”

Choose a setting detail that matters. If the hall opens in ten minutes, the time creates pressure. A list of unrelated colours may picture the room but will not establish the goal.

**Draft in two small moves.**
First introduce someone doing something in a place. Then show what they want. You can combine the moves, but drafting them separately helps when you are beginning.

**Try and check.**
Read your opening and ask a reader's question: “What is this character trying to do?” If your own words do not answer it, add a specific goal.

### Show me an example

I DO:
At the community hall, Eli balanced a cardboard model on the display table. He wanted to show how its moving part worked before the visitors arrived.

My first sentence places Eli and the model. My second names a goal and a time pressure. I leave out the colour of every chair because it does not help this opening.

COMPARE:
“Eli had blue shoes, a hat, a table and a bag.” This lists possessions but leaves the reader guessing about the story's purpose.

YOU TRY:
Start a story about a child preparing a display. Write one place-and-action sentence and one goal sentence.

CHECK:
There are many answers. Check for a named character, a place and an intended achievement. A detail matters when it helps the reader understand the situation.

**Notice:** A setting description should help the story, not postpone it indefinitely. Keep the goal specific enough to create a meaningful problem.

**Question:** Which opening gives a character a clear goal?

1. Eli had a shirt, shoes and a bag
2. There were many chairs in the hall
3. Eli wanted to demonstrate his model before visitors arrived
4. The table was beside a chair

**Reviewer answer:** Eli wanted to demonstrate his model before visitors arrived

**Explanation:** Wanting to demonstrate the model before visitors arrive names a specific achievement. The other choices give details without a goal.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the opening's two small moves.
2. Separate place-and-action information from what the character wants.
3. Ask what success would look like. Add that intended achievement to your opening.

## Teaching 2: ⚙️ Make an obstacle matter

**Put something between the character and the goal.**
An obstacle makes the goal harder to reach. The complication is the story's problem or disruption. It should matter to the goal rather than appear as an unrelated surprise.

If Eli wants to demonstrate a model, a jammed moving part is a relevant obstacle. A distant event that never affects him may be dramatic but will not help this plot.

**Show the effect.**
Do not only name the obstacle. Show what the character cannot do now: the part will not turn, so Eli cannot demonstrate the action. This gives the next choice a reason.

**Plan a connected attempt.**
The character tries something in response. Its consequence, or result, may solve part of the problem or make another attempt necessary. You can use the planning link “Because ____, the character decides ____.”

**Keep the size manageable.**
For a short narrative, one main problem with two connected attempts is enough to practise. You do not need a new disaster in every sentence.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Obstacle: Eli's model wheel jams.
Effect: He cannot demonstrate the moving part.
First attempt: He pushes the wheel harder.
Consequence: The cardboard bends, so pushing harder is not useful.

The sequence connects. The obstacle affects the goal, the attempt responds to the obstacle and the result gives Eli a reason to change his approach.

COMMON MISTAKE:
“His wheel jams. Then he wins a prize in another town.” The new event does not explain how he deals with this problem.

YOU TRY:
A character wants to label a display, but the marker runs out. Name one relevant attempt and a possible result.

CHECK:
Borrowing a pencil or making raised-letter labels could be relevant choices. Explain how the attempt changes what the character can do; avoid solving a different problem.

**Teaching diagram:** A planning chain starts with demonstrating a model, meets a jammed wheel, and connects an attempt to a result and new choice.

*Each action should respond to what happened before.*

**Notice:** An attempt can fail usefully: it should reveal something or lead to the next decision. Random events do not automatically create a connected plot.

**Question:** Eli wants to demonstrate his model. Which obstacle most directly affects that goal?

1. His model's moving part jams
2. An unrelated street changes its name
3. A visitor owns a red bag
4. The hall has a window

**Reviewer answer:** His model's moving part jams

**Explanation:** A jammed moving part prevents the demonstration. The other details do not establish a relevant barrier to that goal.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the link between obstacle and goal.
2. Ask what the character cannot do because of the problem.
3. Use “Because ____, the character decides ____.” Test whether the decision addresses this obstacle.

## Teaching 3: ↪️ Earn the turning point and ending

**Let the character respond to what they learn.**
A turning point changes the direction of the problem. For this draft, make it a discovery or decision that leads to a new approach. The resolution shows how the main problem settles.

**Prepare the solution.**
If a character uses a useful object or idea at the end, introduce it earlier or explain where it comes from. A solution that suddenly appears without explanation can leave a reader confused.

This does not mean every ending must be predictable. A surprising solution can still follow from earlier information.

**Connect action to result.**
Eli might notice that a folded edge is blocking the wheel. Straightening that edge can let it move. Finding the blockage changes the approach from pushing harder to checking the model.

**Test the ending.**
Ask, “Would this ending still make sense if I removed the turning-point choice?” If the answer is no, the connection is probably important. Show what happens to the original goal.

**Original in-site episode:** Build an ending from a choice

**Pip:** My ending says the model works, but I have not explained how the character fixes it.

**Juno:** Look back at the obstacle. What does the character notice, choose and change?

**Pip:** I can show the action and its consequence instead of jumping straight to success.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Earlier detail: Eli knows the cardboard wheel should spin freely.
Discovery: He notices a folded edge touching it.
New choice: He straightens the edge instead of pushing harder.
Resolution: The wheel turns and he can demonstrate it.

I prepared the ending with the model's design. I did not make a stranger appear with a perfect new model at the last moment.

YOU TRY:
A child cannot hang a display label. Earlier, she put spare string in her bag. What could a connected solution use?

CHECK:
Using that spare string follows from the earlier detail. You still need to explain what she does and how it solves the hanging problem. Owning string alone is not a resolution.

**Notice:** The ending should deal with the original goal, even if the character only partly succeeds. Avoid an unexplained rescuer doing everything.

**Question:** Which planning choice best prepares a solution?

1. Inventing a perfect helper in the final sentence with no explanation
2. Introducing a useful item earlier, then showing how the character uses it
3. Ending before the character acts
4. Adding a new goal after every sentence

**Reviewer answer:** Introducing a useful item earlier, then showing how the character uses it

**Explanation:** Introducing and using a relevant item gives the solution an understandable connection. The other choices avoid or disrupt that connection.

### Help with this step

1. Find the earlier detail that can support a later solution.
2. Compare the first attempt with the changed approach.
3. Ask what the character notices and does differently. Then show its effect on the original problem.

## Teaching 4: 📝 Draft, then revise the connection

**Turn notes into a readable story.**
Notes help you remember the plot, but a draft gives a reader the experience of it. Use sentences to show actions and reactions. Start a new paragraph when an important event or focus changes.

**Use a manageable sequence.**
For this task, write an opening, problem and first attempt, new approach, and ending. These are useful drafting sections, not a rule that every story must have exactly four paragraphs.

**Reread for gaps.**
Ask why each important action happens. If the reason exists only in your head, add it to the draft. If an attempt fails, show what the character notices or chooses next.

**Revise one point before polishing everything.**
Revision changes meaning or structure. Editing checks details such as spelling and punctuation. Both matter, but fixing a missing cause comes before decorating an unclear plot.

**Use a success checklist.**
Check for a clear goal, relevant obstacle, connected attempts and a resolution linked to a choice. Then improve one place where the link is weak.

### Show me an example

I DO:
First draft: “The wheel jammed. Eli used his ruler. The model worked.”

I know what I mean, but the reader does not. How does a ruler help? I revise the middle:
“Eli saw a folded cardboard edge pressing against the wheel. He used the ruler's straight edge to smooth the fold. When he turned the wheel again, it moved.”

The extra explanation connects the tool, action and result. I then check capitals and full stops.

YOU TRY:
Revise “The label fell. Ana used string. It stayed up.” Explain what Ana does with the string.

CHECK:
A possible revision shows Ana tying the string through the label's hole and around the display hook. The reader can now follow how her action helps.

**Notice:** Do not confuse adding more adjectives with fixing a plot gap. Add the missing action or reason first.

**Question:** What is a useful revision when a draft jumps from a tool to a solved problem?

1. Add unrelated colour words
2. Remove the character's goal
3. Repeat the same unclear sentence
4. Explain how the character uses the tool and what happens

**Reviewer answer:** Explain how the character uses the tool and what happens

**Explanation:** Explaining the action and its consequence repairs the missing connection. Unrelated detail, removing the goal or repeating the gap does not help the reader understand it.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the draft with a missing connection.
2. Find what the reader needs to know about the action.
3. Explain how the tool or choice changes the problem before adding decorative detail.

## Put it all together

I DO: A complete short draft
THE WHEEL THAT WOULD NOT TURN

At the hall, Eli placed his cardboard model on a table. Visitors would arrive in ten minutes. He wanted to demonstrate how its wheel turned.

When he tested it, the wheel stuck. He pushed harder, but the cardboard bent. Eli pulled his hand away. Another push might make the model worse.

He leaned close and followed the wheel's edge. A folded strip of cardboard pressed against it. Instead of pushing the wheel, he used the straight edge of his ruler to smooth the strip. He tried a gentle turn.

The wheel moved. When a visitor reached the table, Eli could show the moving part and explain his repair. He left the ruler beside the model for the next demonstration.

HOW THE PARTS CONNECT
The opening establishes a goal. The jam blocks it. Pushing fails and makes Eli stop. Looking closely reveals the blockage, so he changes his approach. The repair enables the demonstration.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“We have a jam, then the model works” skips the action. The model draft includes what Eli notices, what he changes and how he checks the result. A ruler is introduced as the tool he uses, not as a magic solution.

**Teaching diagram:** Four drafting sections connect a person and goal, an obstacle and attempt, a new choice and result, and the goal's outcome.

*Use the sections to guide a draft, not to force every story into a fixed formula.*

### Help with this step

1. Read the model story before its explanation.
2. Trace the goal, jam, failed push, investigation and repair in order.
3. Choose one action and explain what earlier event gives it a reason.

## Try it with help

WE DO: Plan a different display story.
Prompt: A child wants visitors to read a display label, but the label keeps falling.
1. Name a character and goal. Try your own wording. Suggestion: Ana wants her display label readable before visitors arrive.
2. Choose one obstacle that blocks it. Suggestion: the tape does not hold the label.
3. Name an attempt and result. Suggestion: more tape still fails, so Ana examines the display hook.
4. Prepare a useful earlier detail. Suggestion: she has string in her bag.
5. Show a changed approach and ending. Suggestion: she ties the label to the hook with string, checks that it is readable and is ready for visitors.
Now draft two connected sentences about the change. Starter: “When Ana noticed ____, she decided ____. After she ____, ____.”
Check that the reader can follow how the choice addresses the falling-label problem. Another sensible solution is fine.

### Help with this step

1. Begin with the falling-label goal before choosing a solution.
2. Write one obstacle and one attempt with a result.
3. Use the two-sentence starter to show what Ana notices and chooses next.

## Build your cause-and-choice plan

Use short notes in all three boxes. Include the action and reason, not just one-word headings.

**Character, place, goal and obstacle**

Who wants to do what, where, and what prevents it?

**Attempt, consequence and changed approach**

What fails or reveals something? What will the character choose next?

**Ending and its preparation**

How does a choice affect the goal? What earlier detail prepares that solution?

### Help with this step

1. Start with the first box and one specific goal.
2. Add what prevents that goal, then move to the attempt box.
3. Write “My character wants ____, but ____.” Use those notes to choose a relevant first attempt.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Draft a connected narrative (about 20–25 minutes)
Choose the falling-label prompt or invent another small community-display problem.
1. Complete your three plan boxes. Give your character one clear goal and a relevant obstacle.
2. Draft about 180–250 words, or a shorter complete story if you need more practice. Use the four sections in the model to help organise events.
3. Show an attempt and consequence, then a changed approach. Prepare a useful idea or item before relying on it in the ending.
4. Read the draft and explain why each important action happens. Revise one missing link.
5. Check: goal clear; obstacle relevant; choices connected; ending shows the goal's outcome; capitals and sentence endings checked.
Type your draft in the big box or write on paper and type a concise summary plus the two sentences you revised. Keep paper work for review; an upload is optional.
The suggested word range is a guide. A coherent complete plot matters more than filling space.

**Reflect:** Which revision made your plot connection clearer, and how?

### Help with this step

1. Look back at your plan and draft only the opening first.
2. Begin “At ____, ____ wanted ____.” Add the obstacle after that situation is clear.
3. A possible opening shape is “At the display, my character checked ____. They hoped ____.” Fill the gaps from your own plan.

## Quest check

Eight multiple-choice questions and two written responses. The unchanged 90% threshold requires 8/8 scored choices. The written responses accompany the submission for manual review.

**Question:** What should an opening help a reader understand?

1. Every item the character owns
2. A character, useful setting and goal
3. The exact ending before anything happens
4. Only the room's colours

**Reviewer answer:** A character, useful setting and goal

**Explanation:** A purposeful opening establishes the character's situation and goal. Listing possessions or colours alone does not establish why the story matters.

**Question:** Why should an obstacle connect to the goal?

1. It must contain a long word
2. It must happen in another town
3. It makes the character's intended achievement harder
4. It should remove every choice

**Reviewer answer:** It makes the character's intended achievement harder

**Explanation:** A relevant obstacle blocks or complicates what the character wants to achieve, giving subsequent actions a reason.

**Question:** What is a consequence?

1. The result of an action or event
2. The title's font
3. A list of possible names
4. A description of every object

**Reviewer answer:** The result of an action or event

**Explanation:** A consequence is a result. It helps explain what a character may need to do next.

**Question:** Why is Eli pushing the wheel harder a useful failed attempt?

1. It guarantees every story must include a ruler
2. It removes the problem permanently
3. It describes his clothing
4. The bent cardboard gives him a reason to change his approach

**Reviewer answer:** The bent cardboard gives him a reason to change his approach

**Explanation:** The result shows pushing harder is unhelpful and prompts a different decision. A failed attempt matters when its consequence shapes what follows.

**Question:** Which change is the turning point in the model?

1. Visitors have shoes
2. Eli stops pushing and investigates the blocked edge
3. The table has a surface
4. The story has four sections

**Reviewer answer:** Eli stops pushing and investigates the blocked edge

**Explanation:** Investigating the blockage changes Eli's approach to the jammed wheel. The other details do not explain that change of direction.

**Question:** How can a writer prepare an ending?

1. Introduce a useful detail and show how it later helps
2. Avoid explaining the problem
3. Make every event unrelated
4. Skip all attempts

**Reviewer answer:** Introduce a useful detail and show how it later helps

**Explanation:** An earlier useful detail gives a later solution a foundation. The writer still needs to show the relevant action and result.

**Question:** Which is a structural revision rather than only spelling editing?

1. Checking the letters in wheel
2. Changing one misspelt name
3. Adding an explanation of how an action solves the problem
4. Checking a capital letter

**Reviewer answer:** Adding an explanation of how an action solves the problem

**Explanation:** Explaining an action's role improves the story's meaning and connections. The other choices check surface details.

**Question:** Which ending addresses the original demonstration goal?

1. Eli suddenly wants a different model with no explanation
2. The table is still a table
3. The last paragraph lists colours
4. Eli demonstrates the repaired moving part to a visitor

**Reviewer answer:** Eli demonstrates the repaired moving part to a visitor

**Explanation:** The demonstration shows how the original goal is achieved after the repair.

**Question:** Explain how your planned obstacle affects your character's goal.

Written response, reviewed by a grown-up.

**Reviewer model:** Ana wants visitors to read her display label, but the label keeps falling, so they may not see it.

**Review guide:** Accept a specific goal and a relevant obstacle with an explanation of how it matters.

**Explanation:** A strong response explains the connection rather than merely listing a person and an object.

**Question:** Describe one revision that makes an action-and-result connection clear.

Written response, reviewed by a grown-up.

**Reviewer model:** I changed 'Ana used string' to show her tying the label to the hook, so the reader understands why it stays up.

**Review guide:** Accept an actual or proposed revision adding a relevant action or reason. Require its effect on clarity.

**Explanation:** A strong response shows what information the reader needs and how the revision supplies it.

3. For a written explanation, state the goal and obstacle or show the information a revision adds. Use your own example.
