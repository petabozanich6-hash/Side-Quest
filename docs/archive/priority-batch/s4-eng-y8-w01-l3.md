## Teaching 1: Find the subject and verb in each clause

**A clause contains a subject and a verb.**
The subject identifies who or what the clause is about; the verb expresses action or state. In “Kai checked the table”, Kai is the subject and checked is the verb.

**A sentence can contain more than one clause.**
“Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands” contains two subject–verb units. A long list of objects does not automatically create extra clauses.

**A verb group may contain several words.**
“Sam was holding the board” has the verb group was holding. Those words work together within one clause; counting verb-shaped words alone can overcount clauses.

Mark subject and verb before naming structure. This gives evidence for a clause count instead of judging only the sentence’s length.

I DO:
“Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands.”
Unit one: Sam / held.
Unit two: Kai / needed.
“Sam was holding the board” is one subject with one multiword verb group.

YOU TRY:
Identify the subject and verb in “The sign was visible”.

CHECK:
The sign is the subject; was is the verb connecting it with the description visible. A verb can express state rather than movement.

Diagram: A clause diagram identifies a main action and a dependent reason, with a comma after an introductory subordinate clause.

Structure is about clause relationships, not length.

Notice: Do not count every word in a verb group as a separate clause.

**Question:** Which is the verb group in “Sam was holding the board”?

1. Sam was
2. Holding the board Sam
3. Was holding
4. Board only

Reviewer answer: Was holding

Why: Was holding works as the clause’s verb group with Sam as subject.

### Help with this step

1. Look for who or what the statement concerns.
2. Find the action or state words working together.
3. Mark one subject–verb unit before deciding whether another clause begins.

## Teaching 2: Distinguish main and subordinate clauses

**A main clause can stand as a complete statement in these models.**
“Sam held the board” gives an independent idea. A subordinate clause depends on another clause for the complete sentence relationship.

**A subordinating conjunction marks the relationship.**
Because Kai needed both hands gives a reason but leaves a reader waiting for the main statement. Words such as because, although and when can introduce dependent relationships.

**A complex sentence combines a main and a subordinate clause.**
“Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands” connects action with reason. Complex describes clause structure, not how difficult or long the sentence looks.

Read the clauses separately to test their jobs, then together to check the complete meaning. Not every conjunction makes the same kind of structure.

I DO:
Main: Sam held the board.
Subordinate: because Kai needed both hands.
Complete: Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands.

YOU TRY:
Why is “Because Kai needed both hands” incomplete as the whole task sentence?

CHECK:
It gives a reason but not the main action linked to that reason. Add the main clause rather than only a full stop.

Notice: A capital and full stop do not supply a missing main clause.

**Question:** Why is “Because Kai needed both hands” incomplete here?

1. It contains no letters
2. It provides a dependent reason without its main statement
3. Every sentence must be long
4. Because can never occur in writing

Reviewer answer: It provides a dependent reason without its main statement

Why: The clause depends on a main statement to complete the reason relationship in this task.

### Help with this step

1. Read the clause by itself and notice what the relationship expects next.
2. Separate the dependent reason from the main action.
3. Attach a suitable main statement so the sentence expresses the complete relationship.

## Teaching 3: Choose a conjunction for the intended relationship

Juno: Pause and identify each subject and verb before reordering your own clause pair.

Learner pause: the final line pauses before continuing.

**Different links carry different meanings.**
Because introduces reason; when can indicate time; although introduces contrast or concession. Replacing one with another can change the relationship even if the surrounding words stay similar.

**Check the intended logic.**
“Although the sign fell, the display was ready on time” contrasts a setback with a completed outcome. “The sign fell because the fan faced the table” suggests a cause; in the story this is an inference, so qualify it if making an analytical claim.

**Preserve the level of certainty.**
For analysis, “The sign may have moved because the fan faced the table” avoids claiming an unobserved cause as proven. For original fiction, an author can establish a cause through the chosen narration.

Choose the relationship before choosing the conjunction. A fluent sentence can still express the wrong reason, time or contrast.

I DO:
Reason: “Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands.”
Time: “Kai found the sign when Sam suggested checking the gap.”
Contrast: “Although they lost time, the display was ready.”

YOU TRY:
Link a setback with a successful outcome using a contrast relationship.

CHECK:
“Although the sign fell, the display was ready on time” preserves the contrast. A reason conjunction would express a different connection.

Notice: The link changes meaning. Check whether the clause expresses the relationship you intended.

**Question:** Which word introduces contrast in the taught models?

1. Because
2. When
3. And only
4. Although

Reviewer answer: Although

Why: Although introduces a concession or contrast between the dependent clause and the main outcome.

### Help with this step

1. Name the intended relationship before picking a linking word.
2. Distinguish reason, time and contrast.
3. Read the full sentence and check that the conjunction preserves that relationship.

## Teaching 4: Reorder clauses and punctuate the model

**The subordinate clause can come first.**
“Because Kai needed both hands, Sam held the board.” In this model, a comma marks the end of the introductory subordinate clause before the main statement.

**The main clause can come first too.**
“Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands.” In this model, no comma is needed before the following reason clause. Other clause types can use different comma patterns; keep this task to the demonstrated structures.

**Avoid fragments and comma splices.**
Do not leave “Because Kai needed both hands” alone. Do not join two independent statements with only a comma and call that a complex sentence. Our target needs a subordinate relationship as well as a main clause.

Reorder one sentence and check that the cause, time or contrast remains clear. Punctuation should help the reader follow the structure, not replace a missing clause.

I DO:
Main first: “Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands.”
Subordinate first: “Because Kai needed both hands, Sam held the board.”
The meaning is preserved while the introductory clause is marked with a comma.

YOU TRY:
Move “although they lost time” to the beginning of a sentence about the display being ready.

CHECK:
“Although they lost time, the display was ready.” The comma follows the introductory clause; the main statement remains complete.

Notice: Check both structure and marks. Punctuation cannot turn an unfinished reason into a complete thought.

**Question:** Which follows the demonstrated introductory-clause pattern?

1. Because Kai needed both hands, Sam held the board.
2. Because, Kai needed both hands Sam held the board.
3. Because Kai needed both hands.
4. Sam held, because the board.

Reviewer answer: Because Kai needed both hands, Sam held the board.

Why: The introductory subordinate clause is followed by a comma and a complete main statement.

### Help with this step

1. Identify the end of the introductory dependent clause.
2. Keep the main statement complete after it.
3. Use the model’s comma boundary and read both clauses together for meaning.

## Put it all together

I DO: Build and edit a complete set of complex sentences.
Reason: Sam held the board because Kai needed both hands.
Reason, reordered: Because Kai needed both hands, Sam held the board.
Time: Kai found the sign when Sam suggested checking the gap.
Time, reordered: When Sam suggested checking the gap, Kai found the sign.
Contrast: Although they lost time, the display was ready.
Contrast, main first: The display was ready although they lost time.

CLAUSE LABELS
Sam / held and Kai / needed are two subject–verb units in the first sentence. Because introduces a dependent reason; Sam held the board is the main statement. Was ready is a verb group expressing a state in the contrast model.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“Because the sign fell.” gives a dependent reason without its main statement. Repair: “Because the sign fell, Kai checked behind the board.” The repair supplies the main action and marks the introductory boundary.

WHY IT WORKS
The linking word expresses the intended relationship, both clauses are present, and the punctuation follows the demonstrated order. Reordering does not replace the reason with a different event.
