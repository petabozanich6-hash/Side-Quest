# DRAFT for review: Lesson 1, Spelling Detective (explicit teaching version)

This is a review draft only. Nothing in the app reads this file. If the style is right, the same text becomes the teaching content of the lesson.

Pitched at a Stage 2 reader (about 8 to 10 years old). Each chapter is meant to be read slowly, aloud if you like, with a pencil in hand.

---

## Chapter 1. Why spelling is not random

A lot of people think English spelling is a pile of random rules that somebody invented to trick you. It feels that way when you write *friend* and it looks wrong, or when you wonder why *knife* starts with a letter you never say. But spelling is not random. It is built from three layers, and good spellers use all three without even noticing.

The first layer is **sound**. Most words are written to match the sounds you hear. The word *chimp* has four sounds, ch, i, m and p, and each sound is written down. If you can hear the sounds, you can often write the word.

The second layer is **meaning**. Many words are built out of smaller pieces that each carry a meaning. Look at *unhappy*. It is made of *un*, which means not, and *happy*. You do not need to learn *unhappy* as a brand new word. You already know both pieces, so you can spell it and understand it at once.

The third layer is **history**. Some words are spelled strangely because they came from other languages or were pronounced differently hundreds of years ago. *Said*, *friend* and *because* belong here. They do not follow the sound or the pieces, so for these words we need a different tool: memory.

So a spelling detective has a toolkit with four tools: sounds, syllables, word parts, and a memory method. The skill is not knowing every word. The skill is looking at a word and asking, "Which tool fits this one?" That is what the rest of this lesson teaches.

**Try it:** Say the word *shop*. How many separate sounds can you hear? Put a finger up for each one. (Answer: three, sh, o, p. Notice that the sound *sh* is made by two letters, but it is only one sound.)

**Check:** Which layer of spelling does the word *unhappy* use?
- Sound
- Meaning (word parts)
- History

Answer: meaning, because it is built from the pieces un and happy.

## Chapter 2. Tool one: listening to sounds

Every word is a chain of sounds, and each separate sound is called a **phoneme**. Writing a word is like building that chain one link at a time. You say the word slowly, listen for each sound, and write a letter or a group of letters for it.

Here is the first important idea: the number of sounds is not always the number of letters. The word *ship* has four letters but only three sounds, sh, i and p. Two letters, *s* and *h*, work together to make one sound. A pair of letters that make one sound is called a **digraph**. You already know many: *sh* in ship, *ch* in chop, *th* in thin, *ng* in ring, *ck* in duck and *ph* in phone.

The second important idea is that one sound can often be written in more than one way. Say the long *a* sound, the one in the middle of *rain*. You can write that sound as *a_e* in cake, *ai* in rain or *ay* in play. Which one is right depends on the word. When you are not sure, do what detectives do: gather the suspects. Write the word each possible way on scrap paper and ask yourself which one looks like a word you have seen before. Your eyes know more than you think.

Here is a quick example. You want to write the word for something that falls from the sky when it is wet. You hear r, long a, n. You try *rane*, *rain* and *rayn*. *Rain* looks right, because you have read it many times. You have used sound to get close and your eyes to finish the job.

When does sounding out fail? It fails on words that are not spelled the way they sound. If you sound out *said* you get *sed*. That is a signal that a different tool is needed, and we will meet that tool in Chapter 6.

**Try it:** Write the word *chimp*. First say it slowly and count the sounds (ch, i, m, p is four). Now count the letters (five). Why are the numbers different? (Because *ch* is a digraph.)

**Check:** How many sounds are in the word *thing*? (th, i, ng, three.)

## Chapter 3. Tool two: syllables

Sounding out works well for short words. For longer words it is like trying to eat a whole apple in one bite. The trick is to cut it into pieces. In spelling, the pieces are called **syllables**.

A syllable is one beat in a word. You can feel the beats by putting your hand under your chin and saying the word. Your chin drops once for each beat. *Cat* has one beat. *Rab-bit* has two. *But-ter-fly* has three. *Un-for-get-ta-ble* has five. Every syllable has at least one vowel sound, which is a good way to check yourself.

Here is how to use syllables to spell. Say the word and find the beats. Then write one beat at a time, saying each part as you write it. A long word turns into several small, easy words.

There is also a pattern worth knowing. When a short vowel sound is followed by another syllable, the middle of the word often has a double letter. Listen to *rabbit*: rab-bit. The short *a* is followed by a break, and the word needs a double *b*. The same thing happens in *ribbon*, *ladder*, *mitten* and *summer*. When you hear a short vowel in the middle of a longer word, try doubling the letter that follows it and see if it looks right.

Some long words are really two short words stuck together. These are **compound words**. *Sunshine* is sun plus shine. *Football* is foot plus ball. Spell each piece, then join them, and no letters are lost.

**Try it:** Clap the beats in *adventure* (ad-ven-ture, three) and *wonderful* (won-der-ful, three). Then write each one, a syllable at a time.

**Check:** How many syllables are in *elephant*? (el-e-phant, three.)

## Chapter 4. Tool three: word parts, prefixes first

Now for the tool that makes the biggest difference. Many words are built from a **base word** with extra pieces added. The base word is the main part, the part that carries the core meaning, and it can often stand on its own. In *replaying*, the base word is *play*.

A piece added to the front is called a **prefix**. A prefix changes the meaning, and here is the great news for spellers: a prefix never changes how the base word is spelled. You simply put the pieces together and keep every letter.

Here are five prefixes worth knowing.
- **un-** means not or the opposite. Unhappy, unlock, unkind.
- **re-** means again. Replay, rewrite, rebuild.
- **dis-** means not or apart. Disagree, disappear, dislike.
- **mis-** means wrongly. Misread, mistake, misspell.
- **pre-** means before. Preview, preheat, preschool.

Because nothing is dropped, you sometimes get a double letter and it is not a mistake. *Dis* plus *appear* gives *disappear*, with one *s* and two *p* letters. *Mis* plus *spell* gives *misspell*, with two *s* letters side by side. People often leave a letter out of *misspell* because they think they are making an error. They are not. The word is just two pieces, joined whole.

There is a second benefit. When you know what a prefix means, you can work out words you have never seen. If *un-* means not and *breakable* means able to be broken, then *unbreakable* means not able to be broken. You did not need anyone to tell you.

**Try it:** Add the prefix and say what the new word means. *Re* plus *build*. *Mis* plus *place*. *Dis* plus *like*.

**Check:** What does the prefix *re-* mean? (Again.)

## Chapter 5. Tool three, continued: suffixes and the adding rules

A piece added to the end of a base word is called a **suffix**. Some suffixes change the tense, like *-ing* and *-ed*. Some change the meaning: *-ful* means full of, so careful means full of care, and *-less* means without, so careless means without care. Others change the job of the word, like *-ly* in quickly and *-ness* in kindness.

Unlike prefixes, suffixes sometimes force the base word to change a little. There are three rules, and they are worth learning properly because they cover hundreds of words.

**Rule one: drop the e.** If a base word ends in a silent *e*, and the suffix starts with a vowel, drop the *e*. *Hope* plus *ing* is *hoping*. *Make* plus *ing* is *making*. The reason is simple: the silent *e* was only there to make the vowel say its name, and the suffix does the job instead. If you kept it you would get *hopeing*, which looks clumsy and is wrong.

**Rule two: double the last letter.** If a short word has one short vowel followed by one consonant, double that consonant before *-ing* or *-ed*. *Hop* plus *ing* is *hopping*. *Sit* plus *ing* is *sitting*. This rule is the reason *hoping* (from hope) and *hopping* (from hop) are different words. The doubled letter keeps the vowel short. Without it, *hoping* would be read as hope-ing.

**Rule three: change y to i.** If a word ends in a consonant followed by *y*, change the *y* to *i* before most suffixes. *Happy* plus *ness* is *happiness*. *Carry* plus *ed* is *carried*. Notice that this does not happen before *-ing*: we say *carrying*, because two *i* letters in a row would look odd.

And when the suffix starts with a consonant, such as *-ful*, *-less* or *-ly*, you usually do not need to change anything. *Hope* plus *ful* is *hopeful*. *Care* plus *less* is *careless*.

Your checklist before adding any suffix is three questions. Does the suffix start with a vowel? Does the word end in a silent *e*? Does it end in a short vowel and one consonant, or in a consonant and *y*? Answer these and the right spelling almost writes itself.

**Try it:** Add the suffix. *Make* plus *ing*. *Run* plus *ing*. *Study* plus *ed*. *Care* plus *less*. (Making, running, studied, careless.)

**Check:** What is *hop* plus *ing*? (Hopping, because the short vowel needs the consonant doubled.)

## Chapter 6. Tool four: memory, for the rule-breakers

Some words refuse to follow any of the tools. *Said*, *friend*, *because*, *people* and *beautiful* come from history, not from sound or pieces. You cannot work them out. You have to learn them. But learning does not mean staring at them and hoping. There is a method that works.

It is called **Look, Say, Cover, Write, Check**, and every step has a purpose. First **look** at the word carefully and find the tricky part. In *friend* it is the *ie*. Next **say** the word out loud, sometimes in a silly, over-the-top way. Say *Wed-nes-day* with all its letters. This makes the strange letters stand out. Then **cover** the word so you cannot see it. Now **write** it from memory, and finally **check** by uncovering it and comparing letter by letter. If you made a mistake, do not rub out the whole word. Fix only the wrong part and try the whole routine again.

You can make a word stick by adding a **memory trick**, called a mnemonic. For *because*, use: Big Elephants Can Always Understand Small Elephants. For *friend*, use: a friend is there to the end. For *necessary*, use: one collar and two sleeves, one *c* and two *s*. A silly trick is better than a sensible one, because silly is easier to remember.

One more thing: practise a word on three different days, not three times in a row. Your memory keeps what you come back to. That is why three short sessions are worth more than one long one.

**Try it:** Choose one word that you often get wrong. Find the tricky part, circle it in colour, and invent a memory trick for it.

**Check:** In the routine, what comes straight after Cover? (Write, from memory, and then check.)

## Chapter 7. Choosing the right tool

A detective does not use every tool on every case. A spelling detective has a thinking order.

First ask: is it short and regular? Use **sounds**. Second ask: is it long? Cut it into **syllables**. Third ask: can I see a prefix, a suffix or a compound? Use the **word parts** and the three adding rules. Fourth ask: does it break the rules? Use **Look, Say, Cover, Write, Check** and a memory trick.

Here it is in action with the word *unforgettable*. It is long, so split it into syllables: un-for-get-ta-ble. It has a prefix, *un-*. The base word is *forget*, and before a vowel suffix the short vowel and single consonant mean the *t* doubles, giving *forgettable*. So the word is un, forget, t, able. Strategies used: word parts first, syllables second. No memory trick was needed.

When you proofread your own writing, read slowly with a finger under each word. When a word looks wrong, do not just ask someone. Try it two ways on scrap paper, choose the one that looks right, then check it in a dictionary. Every time you do this, you are training your eyes.

And always say out loud which tool you used and why. A spelling detective who can explain their reasoning has stopped guessing.

**Try it:** Take the word *misunderstanding*. Which tools would you use, in what order? (Syllables: mis-un-der-stand-ing. Word parts: mis + under + stand + ing. No change is needed.)
