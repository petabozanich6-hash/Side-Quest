## Accept the quest

## Teaching 1: Read the setting for its effect

**Setting is more than a place name.**
Setting includes where and when events occur and the circumstances around them. A writer selects details to help us picture the situation and feel its pressure or atmosphere. Mood is the feeling a passage creates for a reader.

**Begin with what is stated.**
Read the short extract below. Identify a place, a time and one selected detail before deciding what it suggests. Do not invent background history because a place seems familiar.

**Explain a detail’s job.**
An empty foyer behind a locked door can create uncertainty when a character expects an event to begin. The detail matters because it conflicts with the character’s expectation.

**Distinguish detail from effect.**
“There is a door” identifies a thing. “The unmoving door interrupts Mara’s expectation of getting ready” explains what it does in this situation. Readers can have different reactions, but a supported effect needs a link to the words.

Try the frame “The detail ____ creates/suggests ____ because ____.” Use it to organise an explanation, not to replace thinking with a formula.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Extract: “Through the front glass she could see chairs, a folded display board and an empty foyer. The door would not move.”
Place: the front of the community hall.
Selected detail: an empty foyer visible behind an unmoving door.
Effect: uncertainty, because the expected exhibition preparations seem inaccessible.

COMPARE:
“The foyer is empty” repeats the detail. I add how it affects the situation rather than listing another object.

YOU TRY:
“Warm light fell across the concrete” appears when the side entrance is open. How might that detail change the atmosphere? Try before reading on.

CHECK:
It can suggest welcome or relief, contrasting with the blocked front entrance. Link the effect to the newly accessible entrance; do not claim warm light always has that meaning in every story.

**Notice:** An effect depends on context. Do not turn one setting detail into a universal symbol without evidence.

**Question:** Which statement explains a setting detail’s effect in this story?

1. The hall contains several nouns
2. The blocked entrance interrupts Mara’s expectation of getting ready
3. The story has a place name
4. Every locked door symbolises the same idea

**Reviewer answer:** The blocked entrance interrupts Mara’s expectation of getting ready

**Why:** The blocked entrance matters because Mara expects to prepare her display. This connects the detail to its effect in this particular situation.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a detail and its effect as different parts of an explanation.
2. Ask how the detail interacts with what Mara expects to do.
3. Use “This detail suggests ____ because ____.” Keep the link specific to this situation.

## Teaching 2: Infer character from action and dialogue

**Characterisation is how a writer presents a character.**
A writer may state a quality directly or reveal it through actions, dialogue and reactions. An inference is an interpretation supported by evidence, not a fact you add to the story.

**Look for a pattern, not only a label.**
Mara pulls the handle again and worries about the roof. These details can suggest anxiety about the exhibition. One action does not prove a person always behaves that way.

**Consider another explanation.**
Pulling again could also show determination to get inside. To choose between interpretations, combine details and explain which reading the wider passage supports.

**Avoid unsupported mind-reading.**
“Mara thinks every person dislikes her” is stronger than the text permits. “Her attention to the crooked roof suggests concern about how the model will be judged” is a more careful claim.

Use suggests, implies or may reveal where you are interpreting. Then name the action or words that support the idea. A cautious verb does not make an unsupported guess valid by itself.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Evidence: Mara notices the crooked roof in the glass and later stops adjusting it when she reaches the open entrance.
Inference: she seems self-conscious about presenting the model, and access to the event eases that pressure.
Why: her attention shifts from checking the roof to stepping forward.

COMPARE:
“Mara is bad at everything” is an unfair generalisation. The passage describes a particular moment, not her whole life.

YOU TRY:
Ari checks the message and says, “We can check before deciding it’s closed.” What response does this suggest?

CHECK:
It suggests a practical, evidence-seeking approach. Support that interpretation with his checking action rather than simply calling him clever.

**Notice:** Describe a response in this situation before making a claim about a permanent personality trait.

**Question:** Which is an evidence-based inference about Ari?

1. He secretly owns the hall
2. He has never made a mistake
3. He takes a practical approach by checking the instructions
4. He knows everyone’s private thoughts

**Reviewer answer:** He takes a practical approach by checking the instructions

**Why:** Checking the message and proposing that they investigate support a practical approach. The other claims add information the story does not supply.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at an action and a proportionate inference.
2. Consider what else the action might suggest before deciding.
3. Combine two relevant details and explain why they support your reading of this moment.

## Teaching 3: Trace plot through choices and consequences

**Plot is a connected sequence.**
A narrative’s events are more than a list of things happening. Identify a goal, obstacle, response and consequence. A consequence is the result of an action or situation.

**Order is not the same as cause.**
Then shows that an event follows another. Because or so can help explain why it happens or what follows from it. Only claim a causal link when the text supports it.

**Locate a change of direction.**
A turning point is a moment that changes how the main situation develops. Ari’s message-check and the decision to investigate the side entrance redirect the response from assuming closure to testing the instructions.

**Explain how the problem settles.**
The resolution shows what happens to the central obstacle or goal. Getting inside lets Mara place her model. Her later sign change responds to the earlier confusion and suggests learning from the experience.

Different turning-point choices can be defensible. Explain the approach before and after your chosen moment rather than relying on its position on the page.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Goal: prepare the model before visitors arrive.
Obstacle: the front door does not open.
Response: check the message and investigate the side entrance.
Consequence: Mara gets inside and sets up.
Later response: improve the sign with permission.

My connection: “Because Ari checks the instructions, they investigate a different entrance rather than accepting the first assumption.”

YOU TRY:
Explain why the second arrow at the front door connects to an earlier event.

CHECK:
It responds to Mara missing the first sign and pulling the blocked handle. It is not an unrelated ending decoration: she uses her experience to help later arrivals.

**Notice:** An exciting event is not automatically the turning point. Show what it changes in the developing problem.

**Question:** Why does checking the message matter to the plot?

1. It gives a reason to investigate another entrance
2. It removes every character from the story
3. It proves the front door is broken forever
4. It adds an unrelated setting detail

**Reviewer answer:** It gives a reason to investigate another entrance

**Why:** The instruction about a side entrance changes the next response and helps the characters reach their goal. The other interpretations are unsupported.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the character’s goal and obstacle.
2. Identify the choice before naming its result.
3. Use a supported because/so connection and compare the approach before and after the key moment.

## Teaching 4: Build a claim, evidence and explanation

**A claim answers an interpretive question.**
For example: “How does the story present Mara’s changing response?” Your claim should offer an idea about the text, not only repeat the question.

**Choose precise evidence.**
Evidence may be a short quotation or an accurately described action. Select the part that supports this claim. You do not need to copy an entire paragraph.

**Explain the link.**
After evidence, answer “How does this support my idea?” Naming a technique such as dialogue is useful only if you explain its role. A reader needs to follow your reasoning.

**Keep the claim proportionate.**
One incident can suggest a change in this situation, not prove a character has permanently changed every belief. Check your wording against the strength of your evidence.

Reread the paragraph. Does the claim answer the question? Is the evidence accurate? Does the explanation add reasoning rather than retell the same event? Improve one weak link.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Claim: The story presents Mara moving from quick frustration towards a more considered response.
Evidence: She first says, “That could mean anywhere,” but later asks permission to add another arrow.
Explanation: Her early comment dismisses the sign; the later action acknowledges the problem and tries to improve it. The ending also lets both students recognise a useful point in the other’s view.

COMMON MISTAKE:
“Mara changes. There is a sign.” The evidence is vague and the link missing. I name the contrasting responses and explain their effect.

YOU TRY:
Make one claim about Ari and choose one short supporting action. Explain the connection before comparing the model paragraph later in the lesson.

**Teaching diagram:** An analysis links a claim with precise evidence and an explanation of how the evidence supports it.

*Explanation turns evidence into reasoning.*

**Notice:** A quotation does not explain itself. Add how the selected words or action support the particular claim.

**Question:** What should follow a piece of textual evidence in an analytical paragraph?

1. An unrelated fact about your weekend
2. The same evidence copied again
3. A guarantee that no other reading is possible
4. An explanation linking the evidence to the claim

**Reviewer answer:** An explanation linking the evidence to the claim

**Why:** The explanation shows why the evidence supports the claim. Repetition or unrelated information does not supply that reasoning.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the three parts of the analytical model.
2. Check that your evidence can be located and relates to the claim.
3. After a detail, add “This suggests ____ because ____.” Explain the relationship instead of repeating the event.

## Put it all together

I DO: Read the complete original story.
THE SIDE ENTRANCE

Mara reached the community hall twenty minutes before the student exhibition opened. She had spent the week building a cardboard model and wanted it on a table before anyone arrived. Through the front glass she could see chairs, a folded display board and an empty foyer. The door would not move.

“They said we could bring our work early,” she told Ari. She pulled the handle again. In the glass, her model’s crooked roof looked more noticeable than it had at home.

Ari set down his folder and checked the message on his phone. “It says side entrance for exhibitors.”

Mara looked along the wall. A small paper arrow pointed towards the corner, but she had walked straight past it. “That could mean anywhere,” she said.

“We can check before deciding it’s closed.” Ari picked up his folder. He did not touch Mara’s model.

At the side door, a volunteer was holding the frame open while another student carried a painting through. Warm light fell across the concrete. Mara stopped adjusting the roof and stepped forward.

“There’s a table for models at the back,” the volunteer said. “Take your time.”

Inside, Mara found a space and placed the model beside a bridge made from paddle-pop sticks. Nobody commented on her roof. Ari put his folder on the neighbouring table.

Mara took the paper arrow from the outside wall, showed it to the volunteer and asked whether they could add a second arrow near the front handle. With permission, she placed the new sign where the next student would see it before pulling.

When she came back, Ari was straightening his display label. “You were right about checking,” she said. He looked up. “And you were right that the sign could be clearer.”

MODEL ANALYTICAL PARAGRAPH
The story presents Ari as practical without making him dismissive of Mara’s concern. He checks the message and proposes that they investigate before deciding the hall is closed. This response uses available evidence to change their next action rather than arguing about who is right. However, his final comment that the sign “could be clearer” also recognises Mara’s experience. Together, the actions and dialogue suggest that a useful response combines checking an assumption with listening to someone affected by it.

WHY THE PARAGRAPH WORKS
The claim is specific, the evidence includes action and dialogue, and the explanation identifies their relationship. It does not claim Ari is perfect or knows facts outside the text.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“Ari has a phone, so he is practical” names an object rather than explaining its use. Fix: specify that he checks the instructions and uses what he finds to guide the next action.

**Teaching diagram:** A causal chain moves from an initial assumption through checking instructions to investigating, setting up and improving the sign.

*Use the chain to explain change, not merely list events.*

## Quest check

10 multiple-choice questions. The unchanged 90% rule requires 9/10. Written reasoning is assessed in the main task and reflection.

**Question:** What does setting include?

1. Only a place name
2. Only the title
3. Where, when and the surrounding circumstances
4. Every thought a reader invents

**Reviewer answer:** Where, when and the surrounding circumstances

**Why:** Setting includes place, time and relevant circumstances. A place name alone does not capture all its possible roles.

**Question:** Which detail supports uncertainty at the front entrance?

1. An empty foyer behind an unmoving door
2. A narrator’s invented school history
3. A fact about an unrelated city
4. A guarantee about all locked doors

**Reviewer answer:** An empty foyer behind an unmoving door

**Why:** The visible emptiness and blocked access conflict with Mara’s expectation of setting up early.

**Question:** What is an inference?

1. A fact you add without evidence
2. A copied title
3. A claim that must be certain
4. An interpretation supported by textual evidence

**Reviewer answer:** An interpretation supported by textual evidence

**Why:** An inference reasons from evidence. It is not an invented fact or an automatic certainty.

**Question:** Which action supports Ari’s practical approach?

1. He changes the exhibition date
2. He checks the instructions before proposing a next step
3. He comments on every model
4. He knows all visitors’ thoughts

**Reviewer answer:** He checks the instructions before proposing a next step

**Why:** Checking instructions supplies evidence for his proposed response. The other actions are not stated.

**Question:** Why can pulling the handle again have more than one interpretation?

1. Actions have no context
2. Every interpretation is equally supported
3. A reader must consider the action alongside other details
4. The event never happened

**Reviewer answer:** A reader must consider the action alongside other details

**Why:** An action can suggest different responses; context and other evidence help assess which reading is supported.

**Question:** What is a consequence in plot analysis?

1. The result of an action or situation
2. The number of letters in a name
3. An unrelated object
4. A word that always means a happy ending

**Reviewer answer:** The result of an action or situation

**Why:** A consequence is a result that may shape what follows. It need not be happy.

**Question:** Which moment changes the approach to the blocked entrance?

1. Ari has a folder
2. The story mentions a roof
3. A model has cardboard
4. They check the message and investigate the side entrance

**Reviewer answer:** They check the message and investigate the side entrance

**Why:** The checked instruction redirects them from an assumption of closure to investigating another entrance.

**Question:** How does the new arrow connect to an earlier event?

1. It proves every visitor was lost
2. It responds to Mara missing the first sign
3. It creates a different exhibition goal
4. It removes the need for permission

**Reviewer answer:** It responds to Mara missing the first sign

**Why:** The extra arrow responds to the earlier confusion. Permission remains part of the stated action.

**Question:** What makes evidence precise?

1. It is long enough to fill a page
2. It sounds impressive but cannot be located
3. It identifies relevant words or an accurately described action
4. It replaces all explanation

**Reviewer answer:** It identifies relevant words or an accurately described action

**Why:** Precise evidence can be located and connected to the claim. Length or impressiveness alone does not make it useful.

**Question:** Which claim is proportionate to the evidence?

1. Mara’s actions suggest a changed response in this situation
2. Mara never makes another mistake in her life
3. Everyone secretly dislikes the model
4. The hall will always be closed

**Reviewer answer:** Mara’s actions suggest a changed response in this situation

**Why:** The passage supports a situational change. The other claims go beyond what its events establish.

### Help with this step

1. Return to setting, inference, plot or evidence reasoning as the question requires.
2. Distinguish a stated event from a claim about what it suggests.
3. Compare each option with the passage and avoid claims that require invented background information.
