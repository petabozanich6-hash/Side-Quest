# Year 3: Tell a story and prepare readable notes

Stage 2 · Year 3 · English · Week 1 · Lesson 4 · 60 minutes

Seed key: `s2-eng-y3-w01-l4`. Status: unreviewed. Original content. Outcomes: EN2-OLC-01, EN2-HANDW-01. These are contributions to the outcomes, not full coverage.

## Accept the quest

Become a Story Teller! Practise a short story so a listener can follow it, then make a clear note card to help you remember.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to retell a short story in order and prepare readable handwriting cues.

- I can include a beginning, important middle events and an ending.
- I can use a few notes to retell rather than copy every word.
- I can improve a pause or pace in my second telling.
- I can practise a simple letter join and check my note card is readable.

**Materials:** This lesson contains the story and models; Paper and a pencil for handwriting practice; A text box is available if handwriting is not accessible today

### Help with this step

1. Read the mission and notice the telling and note-card parts.
2. Begin with the first story; your notes can grow after the examples.
3. Choose paper for handwriting practice if accessible. You can still use typed notes to plan your telling.

## Teaching 1: Find the beginning, middle and ending

**A listener needs the important events.**
A retelling tells a story again in your own words. Keep what happens accurate. You can shorten the wording without changing the events.

**Begin with someone and a situation.**
Who is the story about? Where are they and what are they doing? Choose details that help the listener follow what happens next.

**Keep the middle connected.**
Tell the important event or problem and what someone does about it. Do not list every object while leaving out the action.

**Tell how the event ends.**
The ending shows what happens after the important action. A listener should not be left asking whether the main event was finished.

Try three short notes: beginning, middle, ending. These help with our short practice story. They are not a rule that every story must have exactly three sentences.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Short story: “Ava made a kite at the table. Its string came loose. She tied it again and put the kite beside her bag.”
Beginning: Ava makes a kite.
Middle: the string comes loose, so she repairs it.
Ending: she puts the repaired kite away.

COMPARE:
“There is a table and a bag” names things but leaves out what happens.

YOU TRY:
“Ben made a paper boat. A fold opened. He pressed it back and placed the boat on a tray.”
Say three event notes before checking.

CHECK:
Keep making the boat, fixing the opened fold and placing it on the tray. Your words can differ if the events stay accurate.

**Notice:** Select events that help a listener follow the action. Do not invent a new ending to fill a gap.

**Question:** What belongs in a useful retelling of Ben’s boat story?

1. Only the colour of every object
2. An invented journey to another country
3. A list of all letters in boat
4. The opened fold and what Ben does to fix it

**Reviewer answer:** The opened fold and what Ben does to fix it

**Why:** The opened fold and its repair explain the important middle event. Unrelated lists or invented events do not preserve the story.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at important events rather than lists of things.
2. Find what happens to the object and what the person does next.
3. Use a beginning, middle and ending note to check that the main action stays understandable.

## Teaching 2: Use notes to speak in your own words

**A note is a reminder.**
A short note helps you remember an event. It does not need to contain every word you will say. You turn the note into a complete spoken idea.

**Introduce the person first.**
Say who you mean before using he, she or they. A listener who has not read the story needs that information.

**Link the order.**
First, next, then and finally can help a listener follow the sequence. Choose the important actions rather than putting a link before every word.

**Check against the story.**
Read a section, look away and explain it. Look back to check the event. It is fine to keep names or an important word, but you do not have to memorise the whole text.

Your notes should help you explain; they should not replace the explanation with a list of vague words.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Note: loose string.
Spoken idea: “Next, Ava noticed that the kite’s string had come loose.”
The note reminds me; my sentence tells the listener what happened.

COMMON MISTAKE:
“She did it, then that happened” does not name the person or event. I fix it by saying Ava and the loose string.

YOU TRY:
Use the note pressed the fold to tell Ben’s action in a complete spoken sentence.

CHECK:
“Ben pressed the opened fold back into place” explains the note. Another accurate sentence is fine. Do not add a different repair that the story never mentions.

**Notice:** Notes can be short, but your spoken ideas still need enough information for a listener.

**Question:** What is the job of a short note?

1. To replace all speaking with one unclear word
2. To remind you of an event you will explain
3. To change the original story
4. To list every letter on the page

**Reviewer answer:** To remind you of an event you will explain

**Why:** A note prompts an event; you explain that event in a spoken sentence. It should not change the story or remove needed information.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a note becoming a full spoken idea.
2. Name the person before using a pronoun the listener cannot identify.
3. Use your note to explain what happened, then check the original story for accuracy.

## Teaching 3: Practise a clear telling twice

**Help the listener hear the ideas.**
Pace is how fast you speak. Volume is how loud or quiet your voice is. Speak comfortably and keep the words clear. You do not need a performance voice.

**Pause between thoughts.**
A brief pause lets a listener follow one event before the next. Do not stop between every word or rush all the events together.

**Practise one part first.**
Say the beginning and one middle event. Notice whether you lose a word, speak too quickly or miss the important action.

**Make one improvement.**
Tell the story once. Choose one change, such as a slower middle event or a clearer ending. Tell it again using that change.

You can practise alone. A listener or recording can help if available, but is not required. Explain what your change helps a listener understand.

**Original episode:** A second telling has a purpose

**Pip:** I remembered the story, but I rushed the part where the character fixed the problem.

**Juno:** Try that event again at a steadier pace. Keep the action clear for your listener.

**Pip:** I can use a short note and a brief pause before the next event.

**Juno:** Pause and try. Tell one event twice and explain what you improved. **[Pause to try; press Play to continue.]**

### Show me an example

I DO:
First try: I rush the part about the string, and the repair is unclear.
My change: I give that event more time and pause before the ending.
Second try: “The string came loose. [brief pause] Ava tied it again. [brief pause] She put the kite away.” I do not say the bracketed notes aloud.

YOU TRY:
Tell Ben’s opened-fold event twice. In the second version, keep each word clear and choose one helpful pause.

CHECK:
The pause should separate understandable thoughts, not turn each word into a separate event. You can explain your improvement without sounding exactly like someone else.

**Notice:** Making the voice louder does not automatically fix rushed words. Start with clarity and a pace you can manage.

**Question:** Your middle event was rushed. Which change is useful?

1. Delete the event entirely
2. Shout every word
3. Give the event more time and keep the words clear
4. Invent a different problem

**Reviewer answer:** Give the event more time and keep the words clear

**Why:** A steadier pace helps the listener follow the same important event. Deleting, shouting or inventing does not repair the rushed explanation.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a pace or pause improvement.
2. Choose one rushed event and say it carefully by itself.
3. Tell that short event twice and compare whether the action is easier to follow.

## Teaching 4: Write and test a readable note card

**Write notes you can use.**
Give each event its own line. Leave enough space between words to read them at a glance. Use letter shapes and sizes you can recognise consistently.

**Try one simple join.**
Our drawing models a practice join in the lower-case word in. Start at the entry point, move into i, bring its downstroke to the writing line, then continue into the entry and arch of n. Add the i dot after the connected strokes. Follow the drawing slowly on paper.

You lift the pencil to add the dot; joining letters does not mean keeping the pencil down for the whole word. Leave a space before the next word. Do not force letters together if the join makes them unreadable.

**Practise, then apply.**
Try in three times, checking the shapes and connection. Use it in a short note such as in the garden. If you already use another taught joining style, keep that style and focus on clear letters.

Test the card by looking up, glancing back and explaining one event. If a note is too cramped or vague, revise it. A typed alternative can support your telling, but shows different evidence from handwriting.

### Show me an example

I DO:
My three event notes:
Garden: paper boat
Opened fold: pressed back
Ending: boat on tray

I keep each event on its own line. I practise the small in join separately, then write in the garden clearly below the notes.

COMMON MISTAKE:
One crowded line of tiny letters is hard to use. Fix: separate the notes and leave clear word spaces.

YOU TRY:
Write in slowly using the model. Then write one short event note and read it back after looking away.

CHECK:
Can you identify both letters and the space between words? If the join hides a letter, slow down or use your familiar taught form. Your readable note matters more than extra decoration.

**Teaching diagram:** A simple continuous practice stroke connects lower-case i and n between a midline and baseline. The i dot is added separately. Start and line guides are labelled.

*Follow slowly; use your familiar taught form if it differs.*

**Notice:** This drawing is a simple practice model, not a complete handwriting alphabet. Keep a familiar taught style when you have one and check the result for readability.

**Question:** What is a useful handwriting check for a note card?

1. Clear letter shapes, word spaces and readable size
2. Only the amount of decoration
3. Whether every word is crowded together
4. Whether the page has no blank space

**Reviewer answer:** Clear letter shapes, word spaces and readable size

**Why:** Letter clarity, spacing and size help you read a note at a glance. Decoration or crowding does not establish readability.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the line guides and start point in the drawing.
2. Follow the main strokes slowly and check that both letters remain recognisable.
3. Practise one join, then test a short note for clear letters and word spaces.

## Put it all together

I DO: Read the original short story.
THE PAPER BOAT
Mila sat in the garden with a paper boat. She wanted to keep it ready for an afternoon game.
When she lifted it, one fold opened. Mila pressed the fold back carefully and checked the shape.
She put the boat on a tray beside the door. Now she could find it when the game began.

MY EVENT NOTES
Beginning: Mila in garden, boat ready
Middle: fold opens, Mila presses it back
Ending: boat on tray for game

MY RETELLING
“Mila had a paper boat in the garden. A fold opened when she lifted it, so she pressed it back carefully. Finally, she put it on a tray where she could find it for her game.”

WHY IT WORKS
I name Mila and the situation, explain the problem and repair, then tell the outcome. I keep the events but choose my own wording. My short notes prompt full spoken ideas.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“A boat was there, then it was there again” loses the middle event. I add the opened fold and Mila’s action. In rehearsal I give that repair enough time to be clear.

**Teaching diagram:** A short retelling includes the person and situation, an important event and response, and the outcome.

*Use notes to remember, then speak complete ideas.*

### Help with this step

1. Read the original story before the model telling.
2. Match each short note to an important event in the original.
3. Use one note to speak a complete idea, keeping the event the same.

## Try it with help

WE DO: Build a telling from the paper-boat story.
1. Say who is in the story and what she has. Compare after your try: Mila has a paper boat in the garden.
2. Name the important middle event and response. Compare: a fold opens and Mila presses it back.
3. Say where the boat ends up and why that matters. Compare: it is on a tray so she can find it for the game.
4. Write three short notes and speak the connected telling from them. Look back at the original to check accuracy.
5. Practise your in join slowly three times, then write in the garden with clear spaces. Use your familiar taught join if different.
6. Tell the story again and choose one useful pace or pause improvement. Explain the change rather than just saying it was better.

### Help with this step

1. Start with Mila’s situation before the middle event.
2. Make three short notes before trying the connected telling.
3. Use “At the beginning, ____.” Fill that gap, then move to what changes in the middle.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Story telling and note-card task (about 20 minutes).
Use the original paper-boat story included above.
1. Prepare three short event notes: beginning, middle and ending. Keep one note per line.
2. On paper, practise the in join three times and write in the garden. Check clear shapes and spaces. If handwriting is not accessible, type the notes and tell a grown-up that no handwriting sample was produced.
3. Retell the story in your own words using the notes. You can practise alone; a listener or recording is optional.
4. Choose a clearer word, steadier pace or helpful thought pause. Retell it again with that improvement.
5. In the big box, submit your three notes, retelling wording or summary, and improvement with its reason. Keep any handwritten page for review; an upload is not required.
Check: important events accurate; person introduced; ending included; notes readable; one improvement explained.

**Reflect:** Which note or delivery change helped your second telling, and how?

### Help with this step

1. Prepare the three event notes before your full rehearsal.
2. Begin “This story is about ____.” Introduce the person before the event.
3. A partial opening shape is “In the garden, ____ had ____.” Complete it from the story and continue in your own words.

**Question:** What does a retelling do?

1. Changes every event into a different story
2. Tells important events again in your own words
3. Lists every object but no actions
4. Copies only the title

**Reviewer answer:** Tells important events again in your own words

**Why:** A retelling keeps important events and meaning while using your own wording.

**Question:** What helps an unfamiliar listener at the beginning?

1. An unexplained she
2. Only the last event
3. Who the story is about and the situation
4. A list of all punctuation

**Reviewer answer:** Who the story is about and the situation

**Why:** Introducing the person and situation gives the listener a starting point for later events.

**Question:** Which middle event belongs in the paper-boat retelling?

1. The fold opens and Mila presses it back
2. Mila travels overseas in the boat
3. The tray turns into a kite
4. A new person wins an invented race

**Reviewer answer:** The fold opens and Mila presses it back

**Why:** The opened fold and repair are stated in the original. The other choices invent events.

**Question:** Why include the boat being put on a tray?

1. It means the middle can disappear
2. Every story must include a tray
3. It lists a colour
4. It explains where the boat ends up ready for the game

**Reviewer answer:** It explains where the boat ends up ready for the game

**Why:** This detail gives the event an outcome and shows why the placement matters.

**Question:** What is a note card for?

1. Replacing all explanation with vague words
2. Prompting the events you will explain
3. Changing the names randomly
4. Counting every letter

**Reviewer answer:** Prompting the events you will explain

**Why:** Short meaningful notes prompt full spoken ideas; they do not replace the explanation.

**Question:** Which change helps a rushed repair event?

1. Make every word louder
2. Remove the repair
3. Give it more time and keep the words clear
4. Add a new problem

**Reviewer answer:** Give it more time and keep the words clear

**Why:** A steadier pace improves clarity without changing the original event.

**Question:** Why leave spaces between written words?

1. So the separate words can be recognised easily
2. So all letters become one shape
3. So the story needs no ending
4. So decoration replaces meaning

**Reviewer answer:** So the separate words can be recognised easily

**Why:** Word spaces help the reader identify separate words and use the note at a glance.

**Question:** When practising the modelled in join, which step is appropriate?

1. Never lift to add any dot
2. Join the next word without a space
3. Hide the i letter completely
4. Add the i dot after the connected strokes

**Reviewer answer:** Add the i dot after the connected strokes

**Why:** The connected main strokes can be followed before lifting to add the i dot. Word spaces and clear letters still matter.
