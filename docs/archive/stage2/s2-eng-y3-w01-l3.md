# Year 3: Spell with sounds and syllables

Stage 2 · Year 3 · English · Week 1 · Lesson 3 · 60 minutes

Seed key: `s2-eng-y3-w01-l3`. Status: unreviewed. Original content. Outcomes: EN2-SPELL-01. These are contributions to the outcomes, not full coverage.

## Accept the quest

Become a Word Investigator! Listen to words, notice their letter patterns and use a checking routine to improve your spelling.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to combine sounds, syllables and remembered letter patterns when spelling taught words.

- I can listen for sounds without assuming one letter per sound.
- I can split a spoken word into syllable beats.
- I can use the model to check a tricky letter pattern.
- I can find a difference and practise the corrected word.

**Materials:** Everything you need to read is included in this lesson; Optional: paper and a pencil

### Help with this step

1. Read the mission and notice that listening and written checking have different jobs.
2. Begin with the first taught word; you do not need to guess all five spellings yet.
3. Keep a small space for a first attempt and a checked version. The models are included.

## Teaching 1: Listen for sound parts

**Sounds and letters are different.**
When you say a word, you hear sounds. When you write it, you choose letters to represent them. A word does not always have one letter for each sound.

**Say, stretch and listen.**
Say ship normally. Now say its parts slowly: sh, i, p. Sh is one sound written with two letters. The word has four letters, but these three sound parts.

A digraph is two letters representing one sound. In this example, sh is the digraph. Keep it together when matching the heard sound to letters.

**Do not add sounds accidentally.**
Some sounds can stretch; others are brief. Keep the final p short rather than adding an extra uh. Then join the parts and say ship normally again.

Listening helps you start a spelling. You also need letter-pattern knowledge, because a sound can sometimes be written in different ways. Use the model when you are unsure.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Word: ship.
I hear sh / i / p. I choose sh, i and p, then join them: ship.

COMPARE:
Saying s and h separately does not match the starting sound in ship. The letters work as one group here.

YOU TRY:
Say shop slowly. What letter group represents its first sound? Try before reading on.

CHECK:
Shop begins with sh too. Its middle sound differs from ship, so check the whole word rather than copying the last example automatically.

**Notice:** Listening does not reveal every spelling by itself. Use sounds as clues and check the written pattern.

**Question:** In ship, which letters work together for one starting sound?

1. s and p
2. h and i
3. s and h
4. i and p

**Reviewer answer:** s and h

**Why:** S and h together represent the starting sh sound in ship. The other groups do not represent that first sound.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at how a sound can use a letter group.
2. Say the whole word, then listen to its beginning without separating every letter.
3. Compare the heard start with the written model. Keep a two-letter sound group together where the example shows one.

## Teaching 2: Listen for syllable beats

**A syllable is a spoken beat.**
Syllables help you handle a longer word in smaller parts. Say sunset naturally, then clap its two beats: sun-set. You are counting spoken beats, not letters.

**Hear before you split.**
Say the whole word first. Tap once for each beat as you repeat it. Keep the sounds inside each beat together. Fantastic has three beats: fan-tas-tic.

**Use beats for a spelling plan.**
Write one part, then the next, then read the joined word. The split is a practice aid; do not leave hyphens in an ordinary spelling answer unless the actual word requires them.

**Beats do not settle every letter.**
Rabbit has two beats. The spoken beats help you organise the word, but the model also tells you that its written spelling has two b letters. Listening and checking work together.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Sunset: I say sun-set, tap twice and write sun + set. I join the parts as sunset.
Fantastic: I say fan-tas-tic and tap three times. I keep each part in order.

COMMON MISTAKE:
Counting every letter gives a different number from counting beats. Sunset has six letters and two syllables.

YOU TRY:
Say window naturally, then tap its beats. Check before reading on.

CHECK:
Win-dow has two beats. The split helps you remember the order of the written parts; it is not six separate spoken beats.

**Teaching diagram:** Sunset has two spoken syllable beats; fantastic has three. Syllables are not counted by letters.

*Speak the whole word before tapping its beats.*

**Notice:** Count what you hear when speaking naturally. A row of letters is not a row of syllable beats.

**Question:** How many spoken syllable beats are in fan-tas-tic?

1. One
2. Three
3. Six
4. Nine

**Reviewer answer:** Three

**Why:** Fan, tas and tic are three spoken beats. Counting letters would answer a different question.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at speaking naturally before counting beats.
2. Tap once per spoken chunk rather than once per letter.
3. Repeat the whole word and then its chunks. Use the chunking to plan the order.

## Teaching 3: Notice the written pattern

**Some spellings need a closer look.**
After listening and splitting, compare your idea with the correct model. Find the part you might miss, rather than staring at the whole word without a plan.

**Use this word's evidence.**
Rabbit is written rab-bit: two b letters meet in the middle. Write both. Do not assume that every two-syllable word needs a double letter. Window has two syllables without that doubled b pattern.

Ship begins with sh, a two-letter group for one sound. Sunset joins the written parts sun and set. Fantastic keeps fan, tas and tic in order.

**Say a useful reminder.**
“Rabbit needs two b letters.” “Ship starts with sh.” A reminder should name the particular tricky part.

**Check rather than guess.**
If you are uncertain, look at the model again. Learning a spelling includes noticing and remembering its written pattern, not only sounding it out.

### Show me an example

I DO:
My attempt: rabit.
Model: rabbit.
I compare from the beginning. R and a match. At the middle I notice the model has two b letters. I say, “Rabbit needs two b letters,” and rewrite it.

YOU TRY:
Compare sip with the model ship. Which written part changes? Think first.

CHECK:
Ship includes h after s to make sh. Sip is another real word, but it does not spell the intended word ship. A spelling check needs to keep the intended meaning too.

**Notice:** A word can look familiar and still be the wrong word. Compare your spelling with the intended word, including its tricky part.

**Question:** What needs fixing when rabit is compared with rabbit?

1. The model needs three r letters
2. The final t should disappear
3. The a should become a whole sentence
4. The attempt needs another b in the middle

**Reviewer answer:** The attempt needs another b in the middle

**Why:** Rabbit is written with two b letters. Rabit has only one, so adding the missing b repairs the taught spelling.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at comparing an attempt from its beginning.
2. Find the first place where your letters differ from the model.
3. Name that particular difference, then practise the correct whole word. Do not invent a rule for every word.

## Teaching 4: Use look, cover, write and check

**Practise with a method.**
Look closely at a model and say the word. Identify its sounds, syllable parts and one written detail you want to remember.

Cover the model with your hand or look away. Write the word from memory. Then uncover and compare from the beginning. Check letter order as well as whether all the letters are present.

**Learn from a difference.**
If your attempt differs, mark the first place that needs attention. Look at the correct part, say a reminder and rewrite the whole word correctly. Cover and try again.

**Use the word in meaning.**
Once you have checked the spelling, use it in a short sentence. Reread to make sure it is the word you meant.

**Practice is not a race.**
Copying the same mistake many times will not fix it. A careful comparison and corrected attempt are more useful than rushing a long list.

**Original episode:** A spelling mistake can teach you

**Pip:** I wrote a word from memory, but it does not match the model. Should I keep copying my attempt?

**Juno:** Find the first difference. Look at that part and practise the corrected whole word.

**Pip:** I can use a sound clue, a syllable part and a reminder about the letters.

**Juno:** Pause and try. Check one taught word carefully before your next memory attempt. **[Pause to try; press Play to continue.]**

### Show me an example

I DO:
Model: fantastic.
I say fan-tas-tic and notice the order of the three parts. I cover it and write fantasic.
Checking shows I missed the t in tas. I look at that part, say the reminder and rewrite fantastic. Then I cover and try again.

YOU TRY:
Use the routine with sunset. Check the letters before writing a sentence with it.

CHECK:
You need the joined spelling sunset, with sun before set. A possible sentence is “We watched the sunset.” Your original sentence can be different if sunset fits its meaning.

**Notice:** Keep the corrected version visible for your comparison, then cover it for the next memory try. Do not practise an unchecked mistake.

**Question:** After a spelling attempt differs from the model, what should you do?

1. Notice the difference, study it and try the corrected word
2. Repeat the unchecked attempt ten times
3. Remove random letters
4. Ignore the intended word

**Reviewer answer:** Notice the difference, study it and try the corrected word

**Why:** Finding and studying the difference lets you practise the correct pattern. Repeating an unchecked mistake does not repair it.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at what happens after uncovering the model.
2. Compare the order and presence of letters before your next attempt.
3. Say a reminder about the part you noticed, look away and try the correct whole word again.

## Put it all together

I DO: Spell rabbit with a complete routine.
Listen: I say rabbit normally and hear its parts.
Beats: I tap rab-bit, two spoken beats.
Pattern: I look at the model rabbit and notice two b letters.
Cover: I hide the model and write rabit.
Check: Comparing from the beginning shows one b is missing.
Repair: I say “two b letters in rabbit”, write rabbit and try from memory again.
Use: “The rabbit rested beside the box.” I check the spelling in the sentence too.

WHY THE METHOD WORKS
Sounds and beats give me a starting plan. The written model supplies the exact letter pattern. My correction focuses on a particular difference.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Counting the letters in rabbit does not count its syllables. I speak the word to count beats and use the model to check letters. These are different jobs.

**Teaching diagram:** A spelling routine listens, splits and notices, then looks, covers, writes and checks before repairing a difference.

*Check a taught model rather than guessing the missing part.*

### Help with this step

1. Read each job in the complete routine before copying the final word.
2. Notice which part is heard and which part must be checked in writing.
3. Explain one comparison result in your own words before moving to another word.

## Spelling: Sounds, syllables and patterns

Use the models below for your practice. Say each word normally, listen to its parts, then tap its syllables. Study the listed written detail before covering and writing. Compare each attempt with the model. The hyphens in parts show practice chunks; write the actual word without them.

| Word | Parts | Reminder |
|---|---|---|
| ship | sh / i / p; one syllable | Sh is a two-letter group for one starting sound. |
| sunset | sun-set; two syllables | Join sun and set in this order. |
| rabbit | rab-bit; two syllables | Remember two b letters in the middle. |
| fantastic | fan-tas-tic; three syllables | Keep the t in the middle part tas. |
| window | win-dow; two syllables | Check the written ow ending against the model. |

**Question:** Which reminder matches the taught word ship?

1. It starts with two separate words
2. It needs three b letters
3. Its first sound is written sh
4. Every letter is a syllable

**Reviewer answer:** Its first sound is written sh

**Why:** The starting sh sound is written with the two-letter group sh in ship. The other reminders do not fit the taught word.

### Help with this step

1. Look at a base model and its listed written detail.
2. Speak the word, tap its chunks and notice the part you want to remember.
3. Cover only after studying the model, then uncover for a letter-by-letter comparison.

## Try it with help

WE DO: Compare two attempts.
1. Intended word: ship. Attempt: sip. Say both if you can. Find the difference before reading the suggestion: ship needs h after s for its sh group.
2. Intended word: fantastic. Attempt: fantasic. Say fan-tas-tic and compare the parts. Suggestion: the middle tas needs t.
3. Try rabbit with look, cover, write and check. Give yourself the reminder about its middle letters before covering.
4. Write one original sentence with a corrected word. Reread so both the spelling and meaning fit.
If you make a different mistake, compare your own attempt rather than copying these errors. The goal is to identify and repair the actual difference.

### Help with this step

1. Compare the first attempt with its intended word before reading the suggestion.
2. Check from the beginning and locate a missing or changed group.
3. Use “My attempt ____ differs at ____.” Then write the correct whole word.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Word Investigator record (about 20 minutes).
Use the five taught words: ship, sunset, rabbit, fantastic and window.
1. Say each word naturally. Tap its syllables and write the count beside the word.
2. Choose three words and write a reminder for each: a sound group, chunk order or tricky written part.
3. Use look, cover, write and check with all five words. Keep your first attempt and corrected version if they differ. If an attempt matches, record that it matched.
4. Write two original sentences using two of the checked words.
5. In the big box, record your syllable counts, three reminders, one check result and your sentences. You can work on paper first and type a summary. A photo is optional.
Do not invent a mistake if you spelled every word correctly. Explain which reminder helped instead.

**Reflect:** Which spelling clue or check helped you most, and why?

### Help with this step

1. Start with the five models and one spoken beat count.
2. Use “For this word, I notice ____.” Add a reminder you can actually use.
3. A record can begin “I said ____ in parts. My first attempt ____.” Finish with your own check result.

**Question:** Why can letters and sound parts have different counts?

1. A letter always makes two sounds
2. Two letters can represent one sound
3. Sounds cannot be heard
4. Words have no written forms

**Reviewer answer:** Two letters can represent one sound

**Why:** A group such as sh can represent one sound, so a sound count need not match the letter count.

**Question:** Which starting group is in ship?

1. ow
2. bb
3. sh
4. tas

**Reviewer answer:** sh

**Why:** Sh is the taught two-letter group representing the starting sound in ship.

**Question:** How many spoken syllable beats are in sun-set?

1. Two
2. Six
3. One for each letter
4. Seven

**Reviewer answer:** Two

**Why:** Sun and set form two spoken beats, regardless of the number of written letters.

**Question:** How many spoken beats are in fan-tas-tic?

1. One
2. Four
3. Nine
4. Three

**Reviewer answer:** Three

**Why:** Fan, tas and tic make three spoken syllable beats.

**Question:** Which spelling matches the taught model?

1. rabit
2. rabbit
3. rabbbit
4. rabitt

**Reviewer answer:** rabbit

**Why:** The model rabbit has two b letters and one final t. The alternatives change that taught pattern.

**Question:** What do the hyphens in fan-tas-tic show here?

1. Punctuation to copy into every word
2. Separate sentences
3. Practice syllable parts
4. Three unrelated words

**Reviewer answer:** Practice syllable parts

**Why:** The hyphens mark practice chunks. The actual taught spelling is fantastic without those practice hyphens.

**Question:** What should a check compare?

1. Your attempt with the correct intended word
2. Only whether the page has colours
3. Only how fast you wrote
4. Two unchecked guesses

**Reviewer answer:** Your attempt with the correct intended word

**Why:** Comparing with the correct intended word reveals differences in letters and order.

**Question:** What is useful after finding a missing letter?

1. Ignore the difference
2. Copy the mistake repeatedly
3. Change the meaning at random
4. Study the correct part and practise the whole corrected word

**Reviewer answer:** Study the correct part and practise the whole corrected word

**Why:** Focused study followed by a corrected attempt practises the pattern that was missing.

**Question:** Explain one useful reminder for rabbit.

Written response, manually reviewed.

**Reviewer model:** Rabbit has two spoken beats, rab-bit, and I must remember two b letters in its written middle.

**Marking guide:** Accept a correct taught beat or letter-pattern reminder. Look for a specific clue rather than only “remember it”.

**Why:** A useful reminder names a taught part of the word.

**Question:** Explain how you used a model to check one spelling attempt.

Written response, manually reviewed.

**Reviewer model:** I compared my attempt rabit with rabbit, noticed the missing b and practised the corrected word.

**Marking guide:** Accept an actual comparison or a matching result with a useful explanation. A mistake is not required.

**Why:** A clear explanation shows what was compared and what the learner noticed.
