# Year 4: Add a precise detail and check the ending

Become a Sentence Editor! Help a reader identify a person or object, then check what happens when a word gains a suffix.

Learning intention: We are learning to use identifying relative clauses and apply taught y-to-i and consonant-doubling spelling patterns.

Duration: 60 minutes.

Success criteria:
- I can identify the noun a detail describes.
- I can use who, which or that in a taught relative-clause pattern.
- I can apply the taught y-to-i pattern and its -ing exception.
- I can check the taught one-syllable doubling pattern.
- I can explain an editing decision.

## Accept the quest

### Help with this step

1. Look at the two jobs: identify a noun and check a suffix.
2. You will learn the clause pattern before practising the word endings separately.
3. Begin with the noun a detail describes; keep the spelling models for the later steps.

## Teaching 1: Identify the noun and the detail

**A noun names a person, place, thing or idea.**
In “The child held a label”, child and label are nouns. A detail can help the reader know which child or which label you mean. First locate the noun, then ask what information belongs to it.

**A clause has a verb and a subject.**
The subject tells who or what the clause is about; the verb tells an action or state. In “the child carried the board”, the child is the subject and carried is the verb. A clause can be part of a longer sentence.

**A relative clause describes a noun.**
In “the child who carried the board”, who carried the board is a relative clause. It identifies the child through an action. The word who stands for the child, and carried is the verb inside that clause.

Read the whole sentence: “The child who carried the board found a space.” Found is the main action; carried belongs inside the describing clause. Our examples identify which person or object we mean, so we do not put commas around those identifying clauses. Other relative-clause patterns can use commas; today keep to these models.

I DO:
“The child who carried the board found a space.”
Noun being described: child.
Relative clause: who carried the board.
Main action: found a space.
I remove the describing part briefly to check the main sentence: “The child found a space.” The detail identifies which child.

YOU TRY:
In “The label that lay under the chair was blue”, what noun does that lay under the chair describe?

CHECK:
It describes label. Lay tells the position in that clause; was blue is the main description of the label.

Notice: Find the noun being described. Do not mistake every word after it for one long relative clause.

**Question:** In “The child who carried the board found a space”, what noun does who carried the board describe?

1. Space
2. Found
3. Board only
4. Child

Reviewer answer: Child

Why: The relative clause identifies the child. The main action found a space follows that clause.

### Help with this step

1. Look for the noun beside the describing words.
2. Find the action inside the detail and the main action separately.
3. Temporarily remove the describing clause to locate the main statement, then put it back beside its noun.

## Teaching 2: Join two ideas without repeating the noun

**Use a relative word to connect.**
Who refers to a person in these models. Which or that can refer to a thing. That can also refer to a person, but we will practise who for people and which or that for objects. These are useful patterns, not a claim that each word has only one use.

**Keep the detail beside its noun.**
Start with “The child found a space” and “The child carried the board”. Combine them as “The child who carried the board found a space”. The detail immediately follows child, so its target is clear.

**Do not repeat the subject inside.**
“The child who she carried the board” repeats the person through who and she. In our subject-relative pattern, who already stands for the child. Keep “who carried the board”. Other clause patterns can work differently.

**Reread the full sentence.**
Check the identifying detail and the main idea. It should tell which person or object, then still finish the main sentence. A phrase such as “the child who carried the board” needs a main statement for this task.

I DO:
Main idea: The box sat on the table.
Detail: The box held the cards.
Joined: “The box that held the cards sat on the table.”
That held the cards identifies the box. Sat on the table finishes the main statement.

YOU TRY:
Join “The girl found the ribbon” and “The girl made the map” using who.

CHECK:
“The girl who made the map found the ribbon.” Who replaces the repeated girl in the detail. Do not add she after who in this pattern.

Diagram: The main statement is the child found a space. The relative clause who carried the board sits after child and before the main action.

Identify the noun, add its detail, finish the main idea.

Notice: Put the describing clause next to the intended noun, and preserve the main statement.

**Question:** Which joined sentence follows the taught pattern?

1. The box that held the cards sat on the table.
2. The box that it held the cards sat on the table.
3. The box that held the cards
4. The box the cards that table.

Reviewer answer: The box that held the cards sat on the table.

Why: That held the cards identifies the box without repeating it as it, and sat on the table completes the main idea.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the repeated noun in the sentence pair.
2. Replace that repeated subject with a suitable relative word.
3. Put the detail after its noun and reread the main statement; do not repeat another subject in this pattern.

## Teaching 3: Change final y carefully when adding a suffix

**A suffix is an ending added to a word.**
For example, -ed can mark a past action and -er can compare. Look at the base word first, then the ending. Spelling changes depend on both; do not change a letter simply because you notice y. Happier means more happy.

**Know the letter labels.**
A, e, i, o and u are common vowel letters. R and p are consonant letters. Y can work differently in different words; for this spelling check, look at the letter immediately before it.

**Practise a consonant before y.**
In cry, the r before y is a consonant letter. Before the taught -ed ending, change y to i: cry + ed becomes cried. In happy, the p before y is a consonant; happy + er becomes happier. Add the whole ending after the change.

**Keep y before -ing.**
Cry + ing becomes crying, not criing. This is an important exception to the change practised above. Look at the suffix before deciding.

**Keep y after a vowel in these examples.**
Play has the vowel letter a before y. Play + ed becomes played. The change-to-i pattern does not apply. These models cover the words used here; some other spellings have exceptions and should be checked against a reliable model.

I DO:
cry + ed: consonant r before y; change y to i; add ed → cried.
happy + er: consonant p before y; change y to i; add er → happier.
cry + ing: the -ing exception; keep y → crying.
play + ed: vowel a before y; keep y → played.

YOU TRY:
Explain why cry + ing keeps y, then write the complete word without a plus sign.

CHECK:
The suffix is -ing, so keep the base word’s y: crying. The plus sign is a practice label, not part of the finished spelling.

Notice: Check the letter before y and the suffix. “Always change y” would make crying and played incorrect.

**Question:** Why does cry + ing keep y in the taught pattern?

1. All suffixes change y to i
2. The ending is -ing, which keeps y here
3. Cry has no consonants
4. Plus signs belong in the finished word

Reviewer answer: The ending is -ing, which keeps y here

Why: The -ing exception keeps y in crying. The consonant-before-y change practised with -ed does not apply to this ending.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the base word and suffix as separate parts.
2. Check the letter before y, then whether the suffix is -ing.
3. Choose the matching taught pattern before writing the whole word without a plus sign.

## Teaching 4: Double a final consonant when the pattern fits

### In-site episode: Check the base and the ending

Pip: I see y in cry. Should I change it whenever I add an ending?
Juno: Check which ending. Cry plus ed becomes cried, but cry plus ing keeps y in crying.
Pip: For hop plus ing, I check the short vowel and single final consonant. I need two p letters in hopping.
Juno: Compare jump. It ends in m and p, so jumping needs no extra p. Pause and use the checks before choosing a spelling.

Learner pause: the final line pauses before continuing.

**Hear the spoken parts first.**
A syllable is a spoken beat. Hop has one beat; happy has two. Say each word normally and tap its beats. We practise one-beat base words for this doubling rule.

**Listen to the vowel sound.**
The o in hop has a short vowel sound. Compare it with the o in hope, which sounds like the letter name. Short describes the sound here, not the number of letters in the whole word. Listen to the models hop, stop and rub before checking their written endings.

**Look at the base word’s ending.**
For the one-syllable words taught here, a short vowel followed by one final consonant often needs that consonant doubled before a vowel-starting suffix such as -ing or -ed. Hop has a short vowel, one vowel letter o and a single final consonant p. Hop + ing becomes hopping.

**Work through the checks.**
Say the base word: one syllable? Check a short vowel and one final consonant. Check that the suffix starts with a vowel. Stop + ed becomes stopped. Rub + ing becomes rubbing. We are not learning the rules for every longer word today.

**Compare a word that does not fit.**
Jump ends with two consonant letters, m and p. Jump + ing becomes jumping, with no extra p. Play does not fit the short-vowel single-final-consonant pattern; played keeps its base spelling. Do not double w, x or y using this rule.

**Read and compare the result.**
Say hopping, then check the two p letters against the model. Notice that hoping is a different word from the base hope. Doubling can preserve the intended base word and meaning.

I DO:
hop + ing → hopping: one syllable, short o, final single p, vowel-starting suffix.
stop + ed → stopped: one syllable, short o, final single p, vowel-starting suffix.
jump + ing → jumping: two consonant letters at the end; no extra p.

YOU TRY:
Use the checks for rub + ing. Write the result and explain the doubled letter.

CHECK:
Rubbing has two b letters. Rub fits our one-syllable short-vowel single-final-consonant pattern, and -ing starts with a vowel.

Notice: Check the whole pattern. The fact that a word is short does not mean its last letter must always be doubled.

**Question:** Why does jump + ing become jumping without another p?

1. Every -ing word loses p
2. The word has no vowel
3. The base ends with two consonant letters, m and p
4. All short words have the same ending

Reviewer answer: The base ends with two consonant letters, m and p

Why: Jump does not have just one consonant letter after its vowel; its ending mp does not fit the taught doubling pattern.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the ending of the base word before adding letters.
2. Check one syllable, short vowel, single final consonant and a vowel-starting suffix.
3. Compare a base ending in one consonant with one ending in two; apply only the pattern that fits.

## Put it all together

I DO: Edit a short scene using the taught patterns.
DRAFT
The child found the ribbon. The child made the map. She was hop-ing beside the chair. She was happy-er because the game could begin.

MY EDITS
1. Identify the child: combine the two statements as “The child who made the map found the ribbon.” Who made the map follows child and does not repeat she inside it.
2. Hop + ing fits the taught doubling pattern: hopping.
3. Happy + er has consonant p before y, so change y to i and add er: happier.
4. Read the finished scene for meaning and marks.

FINISHED SCENE
The child who made the map found the ribbon. She was hopping beside the chair. She felt happier because the game could begin.

WHY IT WORKS
The relative clause identifies the child; the main action still finishes the sentence. The two suffix decisions use different checks, not one general instruction to change every ending.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“The child who she made the map” repeats the subject. Remove she: who already refers to the child in this pattern.
“Criing” applies the y-to-i idea before -ing. Keep y instead: crying.

CHECK ROUTINE
Find the target noun → check the clause → find base and suffix → choose the matching pattern → compare with a model → reread the whole scene.

Diagram: Four comparisons show y changing before ed in cried, staying before ing in crying, and p doubling in hopping but not jumping.

Different endings need different checks.

### Help with this step

1. Read each edit and its reason separately.
2. Match the relative-clause edit to a noun and the suffix edit to its base.
3. Use the model’s checking order to explain one decision rather than copying the whole passage.

## Spelling

Say the base word and ending separately. Explain the matching check, then look, cover, write and compare with the model. Repair a difference and try again. Practice parts show word building; omit plus signs in the finished word.

cried: cry + ed → cried — Consonant r before y; change y to i before the taught -ed ending.
crying: cry + ing → crying — Keep y before -ing.
happier: happy + er → happier — Consonant p before y; change y to i before -er.
played: play + ed → played — Vowel a before y; keep y.
hopping: hop + ing → hopping — One-syllable short-vowel base with final single p; double p before -ing.
stopped: stop + ed → stopped — Double final p before -ed in this taught pattern.
jumping: jump + ing → jumping — Base ends in mp; do not add another p.

**Question:** Which reminder fits the taught word played?

1. Always change every y
2. Double the final y
3. The vowel before y means y stays here
4. Remove all ending letters

Reviewer answer: The vowel before y means y stays here

Why: Play has a before y, so the taught consonant-before-y change does not apply; played keeps y.

### Help with this step

1. Say the base and ending before covering a model.
2. Notice the letter before y or the final consonant pattern.
3. Write once, compare the exact letters, repair a difference and try again.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Sentence and suffix editor (about 20 minutes).
1. Join “The girl carried the sign” and “The girl found the ribbon” using who. Put the detail beside girl and keep a complete main statement.
2. Join “The box held the cards” and “The box sat on the shelf” using which or that. Reread both joined sentences.
3. Write these finished words: cry + ed; cry + ing; happy + er; play + ed; hop + ing; stop + ed; jump + ing. Use the taught checks and then compare with the spelling models.
4. Choose two of your words and use each in a complete sentence. They can describe a fictional game or display.
5. Explain one clause decision and one spelling decision. Use “I put the detail after ____ because ____” and “I checked ____ before adding ____.”
6. Reread and improve one part if needed. If your first attempt was correct, explain the check that confirmed it.
Work on paper or in the big box. A typed record is enough; upload is optional. Check: two complete joined statements; accurate suffix words; two sensible example sentences; explained decisions.

Reflect: Which check helped you avoid a repeated subject or a spelling change that did not fit?
