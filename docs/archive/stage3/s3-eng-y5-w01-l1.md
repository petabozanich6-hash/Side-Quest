# Year 5: Read a plot as a chain of choices

## Accept the quest

Become a Plot Investigator. Trace a character's goal, obstacle and choices, then explain how those choices lead to the ending.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to explain how a narrative's events connect through problems, choices and consequences.

- I can identify a character's goal and the obstacle to it.
- I can distinguish an important plot event from a background detail.
- I can explain how a choice causes a later event.
- I can explain a turning point and resolution using evidence.

**Materials:** This lesson contains the texts and examples you need; Optional: paper and a pencil

1. Read the mission and look for the goal, problem and choices you will investigate.

## Teaching 1: 🔎 Find the goal and obstacle

**A story needs more than events.**
A narrative tells a story. Its plot is the connected sequence of events. To follow the plot, begin with what a character wants and what gets in the way.

The opening, often called the orientation, introduces useful information about the people, place and situation. You do not need every detail to understand the problem.

**Name the goal.**
A goal is something a character is trying to achieve. Ask, “What would success look like for this person?” Use a specific action: label the seedling table before the market opens is clearer than do something good.

**Name the obstacle.**
An obstacle makes the goal harder to reach. It may be a damaged object, a difficult decision or a disagreement. The complication is the problem or disruption that drives the story forward.

**Try the two-part sentence.**
“The character wants ____, but ____.” Read the opening carefully before filling the gaps. An object mentioned in a story is not automatically its problem; explain what it prevents.

### Show me an example

I DO: Read this short opening.
Maya wanted to deliver a model to the library exhibition. When she lifted its box, one corner came loose.

I ask what Maya is trying to do. Her goal is to deliver the model. I ask what may stop her. The loose box corner could make carrying it difficult.

My explanation: “Maya wants to deliver her model, but its box has come loose.” The model is not itself the obstacle.

YOU TRY:
Ravi wanted to read his speech at the hall. He discovered that two pages were out of order.
Name the goal and obstacle before reading on.

CHECK:
His goal is to read the speech. The pages being out of order make that harder. “He has pages” is a detail, not an explanation of the obstacle.

**Notice:** Check the relationship between the problem and the goal. If your obstacle is just a noun, add what is wrong and why it matters.

**Question:** Maya wants to deliver her model, but its box has come loose. What is her goal?

1. The corner of a box
2. Delivering her model
3. Having a loose box
4. Finding an exhibition title

**Reviewer answer:** Delivering her model

**Explanation:** Delivering the model is what Maya is trying to achieve. The loose corner is an obstacle to that goal; the other details do not name what she wants to accomplish.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the difference between a goal and an obstacle.
2. Ask what success would look like for the character, then what makes it harder.
3. Use “The character wants ____, but ____.” Fill the first gap with an intended action, not an object.

## Teaching 2: 🔗 Connect a choice to its consequence

**Ask why the next event happens.**
A consequence is a result. A character's choice can cause a consequence, which creates the next part of the plot. Read for the connection, not only for what happens first.

**Order and cause are different.**
Then tells us an event follows another. Because or so can help explain a cause or result. Two events can happen in order without one causing the other.

“The bell rang. Then the sun appeared” tells an order. It does not establish that the bell made the sun appear. For a story connection, find what the text actually supports.

**Use the evidence.**
“The paper tore because Leo traced the faded letters with a pencil” connects an attempt with its result in our story. You can explain that connection without adding a new event.

**Test importance.**
Ask, “If I removed this event, would the next action still make sense?” Background details help us picture a scene. Important plot events usually change what the character can do next.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Maya taped the corner of the box. The model stayed inside when she lifted it.

Choice: Maya tapes the loose corner.
Consequence: She can lift the box with the model inside.
Connection: “Maya taped the corner, so she could carry the model.”

Compare: “Maya wore a green jumper. She taped the box.” The jumper detail does not explain why the box holds.

YOU TRY:
Ravi numbered his mixed pages. He could put the speech back in order.
Explain why the second event happens.

CHECK:
Numbering the pages helps him arrange the speech. You could write, “Ravi could order the speech because he numbered its pages.” Keep the explanation within what the text tells you.

**Teaching diagram:** A plot connection: a goal meets an obstacle; a choice leads to a consequence. Ask why the next event happens.

*Explain the connection, not only the order.*

**Notice:** Do not turn every pair of events into a cause. Look for a supported action-and-result link and leave out causes you have invented.

**Question:** Ravi numbered the mixed pages, so he could order his speech. Which part is the consequence?

1. He had some pages
2. The hall was nearby
3. He could order the speech
4. His name was Ravi

**Reviewer answer:** He could order the speech

**Explanation:** Being able to order the speech is the result of numbering the pages. The other details do not state the result of that action.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the action-and-result example.
2. Separate what the character does from what happens as a result.
3. Try joining the two parts with because or so. Check that the text supports the connection.

## Teaching 3: ↪️ Locate the turning point

**A turning point changes the direction.**
A turning point is a moment when an important discovery, decision or event changes how the problem develops. It is not automatically the exact middle sentence or the loudest moment.

**Track what changes.**
Before the turning point, a character may keep using an approach that does not work. Afterwards, the character follows a different route towards the goal. Name both sides of that change.

In our market story, tracing the large sign fails. Noor notices spare labels and chooses a different solution. The change is from repairing one large sign to making individual labels.

**Support your interpretation.**
Readers may choose different important moments. Explain why your moment changes the character's next action. “It is exciting” describes your reaction but does not explain its role.

**Make a before-and-after statement.**
“Before ____, the character ____. After ____, the character ____.” This helps you test whether the event changes the plot.

**Original in-site episode:** A turning point needs a change

**Pip:** A story's middle is not automatically its turning point. What should I look for?

**Juno:** Find a discovery or decision that changes the character's next action.

**Pip:** So I compare the approach before the moment with the approach afterwards.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Maya first tries carrying the broken box without fixing it. It tilts. She finds tape and decides to repair the corner.

I choose the repair decision as a turning point. Before it, she repeats a risky carrying attempt. After it, she changes the box before carrying it.

Weak explanation: “Finding tape is the turning point because tape appears.”
Better explanation: “Finding tape lets Maya change her plan from carrying the loose box to repairing it.”

YOU TRY:
Ravi tries reading the mixed pages from memory. Then he sees that the pages have numbered headings and rearranges them.
What changes in his approach?

CHECK:
He changes from trying to remember the speech to using the headings to organise its pages. That change explains why the new approach can help.

**Notice:** A turning point needs an explanation of change. Being halfway through the text is not evidence by itself.

**Question:** What best supports a turning-point explanation?

1. It changes the character's approach to the problem
2. It has the longest sentence
3. It is exactly halfway down the page
4. It includes a new colour word

**Reviewer answer:** It changes the character's approach to the problem

**Explanation:** A turning point changes the direction of the problem or the character's approach. Sentence length, page position and colour details do not establish that change.

### Help with this step

1. Reread the before-and-after explanation.
2. Find what approach the character uses on each side of the important moment.
3. Use “Before this moment, ____. Afterwards, ____.” Test whether the approach actually changes.

## Teaching 4: 🧭 Explain the resolution and change

**Show what happens to the problem.**
The resolution tells us how the main problem ends or settles. It may be a success, a partial success or an unresolved outcome. Do not assume every narrative has a happy ending.

For this week's practice stories, ask whether the opening goal has been achieved and what detail proves it. A final setting description is not enough if it tells us nothing about the problem.

**Notice what the character learns.**
A reflection or final response can show how the character's thinking changes. It is useful when supported by words or actions in the text. Avoid claiming someone becomes a different person without evidence.

**Write a plot explanation.**
Include goal, obstacle, significant attempt, turning point and result. Connect them with reasons. A plot explanation is more than copying every sentence.

**Reread to check.**
Could a reader who has not seen the story follow your explanation? Have you supported your claims without adding events? Revise one unclear connection.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Maya carries the repaired box to the exhibition. She puts the model on the display table and adds tape to her bag for next time.

Resolution: The model reaches the display table, so the delivery goal is achieved.
Possible change: Putting tape in her bag suggests she is preparing for future problems. I say suggests because I am explaining evidence.

COMMON MISTAKE:
“The ending is that Maya wears a green jumper.” This background detail does not show what happens to her delivery problem.

YOU TRY:
Ravi reads his reordered speech and keeps the numbered pages for the next event.
What resolves the problem?

CHECK:
Reading the speech in order resolves the mixed-page problem. Keeping the numbered pages suggests he values a useful preparation strategy.

**Notice:** Separate what the text states from what you infer. An inference is an evidence-based interpretation, not an invented event.

**Question:** Which detail best shows Maya's delivery problem is resolved?

1. She owns a bag
2. The box has a corner
3. Her jumper is green
4. The model is on the exhibition table

**Reviewer answer:** The model is on the exhibition table

**Explanation:** The model reaching the exhibition table shows that Maya achieved the delivery goal. The other details do not establish that the model arrived successfully.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the opening goal and the ending evidence.
2. Find an ending detail that tells you what happened to the main problem.
3. Ask whether the goal is achieved, partly achieved or still unresolved. Use a detail, not just the final sentence's position.

## Put it all together

I DO: Read our original story first.

THE SIGN BEFORE THE MARKET

Noor and Leo had promised to label the seedling table at the community hall before the Saturday market opened. Noor had painted a large sign the afternoon before. She hoped visitors would find their table without needing directions.

When they reached the hall, Noor unfolded the sign. A damp patch had blurred three words. The seedlings were ready, but nobody could read their names. The doors would open in twenty minutes.

Leo tried tracing the faded letters with a pencil. The paper tore under the point. Noor pressed her palms against the table. Repainting the whole sign would take too long.

Then she noticed the blank backs of last week's cardboard labels. Instead of one large sign, they could make a clear label for each tray. Noor wrote the names while Leo checked them against the list they had brought.

They arranged the labels where visitors could read them. When the doors opened, a visitor pointed to a tray and said its name correctly. Noor smiled. Their table looked different from her plan, but it was ready.

She tucked the damaged sign into her bag. Next time, she would bring spare labels before anything went wrong.

MY EVIDENCE-BASED PLOT EXPLANATION
Noor and Leo want the seedling table labelled before the market opens. The large sign is blurred and time is short. Leo tries tracing the letters, but the paper tears. Noor's discovery of blank-backed labels changes the approach: they stop trying to fix one sign and make individual labels instead. Because they write and check the labels, a visitor can identify a tray when the doors open. The labelling goal is achieved. Noor's plan to bring spare labels suggests that she has learned to prepare a backup.

WHY MY EXPLANATION WORKS
I connect the obstacle to the goal. I include the failed attempt because it explains why they need another approach. I name a turning point and show its effect. I use the visitor's response as evidence of the outcome. I distinguish the stated plan from my interpretation of what Noor learned.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Weak: “Noor goes to the hall. The doors open. Her plan changes.” This gives an order but leaves out the reason for the change. Fix: add the damaged sign, failed tracing attempt and choice to make individual labels.

**Teaching diagram:** Before and after the turning point: repairing the large sign changes to labelling each tray when spare labels are discovered.

*State what changes, then support it with a story detail.*

2. Follow one part of the model at a time and locate its evidence in the story.
3. Begin with a goal-and-obstacle note, then trace the significant attempt that follows.

## Quest check

Eight multiple-choice questions and two written responses. The unchanged 90% threshold requires 8/8 scored choices. The written responses accompany the submission for manual review.

**Question:** What is a character's goal?

1. A detail describing the weather
2. The longest sentence
3. Something the character is trying to achieve
4. Every object in the story

**Reviewer answer:** Something the character is trying to achieve

**Explanation:** A goal is what a character is trying to achieve. Descriptions, sentence length and lists of objects do not define the desired achievement.

**Question:** In the market story, what mainly obstructs the labelling goal?

1. The large sign is blurred
2. The seedlings have names
3. The hall has doors
4. Noor brings a bag

**Reviewer answer:** The large sign is blurred

**Explanation:** The blurred sign cannot identify the seedlings clearly. The other details do not by themselves prevent the table being labelled.

**Question:** Why does Leo's tracing attempt matter to the plot?

1. It gives the hall a colour
2. It describes the whole market
3. It tells us the time of year
4. Its failure helps explain the change of approach

**Reviewer answer:** Its failure helps explain the change of approach

**Explanation:** The paper tearing shows the old approach failing, which helps explain why a different solution is needed.

**Question:** Which pair clearly shows a choice and its consequence in the story?

1. Noor has a bag; the hall has doors
2. They write labels; a visitor identifies a tray
3. It is Saturday; Noor smiles
4. There are seedlings; Leo has a name

**Reviewer answer:** They write labels; a visitor identifies a tray

**Explanation:** Writing readable labels helps the visitor identify a tray. The other pairs do not establish a supported choice-and-result connection.

**Question:** What changes after Noor notices spare labels?

1. The opening time changes
2. They move to another hall
3. They make individual labels instead of repairing one sign
4. The story stops having a goal

**Reviewer answer:** They make individual labels instead of repairing one sign

**Explanation:** The discovery enables a different method for reaching the same goal: individual labels replace the attempt to repair one large sign.

**Question:** Why is page position alone a weak turning-point explanation?

1. A turning point needs evidence of a meaningful change
2. The middle of a page is never important
3. Stories cannot have paragraphs
4. Every sentence has the same effect

**Reviewer answer:** A turning point needs evidence of a meaningful change

**Explanation:** A turning point is explained by a change in direction or approach. Being in the middle does not prove that function; a middle event can still matter if it causes a change.

**Question:** Which detail supports the resolution?

1. The afternoon before is mentioned
2. Noor once painted a sign
3. The hall has a table
4. A visitor correctly names a tray

**Reviewer answer:** A visitor correctly names a tray

**Explanation:** The visitor's correct identification shows the new labels are doing their job, supporting achievement of the labelling goal.

**Question:** Which explanation distinguishes interpretation from a stated event?

1. Noor secretly builds a new hall
2. Her plan to bring spares suggests she learned to prepare a backup
3. The text says Leo owns ten trays
4. Noor never encounters problems again

**Reviewer answer:** Her plan to bring spares suggests she learned to prepare a backup

**Explanation:** The plan to bring spare labels is stated evidence; saying it suggests learning clearly marks an interpretation. The other claims invent unsupported details.

**Question:** Explain the turning point in the market story using a before-and-after comparison.

Written response, reviewed by a grown-up.

**Reviewer model:** Before Noor noticed the spare labels, they tried repairing the large sign. Afterwards, they made individual labels, giving them a different way to reach the goal.

**Review guide:** Accept a supported change of approach with both before and after. The discovery or decision may be named as the key moment.

**Explanation:** A strong explanation identifies how the moment changes what the characters do, not just where it appears.

**Question:** Choose a consequence of a character's action and explain it using because or so.

Written response, reviewed by a grown-up.

**Reviewer model:** They wrote and checked individual labels, so a visitor could identify a tray correctly.

**Review guide:** Accept another accurate action-and-result pair supported by the story. Require a genuine connection rather than merely two consecutive events.

**Explanation:** The explanation should connect a supported action to its result without inventing a cause.

1. Return to the teaching step matching the term in the question.
2. Separate goal, obstacle, action and result before comparing options.
3. For a written explanation, use a before-and-after comparison or a supported because/so link. Do not add events.
