## Accept the quest

Become a Clarity Editor. Repair a short narrative by keeping its time clear, linking causes and making sure readers know who acts.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to use tense, linking words and clear references to connect narrative events.

- I can keep a short past-time narrative consistent.
- I can distinguish a sequence link from a cause or result link.
- I can replace an unclear pronoun with a clear noun.
- I can explain how one edit improves a reader's understanding.

**Materials:** This lesson contains the texts and examples you need; Optional: paper and a pencil

### Help with this step

1. Read the mission and notice the three kinds of clarity check.
2. You will practise each check separately before editing a paragraph.
3. Begin with a narrated verb and ask when its action happened. You can add linking words later.

## Teaching 1: 🕰️ Keep the story's time clear

**A verb shows an action or state.**
In “Noor checked the label”, checked is the verb. It tells us what Noor did. Verb tense helps a reader understand when an action takes place.

**Choose a time frame.**
This practice narrative tells events that already happened, so it uses past tense: checked, noticed and wrote. Many regular verbs form the past with -ed; some, such as write → wrote, change differently.

Stories can use other time frames deliberately. For this task, practise a consistent past-time account. A sudden shift to present tense without a reason can confuse the reader about when events occur.

**Find and test the verbs.**
Read one sentence. Ask, “Is this action happening now in my account, or did it already happen?” If you are telling past events, choose the past form. Read the next sentence to check that the time remains clear.

**Do not change quoted words automatically.**
A character may say “The label is ready” during a past-time story. The narrator can write “Noor said, 'The label is ready.'” The quoted speech shows the character's words at that moment.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Draft: “Noor checked the label. Leo writes a new one.”
The first verb tells a past event. Writes shifts to present without a reason. I revise: “Noor checked the label. Leo wrote a new one.”

I do not change every word to end in -ed. Write becomes wrote, not writed.

YOU TRY:
“Yesterday, Ana plans the display and checked her notes.”
Find the inconsistent verb and try a past form.

CHECK:
“Yesterday, Ana planned the display and checked her notes.” Planned and checked fit the past-time account. The spelling segment will show why planned has a doubled n.

COMPARE:
“Ana said, 'My plan is ready.'” Said is the narrator's past verb; is belongs to Ana's spoken words. Keeping the quote as spoken can make sense.

**Notice:** A tense change can be purposeful in another story. Here, change only an accidental shift in a past-time account; do not rewrite a character's spoken words simply because the narration is past.

**Question:** Which sentence keeps both narrated actions in past tense?

1. Noor checked the label and Leo writes another
2. Noor checks the label and Leo wrote another
3. Noor checked the label and Leo wrote another
4. Noor checking the label and Leo writes another

**Reviewer answer:** Noor checked the label and Leo wrote another

**Explanation:** Checked and wrote both place the narrated actions in the past. The other choices shift tense or lack a complete consistent past-time account.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the difference between narration and a character's spoken words.
2. Find each narrated action and decide whether it fits the past-time account.
3. Try reading the line with Yesterday at its beginning. Compare the action forms without changing quoted speech mechanically.

## Teaching 2: 🔗 Show order, cause and result

**Choose the link you mean.**
A sequence link shows order: first, next, then or after. A cause link explains why, often using because. A result link can use so to show what follows from an action or situation.

These words are not interchangeable decorations. Choose one because it expresses the relationship you want a reader to follow.

**Compare order with cause.**
“Noor found labels. Then she wrote names” puts events in order.
“Noor wrote new labels because the old sign was unreadable” explains why she wrote them.

Both sentences can be useful, but they do different jobs. Do not invent a cause merely because two events appear together.

**Place the parts carefully.**
Because introduces the reason. “Because the sign was unreadable, Noor wrote labels” puts the reason first and uses a comma after that opening part.
“Noor wrote labels because the sign was unreadable” puts the reason second.

**Read for meaning.**
Check what the link says. If because gives the wrong reason or so gives an impossible result, the sentence needs revising.

**Original in-site episode:** Choose the relationship before the link

**Pip:** I use then in every sentence. My events are in order, but the reason is still missing.

**Juno:** Ask whether you need order, a cause or a result. Choose a linking word for that relationship.

**Pip:** Because can introduce the reason. So can connect a situation to a supported result.

**Juno:** Pause and try. Explain the relationship between two notes before joining them. **[Pause to try; press Play to continue.]**

### Show me an example

I DO:
Two notes: “The old sign was unreadable. Noor made new labels.”
Cause version: “Noor made new labels because the old sign was unreadable.”
Result version: “The old sign was unreadable, so Noor made new labels.”

I keep the same relationship: the damaged sign explains the new-label choice. I am not claiming that making labels damaged the sign.

YOU TRY:
“The tape failed. Ana used string.”
Write a cause or result link before comparing this suggestion.

CHECK:
“Ana used string because the tape failed” explains her reason. “The tape failed, so Ana used string” shows her response. Both need the surrounding story to support that connection.

**Notice:** First decide the relationship, then choose a linking word. Using then everywhere gives order but may leave important reasons unexplained.

**Question:** Which word most directly introduces a reason?

1. then
2. next
3. first
4. because

**Reviewer answer:** because

**Explanation:** Because introduces a reason. Then, next and first usually organise the order rather than explain why something happens.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at order, reason and result as different relationships.
2. Ask whether you mean what comes next or why an action happens.
3. Name the relationship first, then test which word expresses it without adding an unsupported reason.

## Teaching 3: 👤 Make references clear

**A pronoun can stand in for a noun.**
Words such as she, he, they and it can refer back to a person or thing. They avoid unnecessary repetition, but only help when a reader can identify the reference.

**Find the noun behind the word.**
In “Noor wrote a label. She checked it”, she refers to Noor and it refers to the label. The earlier sentence supplies clear references.

Now compare: “The label fell on the poster. It was damaged.” Does it mean the label or the poster? The sentence leaves two possible things.

**Repair uncertainty.**
Repeat the specific noun where it helps. “The label fell on the poster. The label was damaged” makes the intended meaning clear.
A repeated noun is useful when it prevents a misunderstanding.

**Do not remove every pronoun.**
A paragraph repeating a name in every sentence may sound clumsy. Keep clear references and repair uncertain ones. Read as if you have not seen the author's plan.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Draft: “Noor put the card beside the sign. It had a smudged word.”
I intend the sign to have the smudge, but the reader could think it means the card.

Revision: “Noor put the card beside the sign. The sign had a smudged word.”
The added noun makes my intended reference clear.

YOU TRY:
“Leo placed the list under the label. It was torn.”
You intend the list to be torn. Rewrite only what is needed.

CHECK:
“Leo placed the list under the label. The list was torn.” Keeping a specific noun removes the uncertain reference. You do not need to add an unrelated event.

**Notice:** When two nearby nouns could fit it, use the exact noun at the important point. Do not expect the reader to know what you meant privately.

**Question:** In 'The label fell on the poster. It was damaged', why might it need replacing?

1. It could refer to either the label or the poster
2. Pronouns never belong in narratives
3. Poster must always be removed
4. A sentence cannot begin with it

**Reviewer answer:** It could refer to either the label or the poster

**Explanation:** Both objects could be the damaged one, so the reference is uncertain. Pronouns can be useful and can begin sentences when their meaning is clear.

### Help with this step

1. Find both nouns near the uncertain reference.
2. Ask which object the writer intends the pronoun to name.
3. Try replacing the pronoun with a specific noun and check that the intended event stays the same.

## Teaching 4: 🧰 Edit a connected passage

**Edit one kind of problem at a time.**
Checking everything at once can make a small passage feel complicated. Use three passes: time, connections, references.

**Pass 1: time.**
Find the narrated verbs. In a past-time account, check their past forms. Leave direct speech meaningful rather than changing it mechanically.

**Pass 2: connections.**
Ask which events need an order link and which need a reason or result. Add a link only where the relationship is supported.

**Pass 3: references.**
Follow each pronoun back to a person or thing. If two nouns could fit, name the intended one.

**Reread the whole result.**
An edit should make the passage clearer without changing the original events. Explain one change: “I changed ____ to ____ so the reader can ____.”
Then check capitals and sentence-ending punctuation. A corrected spelling alone will not repair a missing cause or uncertain reference.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Draft: “The sign was blurred. Noor makes labels. She puts a card beside the list. It was torn.”
Intended meaning: the list was torn.

Time pass: change makes to made and puts to put.
Connection pass: add so where the blurred sign gives Noor a reason to make labels.
Reference pass: replace the final it with the list.

Revision:
“The sign was blurred, so Noor made labels. She put a card beside the list. The list was torn.”

EXPLANATION:
I kept the events but repaired the time and relationship. Naming the list prevents readers confusing it with the card.

YOU TRY:
“The tape failed. Ana uses string. It holds the label.”
Try a past-time account and a result link.

CHECK:
“The tape failed, so Ana used string. The string held the label.” This makes the time and response clear.

**Teaching diagram:** Three editing passes check verb time, event connections and pronoun references, then reread without changing the events.

*Use one pass at a time.*

**Notice:** Explain edits by their effect on meaning. A longer sentence is not automatically a better sentence; clarity is the test.

**Question:** Which editing method matches this lesson?

1. Change all words at random
2. Check time, connections and references, then reread
3. Remove every noun
4. Add because whether or not a reason exists

**Reviewer answer:** Check time, connections and references, then reread

**Explanation:** The three passes focus on specific clarity problems and finish with a whole-passage check. Random changes or unsupported links can damage meaning.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the three editing passes.
2. Check one type of problem before moving to the next.
3. Underline narrated actions, then inspect links, then follow each pronoun back to its intended noun.

## Put it all together

I DO: An edit with reasons
ORIGINAL PRACTICE DRAFT
Yesterday, Noor checks the display. The sign was blurred. She makes new labels. She puts a card beside the list. It was torn.
Intended meaning: the list was torn.

REVISED PASSAGE
Yesterday, Noor checked the display. The sign was blurred, so she made new labels. She put a card beside the list. The list was torn.

WHY I CHANGED IT
Checked and made fit a past-time account. Put is the same written form in present and past, so its role depends on the sentence; here it narrates a past action. So connects the damaged sign with the supported response. The list replaces an uncertain it.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Weak edit: “She made new labels because she made new labels.” This repeats the action instead of giving a reason.
Fix: identify the actual reason in the text: the old sign was blurred.

FINAL CHECK
I have not invented a new problem or changed which item is torn. I have made the existing events easier to follow.

**Teaching diagram:** Three linking words do different jobs: then shows order, because introduces a reason, so connects to a result.

*Use a link that matches your intended relationship.*

## Spelling: Past-time verb endings

Some regular past verbs add -ed: walk → walked and check → checked. A verb already ending in e adds d: notice → noticed. A consonant before a final y can change y to i before ed: try → tried. In the one-syllable verb plan, a short vowel is followed by one final consonant, so the n doubles before ed: planned. These examples are regular patterns, not a claim that every past verb uses ed; write → wrote is irregular. Say the base word, identify its ending, apply the pattern and read the finished word.

| Word | Parts | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| walked | walk + ed | Add ed to walk. |
| checked | check + ed | Add ed to check. |
| noticed | notice + d | Notice ends in e; add d. |
| tried | try → tri + ed | Change consonant plus final y to i before ed. |
| planned | plan → plann + ed | Double the final n in this short one-syllable verb before ed. |

**Question:** Which past form correctly follows try → consonant plus final y?

1. tryed
2. try
3. tried
4. tryied

**Reviewer answer:** tried

**Explanation:** The consonant before the final y allows y to change to i before adding ed, giving tried. The other forms do not follow this pattern.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the base word before adding a past ending.
2. Check whether the base ends in e, consonant plus y or a simple consonant pattern.
3. Say the base word, identify its ending, apply that example's pattern and reread. Remember some past verbs change differently.

**Question:** What does tense help a reader understand?

1. When narrated actions take place
2. Only the colour of an object
3. How many paragraphs a text has
4. The size of a heading

**Reviewer answer:** When narrated actions take place

**Explanation:** Tense helps establish the time of an action or state. It does not describe heading size, colour or paragraph count.

**Question:** Which keeps both narrated actions in past tense?

1. Leo checked and Noor reviews
2. Leo checks and Noor reviewed
3. Leo checked and Noor reviewed
4. Leo checking and Noor reviews

**Reviewer answer:** Leo checked and Noor reviewed

**Explanation:** Checked and reviewed both fit a past-time account. The other choices mix forms or do not give a complete consistent account.

**Question:** Why need quoted present words not change automatically in a past narrative?

1. Quotation marks remove all grammar
2. Stories can never include speech
3. Every quoted verb must end in ed
4. The quote can show the character's words at that moment

**Reviewer answer:** The quote can show the character's words at that moment

**Explanation:** Direct speech can preserve what the character says at that moment while the narrator uses past tense.

**Question:** What does then usually show?

1. The reason an event happened
2. The order of events
3. Which noun is damaged
4. An irregular verb form

**Reviewer answer:** The order of events

**Explanation:** Then usually signals what follows in the sequence. It does not itself establish a cause, reference or verb pattern.

**Question:** Which sentence explains a reason supported by the practice context?

1. Noor made labels because the old sign was blurred
2. Noor made labels because labels made themselves
3. Noor made labels then because first
4. The sign blurred because the hall had a name

**Reviewer answer:** Noor made labels because the old sign was blurred

**Explanation:** The unreadable sign is the supported reason for making labels. The other choices are circular, unclear or invent an unsupported cause.

**Question:** Which word can connect a situation with its result?

1. yesterday
2. they
3. so
4. card

**Reviewer answer:** so

**Explanation:** So can introduce a result or response. The other words do not function as that cause-and-result link here.

**Question:** What is the problem in 'The list touched the card. It was torn'?

1. It cannot be a pronoun
2. Torn must mean both items
3. The sentence contains no nouns
4. It could refer to either nearby item

**Reviewer answer:** It could refer to either nearby item

**Explanation:** Both the list and card are possible references for it. Naming the intended item removes uncertainty.

**Question:** Which past form of try is correct?

1. tryed
2. tried
3. tryied
4. tryinged

**Reviewer answer:** tried

**Explanation:** Try has a consonant before final y, so y changes to i before ed: tried.
