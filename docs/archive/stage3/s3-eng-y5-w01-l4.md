# Year 5: Prepare and deliver a clear retelling

Stage 3 · Year 5 · English · Week 1 · Lesson 4 · 60 minutes

## Accept the quest

Become a Story Presenter. Turn a complete narrative into a connected retelling, prepare readable cues and improve one part of your delivery.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to retell significant narrative events clearly for a listener, using a readable cue card and purposeful delivery.

- I can select events needed to explain a plot.
- I can connect a goal, problem, choice and result in a retelling.
- I can prepare readable cue notes without copying a whole script.
- I can rehearse and explain one improvement to my delivery.

**Materials:** This lesson contains the texts and examples you need; Optional: paper and a pencil

### Help with this step

1. Read the mission and notice that you will select, prepare and rehearse.
2. Begin with the story's goal and problem; delivery practice comes later.
3. Keep paper ready for short cues if you want handwritten practice, or plan to type notes.

## Teaching 1: 🎯 Choose what your listener needs

**A retelling is a new account of a story.**
You tell the important events in your own words while keeping the original meaning. A listener who has not read the text needs enough information to follow its main problem and outcome.

**Find a small event chain.**
Identify who wants what, what blocks the goal, what the character tries, what changes and how the problem ends. A goal is an intended achievement; a turning point changes the direction; a resolution shows how the main problem settles.

You may keep a setting detail when it explains the pressure or action. If a market opens soon, the timing helps explain why a character changes plans. Listing every object can bury that connection.

**Keep facts and interpretation distinct.**
Do not invent an event to make a short retelling exciting. If you describe what a character learns, support it with a word or action and use a phrase such as suggests when appropriate.

**Test the selection.**
Ask, “Would leaving this out make the next important action confusing?” Keep the information needed for the listener to follow the story.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Short story notes: Maya needs to deliver a model. The box corner breaks. A first carrying attempt tilts. She repairs the corner. The model reaches the exhibition.

I keep the goal, broken corner, unsuccessful attempt, repair and successful delivery. A green-jumper detail is not needed to explain the repair.

COMPARE:
“Maya has a box and a jumper” is too little plot.
A description of every chair at the exhibition may be too much unrelated information.

YOU TRY:
If a speech's pages are mixed and numbering them solves the order problem, which actions would a listener need?

CHECK:
Include the speech goal, mixed pages, the numbering choice and the resulting ordered reading. Explain the main problem rather than listing possessions.

**Notice:** A shorter retelling still needs the connections. Cut unneeded details rather than removing the reason the character changes approach.

**Question:** Which detail is most important in a retelling about a broken delivery box?

1. A list of unrelated chairs
2. The colour of every wall
3. The character's name written three times
4. The repair that lets the model arrive

**Reviewer answer:** The repair that lets the model arrive

**Explanation:** The repair explains how the obstacle is overcome and the delivery goal is achieved. The other details do not explain that plot connection.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the question about what a listener needs.
2. Find which event explains the next important choice.
3. Test removing the detail. If the main action becomes confusing, explain why that detail matters.

## Teaching 2: 🔗 Build a spoken chain

**Order helps, but reasons matter too.**
A listener hears one sentence at a time. Give them an understandable beginning, connected middle and ending. Use the order of the story unless you deliberately explain another arrangement.

Sequence words such as first, then and finally can organise events. Because introduces a reason; so can show a result or response. Choose a link for the relationship, not just to avoid repeating a word.

**Use your own wording.**
Read a part, look away and explain what happened. Then check the text for accuracy. You can keep a character's name or an essential term without copying every original sentence.

**Make an opening useful.**
Name the main character and goal before the obstacle. “This story follows Noor, who wants...” helps an unfamiliar listener more than “And then it happened.”

**Close the chain.**
Show the outcome of the original problem. A final thought about learning can follow, but it should not replace explaining how the goal ends.

### Show me an example

I DO:
“This story follows Maya, who wants to deliver her model. The box corner breaks, so carrying it becomes difficult. After an unsuccessful try, she repairs the corner. The model reaches the exhibition because the repaired box can hold it.”

My opening gives a goal. So explains the problem's effect; after shows sequence; because supports the outcome. I keep the original meaning in my own words.

YOU TRY:
Retell a mixed-page speech problem in three sentences: goal and obstacle; useful choice; outcome.

CHECK:
A possible chain names the speaking goal, explains the mixed pages, shows how numbering helps and ends with the ordered speech. Your exact words can differ.

**Notice:** Do not begin with unexplained he, she, it or they. Introduce the person or thing before expecting the listener to identify it.

**Question:** What is a useful opening for an unfamiliar listener?

1. And then it happened
2. This story follows Noor, who wants the table labelled before opening
3. She did that because of it
4. Finally, without any beginning

**Reviewer answer:** This story follows Noor, who wants the table labelled before opening

**Explanation:** The opening identifies a character and goal so the listener can understand subsequent problems and choices. The other openings rely on missing information.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the model's character-and-goal opening.
2. Ask what an unfamiliar listener knows before your first sentence.
3. Name the character and intended achievement before explaining the obstacle.

## Teaching 3: 🎙️ Rehearse for clarity

**Delivery is how you present the words.**
A clear retelling needs understandable content and delivery. Pace is speed, volume is loudness and pitch is how high or low your voice sounds. Pause briefly between important thoughts.

**Choose from meaning and audience.**
Use a comfortable volume a listener can hear. Give an important cause or change enough time. You may emphasise a key word, but shouting every sentence hides rather than highlights the connection.

**Try a small part.**
Say one opening and one cause-and-result sentence. Listen for words that merge together or become unclear. You do not need a recording; you can notice what you rush while rehearsing.

**Improve one thing.**
First rehearsal: check whether all important plot parts are present.
Second rehearsal: choose one delivery change, such as pausing after the goal or slowing the turning-point explanation.

**Explain your choice.**
“I slowed ____ so the listener could ____.” A useful improvement has a purpose, rather than being a demand to sound dramatic.

**Original in-site episode:** Rehearse with a reason

**Pip:** My retelling includes the problem and solution, but I rush the important change.

**Juno:** Practise that connection at a steadier pace. Give the listener time to follow the choice.

**Pip:** I will use short cues and explain the events in my own words.

### Show me an example

I DO:
First try: I rush “She repaired the box so the model could be carried.” The repair and result blur together.
Second try: I keep the words clear and pause briefly after the repair thought. The listener has time to follow why it matters.

COMPARE:
Getting louder does not automatically fix missing or rushed words. First make the content and pace clear.

YOU TRY:
Say “The pages were mixed, so Ravi numbered them” twice. In the second reading, make the reason for his response easier to follow.

CHECK:
A slightly slower pace or brief thought pause can help. Keep the relationship intact; do not separate every word or add an invented action.

**Notice:** There is no required accent or performance voice. Aim for a listener to follow the meaning using your comfortable way of speaking.

**Question:** Your turning-point explanation was rushed. What is a purposeful rehearsal change?

1. Shout all the words
2. Remove the problem
3. Give the explanation more time and keep the words clear
4. Invent a louder event

**Reviewer answer:** Give the explanation more time and keep the words clear

**Explanation:** A steadier pace gives the listener time to follow the change. Shouting, removing the problem or inventing events does not repair rushed explanation.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the difference between content and delivery.
2. Find a rushed connection and choose one comfortable change.
3. Say that short connection twice and listen for the words and reason becoming clearer.

## Teaching 4: 🗒️ Make a readable cue card

**A cue prompts memory.**
A cue card uses short notes to remind you of the event chain. It is different from a complete script. If you copy every sentence, you may spend the presentation trying to read tiny words instead of retelling.

**Use a small structure.**
For this task, write five labels: goal, obstacle, attempt, change and outcome. Add a few meaningful words beneath each. These are practice categories, not a rule for every kind of presentation.

**Make handwriting usable.**
On paper, keep letter shapes distinguishable, word spacing clear and size consistent enough to read at a glance. Give each cue its own line. If one word is cramped or unclear, rewrite that part rather than decorating the whole card.

You may type the cues if that is your accessible writing method. A typed card can support the retelling, but it does not demonstrate handwriting.

**Test rather than assume.**
Set the card at a comfortable reading distance. Say one cue, then explain its event in a complete spoken sentence. If the cue does not remind you of the event, revise the wording.

### Show me an example

I DO: Five notes for Maya's story
Goal: deliver model
Obstacle: loose box corner
Attempt: carrying tilts box
Change: repair with tape
Outcome: model reaches exhibition

Each line reminds me of a connected event. I say the full explanation aloud rather than trying to perform only the two-word notes.

COMMON MISTAKE:
One crowded paragraph of tiny notes is difficult to glance at. Fix: separate the event labels, use readable words and leave space.

YOU TRY:
Write a cue for a character using page numbers to organise a speech. Can you explain the action from that cue?

CHECK:
“Change: number and order pages” is more useful than a lone word such as stuff. Check the readability of your actual card, not just its appearance.

**Teaching diagram:** A cue card with five separately spaced event labels: goal, obstacle, attempt, change and outcome.

*Use short readable notes, then explain the full connection aloud.*

**Notice:** A cue needs enough meaning to prompt you, but not a whole copied script. Review handwritten clarity only when you actually wrote the card by hand.

**Question:** Which cue is most useful for a page-ordering choice?

1. Stuff
2. Everything happened
3. A whole unrelated paragraph
4. Change: number and order pages

**Reviewer answer:** Change: number and order pages

**Explanation:** The short cue identifies the action that changes the situation. Vague words and unrelated long text do not prompt that important event clearly.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at short cues versus a full script.
2. Ask which note would prompt the action you need to explain.
3. Choose a brief event label and a few meaningful action words, then try speaking a complete sentence from it.

## Put it all together

I DO: Read the complete original story.
THE SIGN BEFORE THE MARKET

Noor and Leo had promised to label the seedling table at the community hall before the Saturday market opened. Noor had painted a large sign the afternoon before. She hoped visitors would find their table without needing directions.

When they reached the hall, Noor unfolded the sign. A damp patch had blurred three words. The seedlings were ready, but nobody could read their names. The doors would open in twenty minutes.

Leo tried tracing the faded letters with a pencil. The paper tore under the point. Noor pressed her palms against the table. Repainting the whole sign would take too long.

Then she noticed the blank backs of last week's cardboard labels. Instead of one large sign, they could make a clear label for each tray. Noor wrote the names while Leo checked them against the list they had brought.

They arranged the labels where visitors could read them. When the doors opened, a visitor pointed to a tray and said its name correctly. Noor smiled. Their table looked different from her plan, but it was ready.

She tucked the damaged sign into her bag. Next time, she would bring spare labels before anything went wrong.

MODEL RETELLING
“This story follows Noor and Leo, who want to label a seedling table before the market opens. Their large sign is blurred, so the plants are difficult to identify. Leo tries tracing the words, but the paper tears. Noor notices spare labels and changes the approach: they label each tray instead of repairing the large sign. Because they write and check the labels, a visitor can identify a tray when the doors open. Noor plans to bring spare labels next time, which suggests she has learned to prepare a backup.”

MY CUE CARD
Goal: label before opening
Obstacle: blurred sign
Attempt: tracing tears paper
Change: individual labels
Outcome: visitor identifies tray

WHY IT WORKS
The listener gets the goal before the problem. Each important choice connects to a result. The final learning interpretation is marked with suggests. The cue notes prompt the model but are not a copied script.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Too vague: “They went to the hall and changed something.” Fix: name the sign problem and the change to individual labels.

**Teaching diagram:** The cue card prompts memory; spoken sentences explain the plot; pauses help separate thoughts.

*Notes and delivery have different jobs.*

## Do the task

YOU DO: A clear retelling and cue-card task (about 20 minutes)
Use the market story included above.
1. Select five important plot notes: goal, obstacle, attempt, change and outcome.
2. Write a cue card by hand on paper, or type it if needed. Keep each note readable and short enough to glance at. If handwritten, check letter shapes, spacing and a consistent readable size.
3. Prepare a retelling of about one to two minutes. Use your own wording and at least one supported cause-and-result connection. You can practise alone; a live listener or recording is optional.
4. Rehearse twice. Between tries, choose and make one purposeful delivery improvement.
5. In the big text box, include the five cues, your retelling wording or a clear written summary, and the delivery change with its reason. Keep the physical cue card for review if you used one.
Check: a new listener can follow the goal and outcome; events are accurate; cues prompt rather than replace the explanation; one improvement is explained.

**Reflect:** Which cue or delivery change most helped you explain the plot, and why?

### Help with this step

1. Look back at your five event notes and begin with the opening.
2. Use “This story follows ____, who wants ____.” Keep the first sentence clear.
3. A partial opening shape is “Before the event began, ____ needed to ____.” Fill the gaps, then introduce the obstacle.

## Quest check

Eight multiple-choice questions and two written responses. The unchanged 90% threshold requires 8/8 scored choices. The written responses accompany the submission for manual review.

**Question:** What is the purpose of a retelling?

1. Copy every sentence without deciding what matters
2. Tell important events in your own words while keeping meaning
3. Invent a different resolution and call it the original
4. List every object in order of colour

**Reviewer answer:** Tell important events in your own words while keeping meaning

**Explanation:** A retelling preserves the important events and meaning using the speaker's wording. It is not a copied inventory or an invented replacement.

**Question:** Which detail is essential to the market retelling?

1. The colour of every chair
2. A list of all visitors' names
3. The sign problem that leads to new labels
4. An invented prize at the end

**Reviewer answer:** The sign problem that leads to new labels

**Explanation:** The damaged sign explains the obstacle and new-label response. The other details are unnecessary or unsupported.

**Question:** Why should a retelling introduce a character before using unclear pronouns?

1. An unfamiliar listener needs to know who is being discussed
2. Pronouns are always forbidden
3. Names must appear in every sentence
4. Every opening must rhyme

**Reviewer answer:** An unfamiliar listener needs to know who is being discussed

**Explanation:** An introduced person gives the listener a reference for later pronouns. Pronouns can still be used when clear.

**Question:** Which link most directly introduces a reason?

1. then
2. finally
3. first
4. because

**Reviewer answer:** because

**Explanation:** Because introduces a reason. The other words commonly organise sequence.

**Question:** What should the end of the retelling explain?

1. Only the number of lines spoken
2. How the main problem ends or settles
3. An unrelated favourite colour
4. All possible future stories

**Reviewer answer:** How the main problem ends or settles

**Explanation:** The ending closes the central event chain by showing what happens to the main problem.

**Question:** Which delivery choice is useful for a rushed cause-and-result explanation?

1. Speak at a steadier pace and keep words clear
2. Shout every word
3. Remove the cause
4. Change the story's events

**Reviewer answer:** Speak at a steadier pace and keep words clear

**Explanation:** A steadier pace improves clarity while preserving the connection. Loudness alone or removing information does not fix rushing.

**Question:** What is the job of a cue card?

1. Replace every spoken sentence with one vague word
2. Hold an unrelated drawing
3. Prompt the main event chain with short notes
4. Prove a story must have five paragraphs

**Reviewer answer:** Prompt the main event chain with short notes

**Explanation:** Short meaningful notes prompt the event chain. The practice categories do not impose a paragraph rule on stories.

**Question:** Which note supports useful handwritten cue-card checking?

1. Decoration alone proves readability
2. Typed notes always prove handwriting skill
3. All notes should be squeezed onto one line
4. Check letter clarity, word spacing and readable size

**Reviewer answer:** Check letter clarity, word spacing and readable size

**Explanation:** Letter clarity, spacing and size determine whether handwritten words can be used at a glance. Decoration and typing do not establish handwriting evidence.

**Question:** Explain why you kept one particular event in your retelling.

Written response, reviewed by a grown-up.

**Reviewer model:** I kept the tracing attempt because its failure explains why Noor needs a different approach.

**Review guide:** Accept a significant event and explanation of its role. Do not require the exact example.

**Explanation:** A strong answer links selection to what the listener needs for understanding.

**Question:** Describe one rehearsal improvement and its purpose.

Written response, reviewed by a grown-up.

**Reviewer model:** I slowed the turning-point sentence so the listener could follow the change from one sign to individual labels.

**Review guide:** Accept a specific change in pace, clarity, pausing, emphasis or organisation and a sensible purpose.

**Explanation:** A strong answer identifies the improvement and its effect rather than simply saying the second try was better.

### Help with this step

1. Return to the step about selection, connection, delivery or cues.
2. Ask what each choice would help a listener understand.
3. For a written response, name an event or improvement and explain its purpose rather than copying a whole model.
