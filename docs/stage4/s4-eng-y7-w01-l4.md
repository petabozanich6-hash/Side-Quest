# Year 7: Read aloud and discuss with evidence

## Accept the quest

Prepare a purposeful reading and a considered response. Use the story’s meaning to guide delivery, then test your interpretation against another supported view.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to read a short narrative passage clearly and discuss interpretations through evidence, clarification and reflection.

- I can prepare a passage by checking meaning and word groups.
- I can choose a delivery change with a reason.
- I can support an interpretation with a precise detail.
- I can respond to another view accurately and respectfully.
- I can explain how rehearsal or another interpretation affected my response.

**Materials:** All texts and examples are included in this lesson; Optional: paper and a pen for notes

### Help with this step

1. Read the challenge and notice preparation, delivery and reasoning as separate parts.
2. Begin by understanding one excerpt before planning its performance.
3. Keep notes for a delivery choice and a supported interpretation as you progress.

## Teaching 1: Prepare meaning before delivery

**Reading aloud starts with understanding.**
Read the chosen passage quietly first. Identify who speaks, what happens and which words need attention. Do not select a dramatic voice before checking the meaning.

**Separate narrator and dialogue.**
Quotation marks mark a character’s spoken words. The surrounding narration can describe actions or qualify how we understand the line. Read those words too rather than treating the speech as a separate story.

**Clarify unfamiliar words in this text.**
A foyer is an entrance space inside a building. An exhibitor is someone showing work at an exhibition. An assumption is something accepted without checking it here. Use these meanings in the passage; do not guess a new event from an unfamiliar word.

**Group words into meaningful phrases.**
A phrase is a meaningful word group. Keep a group such as before deciding it’s closed together enough for its relationship to be heard. Punctuation and meaning can guide brief pauses.

Mark two or three practice cues if helpful. A slash can remind you of a pause, but it is not part of the original text to pronounce.

### Show me an example

I DO:
“We can check before deciding it’s closed.” Ari picked up his folder. He did not touch Mara’s model.

I identify Ari’s spoken line and the following narrated actions. I keep before deciding it’s closed as a connected thought rather than stopping after each word.
I consider the meaning: Ari proposes checking the assumption. The narrated action also preserves Mara’s responsibility for her model.

YOU TRY:
Read “There’s a table for models at the back,” the volunteer said. “Take your time.”
Identify the spoken words and narration before rehearsing.

CHECK:
The volunteer’s two spoken lines are separated by the narration the volunteer said. Read the full passage so a listener can follow who is speaking.

**Notice:** Meaningful grouping matters more than making every comma the same length of pause. Keep practice marks out of the spoken text.

**Question:** What should you do before choosing a delivery style?

1. Skip the narration
2. Guess the ending from one word
3. Copy a dramatic voice regardless of meaning
4. Check the passage’s meaning, speaker and unfamiliar words

**Reviewer answer:** Check the passage’s meaning, speaker and unfamiliar words

**Why:** Meaning and speaker information guide purposeful delivery. Skipping or guessing can distort the passage.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the speaker and narration in the extract.
2. Clarify a word and identify the connected thought before marking a pause.
3. Say what the passage means, then group words so that relationship remains clear.

## Teaching 2: Choose delivery and rehearse a change

**Delivery should serve a listener’s understanding.**
Pace is speed, volume is loudness and pitch is how high or low the voice sounds. Emphasis gives a selected word extra prominence. A pause can separate thoughts or give an important relationship time to register.

**Make a supported choice.**
Ari’s proposal to check can use a steady pace so its reasoning is clear. Mara’s repeated handle-pulling may suggest frustration, but you should explain the chosen reading from context rather than treating every line as a shout.

**Keep accuracy.**
Use a comfortable voice, say the written words clearly and do not replace them to fit a performance. There is no required accent or acting style.

**Rehearse twice with a purpose.**
First check word accuracy and grouping. Then choose one delivery change: slow a rushed reason, give a key word prominence or adjust an unhelpful pause. Compare the effect in your second attempt.

Explain your choice: “I changed ____ so the listener could ____.” An improvement should have a reason, not only sound different.

### Show me an example

I DO:
First try: I rush Ari’s words before deciding it’s closed, so the important order is unclear.
Change: I give check a little emphasis and read the reason at a steady pace.
Second try: the listener can follow that checking comes before the conclusion.

COMMON MISTAKE:
Increasing volume on every word does not fix a rushed connection. I focus on the particular meaning I want heard.

YOU TRY:
Read “Take your time” twice. Choose a comfortable pace that matches the volunteer’s reassurance in context.

CHECK:
You might use a calm, steady delivery. Explain how your choice fits the line rather than claiming everyone must perform it identically.

**Notice:** Select one change you can explain. Different natural voices can communicate the same supported meaning.

**Question:** What makes a rehearsal change purposeful?

1. It is linked to how a listener follows the meaning
2. It is louder on every word
3. It adds events not written
4. It guarantees one required accent

**Reviewer answer:** It is linked to how a listener follows the meaning

**Why:** A purposeful change has an explainable effect on clarity or interpretation. Loudness alone, invented content or an accent requirement do not establish that.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a rehearsal change with an explicit purpose.
2. Choose one rushed or unclear phrase rather than changing every tool at once.
3. Try the phrase twice and compare how a listener could follow the important reasoning.

## Teaching 3: Discuss a claim with evidence

**Discussion develops ideas, not only opinions.**
A claim is an interpretation you offer about the story. Support it with a precise action or short quotation, then explain how the evidence relates to the claim.

**Understand a different view first.**
Paraphrase means expressing someone’s idea accurately in your own words. Before agreeing or disagreeing, state what the other interpretation actually says. Do not make it more extreme to dismiss it.

**Ask a clarification question.**
“Which detail supports that view?” or “Do you mean her initial response or her final action?” can help locate the difference. A question is more useful when it addresses reasoning rather than insulting a person.

**Respond with a link.**
You can build on a view, qualify it or offer a different interpretation. Use evidence for your response. Respect does not require agreement; it requires dealing fairly with the stated idea.

If working alone, use the supplied practice views as a discussion rehearsal. This prepares responses but is different evidence from listening to an actual partner.

### Show me an example

PRACTICE VIEW A:
“Mara’s comment about the sign sounds dismissive because she says it could mean anywhere before checking.”
PRACTICE VIEW B:
“Her concern is also reasonable because she misses the small arrow, and Ari later agrees the sign could be clearer.”

I DO:
Accurate paraphrase: View A focuses on the quick initial dismissal.
Clarification: “Are you describing only her first response, or her whole behaviour?”
Response: “I agree about the first response, but the final permission-seeking action suggests she later tries to improve the problem.”

YOU TRY:
Respond to View B using a specific detail. Try before reading on.

CHECK:
A useful response may agree, disagree or qualify, provided it represents View B accurately and explains relevant evidence. “That view is stupid” does not test its reasoning.

**Notice:** Evaluate the interpretation, not the person. A disagreement should state a reason and refer to the story.

**Question:** Which response best asks for useful clarification?

1. You must be wrong because I disagree
2. Which detail supports your interpretation of her response?
3. Nobody can have another view
4. Your idea proves something about your whole personality

**Reviewer answer:** Which detail supports your interpretation of her response?

**Why:** The question asks about the evidence for an interpretation. The other responses dismiss or personalise without examining reasoning.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at accurate representation before evaluation.
2. State the other view’s scope and ask what evidence supports it.
3. Use “I understand this view as ____. The detail ____ leads me to ____.” Supply your own reason.

## Teaching 4: Reflect, revise and keep evidence honest

**Another view can sharpen your interpretation.**
You do not have to abandon a claim whenever someone disagrees. Consider the new evidence and decide whether to keep, qualify or change the idea. Explain the reason.

**Keep the scope accurate.**
“Mara is always dismissive” goes beyond the story. “Her first response sounds dismissive, but her later action is constructive” recognises contrasting evidence without pretending the incident proves her whole personality.

**Use a short reflection chain.**
Initial idea → evidence or feedback considered → revised or retained idea with a reason. This shows your thinking, not just a statement that you improved.

**Separate evidence types.**
A written delivery plan shows what you intended. An observed or recorded reading can show what you actually did. A solo response to practice views shows discussion preparation, not proof that you listened to a real person.

Complete this lesson independently when needed. Use your usual communication support and describe it honestly so a reviewer knows which skills the evidence demonstrates.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Initial claim: “Mara rejects advice.”
Evidence considered: she later says Ari was right about checking and asks to improve the sign.
Revised claim: “Mara initially resists the suggestion, then recognises its value and responds constructively.”
Reason: the revised wording accounts for both early dialogue and later actions.

For delivery, I record the planned pace change and what I noticed in rehearsal. If no one heard it, I do not label the written plan as an observed performance.

YOU TRY:
Qualify a claim that describes Mara’s entire personality using only one early comment.

CHECK:
Narrow it to the early response and consider the ending. A retained interpretation is fine when you explain why the evidence still supports it.

**Teaching diagram:** A reflection moves from an initial interpretation through considered evidence to keeping, qualifying or changing it with a reason.

*Do not confuse agreement with reflection.*

**Notice:** Reflection can explain a retained view as well as a changed one. State the evidence and its effect on your reasoning.

**Question:** Which reflection shows consideration of contrasting evidence?

1. I was right, so no evidence mattered
2. My plan proves an observed performance even if nobody heard it
3. Another view means I must copy it without thinking
4. I narrowed my claim after considering her later constructive action

**Reviewer answer:** I narrowed my claim after considering her later constructive action

**Why:** The reflection identifies evidence and explains a proportionate change. The other choices ignore reasoning or misdescribe evidence.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the initial-idea and considered-evidence chain.
2. Find a detail that supports or complicates the first claim.
3. Explain whether you keep, narrow or change the view and why; describe the evidence you actually produced.

## Put it all together

I DO: Use the full original story for preparation.
THE SIDE ENTRANCE

Mara reached the community hall twenty minutes before the student exhibition opened. She had spent the week building a cardboard model and wanted it on a table before anyone arrived. Through the front glass she could see chairs, a folded display board and an empty foyer. The door would not move.

“They said we could bring our work early,” she told Ari. She pulled the handle again. In the glass, her model’s crooked roof looked more noticeable than it had at home.

Ari set down his folder and checked the message on his phone. “It says side entrance for exhibitors.”

Mara looked along the wall. A small paper arrow pointed towards the corner, but she had walked straight past it. “That could mean anywhere,” she said.

“We can check before deciding it’s closed.” Ari picked up his folder. He did not touch Mara’s model.

At the side door, a volunteer was holding the frame open while another student carried a painting through. Warm light fell across the concrete. Mara stopped adjusting the roof and stepped forward.

“There’s a table for models at the back,” the volunteer said. “Take your time.”

Inside, Mara found a space and placed the model beside a bridge made from paddle-pop sticks. Nobody commented on her roof. Ari put his folder on the neighbouring table.

Mara took the paper arrow from the outside wall, showed it to the volunteer and asked whether they could add a second arrow near the front handle. With permission, she placed the new sign where the next student would see it before pulling.

When she came back, Ari was straightening his display label. “You were right about checking,” she said. He looked up. “And you were right that the sign could be clearer.”

MODEL DELIVERY NOTE
For Ari’s proposal to check before deciding, I will keep the reason connected and give check a little prominence. This helps a listener follow his measured response. I will compare word clarity in two rehearsals instead of making every line louder.

MODEL DISCUSSION RESPONSE
View A identifies a dismissive early comment. I agree that “That could mean anywhere” resists Ari’s suggestion. However, the ending complicates a claim that Mara simply rejects advice: she acknowledges his point and asks permission to improve the sign. I would qualify the claim to describe an initial response followed by a constructive change.

WHY IT WORKS
The response accurately represents the other view, supplies evidence and explains a qualification. It does not need to declare one reader the winner.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“View A says Mara is a terrible person” exaggerates the stated interpretation. Fix: paraphrase its actual focus on her early response before evaluating it.

**Teaching diagram:** Discussion preparation paraphrases a view, asks about evidence or scope, responds with detail and reflects on the reasoning.

*Fair representation comes before disagreement.*

### Help with this step

1. Read the whole story and model response before judging the alternative.
2. Locate the early comment and later actions the model compares.
3. Explain how the later evidence affects the scope of the initial interpretation.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Reading and discussion preparation (about 25–30 minutes).
Use The Side Entrance, included above. This task can be completed independently.
1. Choose an excerpt of about 60–90 words. Identify speakers and explain its meaning in two short notes.
2. Plan a pace, pause or emphasis choice with a reason. Rehearse twice and note one improvement. A listener or recording is optional; use your usual communication method if speaking is inaccessible.
3. State your interpretation of Mara’s response in one or two sentences, with a supporting detail.
4. Respond in about 120–180 words to View A or View B from the lesson. Paraphrase fairly, ask one useful clarification question, and build, qualify or disagree with evidence.
5. Reflect on whether the other view changed or sharpened your claim.
Success checklist: accurate reading preparation; purposeful delivery choice; fair representation; precise evidence; explained response; honest evidence description.

## Quest check

10 multiple-choice questions. The unchanged 90% rule requires 9/10. Written reasoning is assessed in the main task and reflection.

**Question:** What should guide reading-aloud preparation?

1. A dramatic voice chosen before reading
2. Ignoring the speaker
3. The passage’s meaning, speaker and relevant words
4. Inventing a different event

**Reviewer answer:** The passage’s meaning, speaker and relevant words

**Why:** Understanding the meaning and speaker helps delivery communicate the actual text.

**Question:** What does paraphrase mean here?

1. Express another idea accurately in your own words
2. Make it more extreme before rejecting it
3. Copy an unrelated title
4. Replace the story’s ending

**Reviewer answer:** Express another idea accurately in your own words

**Why:** A paraphrase preserves the idea’s meaning while changing wording. It should not distort the view.

**Question:** Which is a purposeful pace decision?

1. Read every event faster regardless of meaning
2. Treat every word as a separate paragraph
3. Change the dialogue’s words to suit your voice
4. Slow a rushed reason so a listener can follow it

**Reviewer answer:** Slow a rushed reason so a listener can follow it

**Why:** The change has a specific clarity purpose and preserves the text.

**Question:** Why keep “before deciding it’s closed” as a connected thought?

1. It is always the longest phrase
2. It helps the listener follow the relationship between checking and deciding
3. It removes all punctuation rules
4. It proves the hall never opens

**Reviewer answer:** It helps the listener follow the relationship between checking and deciding

**Why:** The connected phrasing makes the order of Ari’s reasoning easier to follow.

**Question:** What should a fair response to another view do first?

1. Insult its author
2. Claim only one view can exist
3. Represent what the view actually says
4. Invent evidence for it

**Reviewer answer:** Represent what the view actually says

**Why:** Accurate representation is necessary before agreement, qualification or disagreement.

**Question:** Which question usefully clarifies an interpretation?

1. Do you mean the initial response or the whole behaviour?
2. Why are you always wrong?
3. Can we ignore all details?
4. Does disagreement prove a bad personality?

**Reviewer answer:** Do you mean the initial response or the whole behaviour?

**Why:** The question checks the scope of the claim without attacking the person.

2. Dismiss every alternative immediately
3. Refuse to read the view
4. Explain a different interpretation using relevant evidence

**Reviewer answer:** Explain a different interpretation using relevant evidence

**Why:** A reasoned disagreement examines the interpretation through evidence rather than personal attack.

**Question:** Which ending detail complicates “Mara rejects all advice”?

1. The story contains concrete
2. She acknowledges Ari’s point and asks to improve the sign
3. The model is made of cardboard
4. The hall has a foyer

**Why:** Her acknowledgement and constructive request provide contrasting evidence about her later response.

**Question:** What does a written delivery plan demonstrate by itself?

4. Every person’s reaction

**Reviewer answer:** Intended preparation and choices

**Why:** A plan records intentions. Actual delivery or interaction needs separate observed or recorded evidence.

**Question:** What can a useful reflection do?

1. Explain why a view was kept, qualified or changed after considering evidence

3. Copy another view without a reason
4. Guarantee the first reading was perfect

**Reviewer answer:** Explain why a view was kept, qualified or changed after considering evidence

**Why:** Reflection explains the effect of considered evidence, whether the final view changes or is retained.
