## Accept the quest

Investigate how a sentence works before changing it. Identify its nouns and verb group, then choose a purpose and structure that suit a narrative moment.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to identify core word functions and compare sentence purposes and structures in narrative writing.

- I can distinguish a common noun from a proper noun in context.
- I can identify an action or state and its verb group.
- I can explain the purpose of a statement, question, command or exclamation.
- I can compare simple, compound and complex structures in the examples.
- I can explain a purposeful sentence revision.

**Materials:** All texts and examples are included in this lesson; Optional: paper and a pen for notes

## Teaching 1: Identify nouns in context

**A noun names a person, place, thing or idea.**
Common nouns name a class, such as student, hall or model. Proper nouns identify particular names, such as Mara. Proper names normally begin with capitals. An abstract noun names an idea or quality, such as uncertainty.

These labels describe different features: uncertainty is an abstract common noun, while Mara is a proper noun. Do not assume each word can belong to only one category.

**Find the noun’s role.**
In “Mara carried the model”, Mara is who the clause is about and model names what she carries. A noun can appear inside a larger group: “the crooked cardboard model” contains the noun model with describing words around it.

**Use context, not spelling alone.**
Some words can do different jobs. “The light faded” uses light as a noun. “They light the room” uses light as a verb. Decide from what the word is doing in this sentence.

When revising fiction, a specific noun can help a reader picture the scene. Check that added specificity fits the situation rather than inventing a different object.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Sentence: “Mara placed the model in the hall.”
Mara: a proper noun naming a particular character.
Model and hall: common nouns naming a thing and place.

More precise group: “the cardboard model”. The noun remains model; cardboard supplies information about it.

YOU TRY:
Find the noun in “The uncertainty slowed Mara.” Compare your answer before reading on.

CHECK:
Uncertainty names an idea and Mara names the character. Slowed gives the action or effect. A word about an idea can still be a noun.

**Notice:** Do not identify a noun only because it is long or important. Explain what it names and what it does in the sentence.

**Question:** Which word is a proper noun in “Mara entered the hall”?

1. Entered
2. The
3. Hall
4. Mara

**Reviewer answer:** Mara

**Why:** Mara is the particular character’s name. Hall is a common noun; entered is a verb.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at what a word names and how it functions here.
2. Distinguish a particular name from a class or idea.
3. Read the whole sentence before labelling a word that can have more than one job.

## Teaching 2: Find actions, states and verb groups

**Verbs express more than movement.**
A verb can express an action, such as carried, or a state/relationship, such as is in “The model is fragile”. Looking only for visible movement will miss some verbs.

**Check the whole verb group.**
In “Mara had waited”, had helps form the verb group had waited. In “Ari could help”, could and help work together. Helping verbs can add time or possibility information.

**Connect the verb with its subject.**
In our statement examples, identify who or what the clause is about, then the verb group. “The students were waiting” connects a plural subject with were waiting. Use a form that fits the intended subject and time.

**Choose a verb for the intended action.**
“Mara moved to the entrance” can become hurried if haste is intended, or approached if the emphasis is getting closer. The alternatives are not identical. A more specific verb should preserve or deliberately develop your meaning.

Read the sentence after changing a verb. Do not add a dramatic action that contradicts the event you meant to describe.

### Show me an example

I DO:
“Mara had waited beside the door.”
Subject: Mara.
Verb group: had waited. I keep both words when identifying the group.
Place information: beside the door.

COMPARE:
“The model is fragile.” Is is a verb even though nothing moves. Fragile describes the model.

YOU TRY:
Identify the verb group in “Ari could check the message.”

CHECK:
Could check is the group. Could adds possibility; check names the action. Message is the noun naming what may be checked.

**Notice:** A verb group may have more than one word. Include a helping verb where it belongs, and distinguish the group from surrounding description.

**Question:** What is the verb group in “Ari could check the message”?

1. The message
2. Could check
3. Ari only
4. Message check

**Reviewer answer:** Could check

**Why:** Could and check form the verb group. The other words identify a person or object rather than the whole group.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a complete verb group rather than one word.
2. Find the action or state and check whether a helper belongs with it.
3. Name the subject, then the whole group expressing the action or relationship.

## Teaching 3: Choose a sentence’s communicative purpose

**Purpose and structure are different questions.**
Here, sentence purpose means what the sentence does for communication. Our examples use four common types: a statement gives information; a question asks; a command directs; an exclamation expresses a strong response.

**Compare one situation.**
Statement: “The model is ready.”
Question: “Is the model ready?”
Command: “Check the model.”
Exclamation: “What a careful repair that was!”

The command can omit an expressed you because the addressee is understood. Do not label it incomplete merely because the person is not written.

**Read beyond the end mark.**
Punctuation helps, but does not do all the classification. “Check the model!” is still directing someone, with strong emphasis. A statement can also carry an exclamation mark. Look at wording and purpose, not only the final symbol.

**Choose for the narrative moment.**
A character’s question can reveal uncertainty; a command can show a request or direction. Decide what the character means before selecting the form.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Neutral information: “The side entrance is open.”
Uncertainty: “Is the side entrance open?”
Direction: “Try the side entrance.”
Strong response: “What a relief this is!”

These are choices about communication, not a rule that every scene needs all four types.

YOU TRY:
Turn “The label is clear” into a question and a direction to someone who should check it.

CHECK:
“Is the label clear?” asks. “Check the label” directs. A full stop or emphatic exclamation mark can finish the direction according to context.

**Teaching diagram:** Sentence purpose and clause structure answer different questions; punctuation is a clue rather than the whole classification.

*Explain both with the actual wording.*

**Notice:** A purpose label does not tell you how many clauses the sentence contains. Keep purpose and grammatical structure separate.

**Question:** Why is “Check the model!” a command in this example?

1. Every exclamation mark proves a question
2. It contains no verb
3. It directs an understood addressee to act
4. Every short sentence is a statement

**Reviewer answer:** It directs an understood addressee to act

**Why:** The wording directs someone to check. The exclamation mark adds emphasis but does not remove the command’s function.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at communication and clause structure as separate questions.
2. Ask whether the example informs, asks, directs or expresses a strong response.
3. Use wording and context alongside punctuation; do not let one end mark decide everything.

## Teaching 4: Compare simple, compound and complex structures

**A clause contains a verb and usually a subject.**
In our statement examples, “Mara waited” can stand as a complete sentence: it is an independent clause. “Because Mara waited” gives a reason but depends on another clause to complete the intended sentence.

**A simple sentence has one independent clause.**
“Mara waited by the door.” It can still contain detail. “Mara had waited by the door” still has one independent clause, even though its verb group has two words. Two words in a verb group do not prove a compound sentence.

**A compound sentence connects independent clauses.**
“Mara waited, but Ari checked the message.” Mara waited and Ari checked the message can each stand alone. But connects them, and the comma marks the boundary in this model.

**A complex sentence includes a dependent clause.**
“Because the front door would not open, Mara waited.” The because clause depends on the main clause. It explains a reason. Put the opening dependent part with its main idea rather than leaving it stranded.

**Choose according to meaning.**
One clear action may suit a simple sentence; a contrast can suit a compound; a reason can suit a complex. Complexity is not automatically better. Identify the relationship you need.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Simple: “Ari read the message.” One independent clause.
Compound: “Ari read the message, and Mara looked for the arrow.” Two independent clauses joined by and.
Complex: “After Ari read the message, Mara looked for the arrow.” The opening after clause depends on the main clause and organises the timing.

COMMON MISTAKE:
“After Ari read the message.” leaves the dependent part without its main idea in this practice task. I add what follows.

YOU TRY:
Join “The door was locked” and “Mara waited” to explain a reason.

CHECK:
“Because the door was locked, Mara waited” links the reason and main clause. Another accurate arrangement is possible. Do not join unrelated facts with because merely to make a complex structure.

**Notice:** Count clauses and identify their independence, not just the number of verbs or words. Use a sentence type because it serves a relationship.

**Question:** Which example is a compound sentence as taught here?

1. Mara waited, but Ari checked the message
2. Because Mara waited
3. Mara waited by the door
4. The patient student waited

**Reviewer answer:** Mara waited, but Ari checked the message

**Why:** Mara waited and Ari checked the message are independent clauses joined by but. The other choices are a dependent fragment or one independent clause.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at which clause can stand independently.
2. Find each subject and verb group, then check the joining relationship.
3. Test an opening reason or time clause on its own before locating the main idea that completes it.

## Put it all together

I DO: Analyse and revise a short passage.
ORIGINAL
Mara waited. Ari had checked the message. The entrance was on the side.

WORD FUNCTIONS
Mara and Ari are proper nouns. Message and entrance are common nouns. Had checked is a verb group; was expresses a relationship in the final statement.

PURPOSE
The original sentences are statements. A character could instead ask “Is the entrance on the side?” if that uncertainty suits the moment.

STRUCTURE AND REVISION
“After Ari had checked the message, Mara looked towards the side entrance.”
The opening after clause depends on the main clause. The complex structure makes the timing explicit. This is a revised fictional passage; I deliberately change Mara’s action from waiting to looking, so I should explain that change rather than pretend the wording preserves every event unchanged.

MEANING-PRESERVING ALTERNATIVE
“Ari had checked the message, and Mara waited.” This compound structure combines the stated actions without inventing a new movement.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Calling had checked two separate clauses only because it has two words misses the verb group. I find the whole group and its subject before counting clauses.

**Teaching diagram:** Three structural examples: a simple sentence has one independent clause, a compound connects independent clauses, and a complex combines a main clause with a dependent clause.

*Identify the relationship before choosing the form.*

### Help with this step

1. Analyse the original passage before reading its revisions.
2. Compare word functions and purpose first, then the clause relationships.
3. Explain whether a revision keeps events the same or deliberately changes them.

## Try it with help

WE DO: Work with “Ari could help. Mara held the model.”
1. Identify nouns before comparing: Ari and Mara name particular characters; model names a thing.
2. Identify the verb groups: could help and held. Do not split could from help when naming the group.
3. Turn “Mara held the model” into a question. One option is “Did Mara hold the model?” Notice that the helping verb and main form change together.
4. Join the original statements as a compound: “Ari could help, and Mara held the model.” Each part can stand independently.
5. For a new fictional version, use because only if you intend and support a reason, such as “Because Ari could help, Mara let him hold the model.” Explain that this version changes the event instead of presenting it as an unchanged copy.
6. State one purpose and one structure separately. Your explanation should refer to the actual wording.

### Help with this step

1. Start by identifying the named people and thing.
2. Keep the helper with its main verb before joining sentences.
3. Test each original statement independently, then explain the relationship your new connector creates.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Sentence Investigator task (about 25–30 minutes).
Practice passage: “Mara waited by the door. Ari had checked the message. The side entrance was open. Mara carried the model inside.”
1. List two proper nouns and two common nouns, identifying what they name. If a name repeats, it is still the same noun; choose Mara and Ari rather than claiming duplicates are new examples.
2. Identify the verb group in each sentence. Include had with checked.
3. Create a question and a command about the model or entrance. Explain each communicative purpose.
4. Combine two supplied events into a compound sentence. Show the two parts that can stand alone.
5. Write a complex sentence linking a reason or time relationship you deliberately intend. Name the dependent part. Explain if your new fictional version changes any event.
6. Check: nouns identified in context; verb groups complete; purpose separate from structure; relationships sensible; marks checked.
Type the examples and explanations in the big box, or work on paper and type a clear summary of your analyses. Do not increase length merely to make a sentence sound advanced.

**Reflect:** Which sentence choice made a relationship clearer, and why?

## Quest check

10 multiple-choice questions. The unchanged 90% rule requires 9/10. Written reasoning is assessed in the main task and reflection.

**Question:** Which is a common noun in “Mara carried the model”?

1. Mara
2. Carried
3. The
4. Model

**Reviewer answer:** Model

**Why:** Model names a class of thing. Mara is a particular name and carried is the verb.

**Question:** Why is uncertainty a noun in “The uncertainty slowed Mara”?

1. Every long word is a noun
2. It names an idea acting as the subject here
3. It describes the roof’s colour
4. It must be a proper name

**Reviewer answer:** It names an idea acting as the subject here

**Why:** Uncertainty names an abstract idea and functions as a noun in the sentence.

**Question:** Why must light be considered in context?

1. It can never be used in a sentence
2. It is always a proper noun
3. It can function as a noun or verb in different sentences
4. Its spelling decides every function

**Reviewer answer:** It can function as a noun or verb in different sentences

**Why:** The light faded uses light as a noun; they light the room uses it as a verb. Function depends on the sentence.

**Question:** Which is the whole verb group in “Mara had waited”?

1. Had waited
2. Mara only
3. Waited Mara
4. Had Mara

**Reviewer answer:** Had waited

**Why:** Had and waited form the verb group. Mara is the subject.

**Question:** Why is is a verb in “The model is fragile”?

1. Fragile must move
2. Every descriptive word is a verb
3. The sentence has no verb
4. It expresses a state or relationship rather than visible movement

**Reviewer answer:** It expresses a state or relationship rather than visible movement

**Why:** Verbs can express states or relationships; they need not name a visible action.

**Question:** What does “Is the entrance open?” do in the example?

1. Directs someone to open it
2. Asks a question about the entrance
3. Lists two independent clauses
4. Guarantees everyone can enter

**Reviewer answer:** Asks a question about the entrance

**Why:** The wording asks about the entrance’s state. It does not by itself direct an action or guarantee access.

**Question:** Why can “Check the sign!” remain a command?

1. All punctuation is irrelevant
2. Every short sentence is a question
3. It directs an understood addressee, despite its emphatic mark
4. It contains no action

**Reviewer answer:** It directs an understood addressee, despite its emphatic mark

**Why:** The direction establishes its command function; the exclamation mark adds emphasis.

**Question:** Which is simple in the examples taught?

1. Mara waited by the door
2. Mara waited, but Ari left
3. Because Mara waited, Ari checked
4. After Ari checked, Mara entered

**Reviewer answer:** Mara waited by the door

**Why:** Mara waited by the door has one independent clause. The other examples combine clauses.

**Question:** Which example connects two independent clauses?

1. Because the door was locked
2. The waiting student
3. Mara waited by the door
4. Mara waited, but Ari checked the message

**Reviewer answer:** Mara waited, but Ari checked the message

**Why:** Both sides can stand alone and but connects them, making the taught compound example.

**Question:** What makes “Because the door was locked, Mara waited” complex?

1. It has more letters than every other sentence
2. It combines a dependent reason clause with a main clause
3. It is an exclamation
4. Every word is a noun

**Reviewer answer:** It combines a dependent reason clause with a main clause

**Why:** The because clause is dependent and supplies a reason for the main clause. Complexity is a clause relationship, not mere length.

### Help with this step

1. Return to the word-function, purpose or structure explanation matching the question.
2. Separate what a word does from how clauses connect.
3. For a structure choice, test clause independence; for a purpose choice, examine the wording and intended communication.
