# The invisible distances between enormous numbers

**Year 5 · Mathematics · Week 2 Lesson 3 · Problem solving · 60 minutes.** Preserve attached outcomes. No reflections.

## A number line is a map of distance

A number line is not simply a row of numbers. Its positions show **how far apart** values are. The space between 10 and 20 must be the same as the space between 20 and 30 if the scale is uniform. The same rule applies to large numbers: a line from 2,000,000 to 3,000,000 can represent a million-unit journey.

Imagine a number line with 2,000,000 at the left end and 3,000,000 at the right end. The midpoint is **2,500,000** because it is 500,000 away from either end. A point at 2,750,000 lies three-quarters of the way across: it is 750,000 above the left end and 250,000 below the right.

If you place 2,750,000 exactly in the middle, the drawing would be misleading even if the number label were correct. A good number line respects the scale.

## Find the interval before the missing number

Suppose five equally spaced marks read:

**4,000,000 — ? — ? — ? — 5,000,000**

There are **four equal intervals**, not five. The total difference is 1,000,000. Divide by four to get **250,000 per interval**. The missing labels are **4,250,000; 4,500,000; 4,750,000**.

A common error is dividing by the number of marks rather than the number of gaps. Count the spaces between the ticks. Five marks create four gaps.

## Position a number between labelled ticks

A number line has 6,000,000 and 6,500,000 at its ends. Where does **6,125,000** belong? The full distance is 500,000. The target is 125,000 above the left end. Because 125,000 is one quarter of 500,000, place the point **one quarter of the way across**.

Now locate **6,375,000**. It is 375,000 above the left end, or three quarters of the distance. This approach is more reliable than placing numbers by eye alone.

## Patterns can grow or shrink

Look at **120,000; 145,000; 170,000; 195,000**. Each term increases by **25,000**. To find the next term, add the same amount: **220,000**. The rule is not “add 25” because the place value matters.

Now examine **9,000,000; 8,750,000; 8,500,000; 8,250,000**. Each step subtracts **250,000**, so the next term is **8,000,000**. A pattern can decrease as well as increase.

Not every pattern uses addition. Consider **125,000; 250,000; 500,000; 1,000,000**. Each term is **double** the previous term. The next term is **2,000,000**. Always compare consecutive terms to determine whether the relationship is additive or multiplicative.

## Guided examples

**1.** A line from 1,000,000 to 1,800,000 has four equal intervals. How large is each? Difference 800,000 ÷ 4 = **200,000**. Labels: 1,000,000; 1,200,000; 1,400,000; 1,600,000; 1,800,000.

**2.** Which is nearer 5,000,000: 4,850,000 or 5,300,000? Distances are 150,000 and 300,000, so **4,850,000** is nearer.

**3.** Continue 3,050,000; 3,150,000; 3,250,000. **3,350,000; 3,450,000**, adding 100,000.

**4.** Continue 6,400,000; 6,200,000; 6,000,000. **5,800,000; 5,600,000**, subtracting 200,000.

**5.** Explain why 2,400,000 should not be placed halfway between 2,000,000 and 3,000,000. **The midpoint is 2,500,000; 2,400,000 is only 40% of the way across.**

## Independent problems

**A.** Six equally spaced marks run from 3,000,000 to 4,000,000. Find the step. **200,000**, because five intervals share 1,000,000.

**B.** Fill the marks: 7,000,000; ___; ___; 7,750,000, equally spaced. **7,250,000; 7,500,000.**

**C.** On a line from 4,000,000 to 4,800,000, where is 4,600,000? **Three quarters of the way from the left.**

**D.** Continue 980,000; 1,030,000; 1,080,000. **1,130,000; 1,180,000.**

**E.** Continue 4,000,000; 2,000,000; 1,000,000. **500,000; 250,000**, halving each time.

**F.** A student labels equally spaced ticks 2,000,000; 2,100,000; 2,300,000; 2,400,000. Identify the error. **The differences are 100,000, 200,000, 100,000, so the labels cannot belong to equally spaced ticks.**

**G.** Write one number between 6,750,000 and 6,800,000 that is a multiple of 10,000. **Examples:** 6,760,000; 6,770,000; 6,780,000; 6,790,000.

## Application: a fictional train route

An imaginary train route is **800 kilometres** long. The planning map uses a number line marked at 0, 200, 400, 600 and 800 kilometres. A station is at kilometre **350**. It belongs between 200 and 400, **three quarters of the way from 200 toward 400**, because 350 is 150 of the 200 kilometres across.

Now imagine a budget number line from **$2,000,000 to $2,800,000**, with the same five-tick layout. Each interval represents **$200,000**, not 200 kilometres. The mathematical structure is identical even though the units change.

**Question:** Where would a budget of $2,350,000 sit? **Between $2,200,000 and $2,400,000, three quarters of the way across that interval.** Explain by calculating 150,000 ÷ 200,000 = 3/4.
