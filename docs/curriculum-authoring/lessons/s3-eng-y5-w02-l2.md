# Write a character who feels real

**Year 5 · English · Week 2 Lesson 2 · Writing · 60 minutes.** Retain existing lesson metadata. No reflections.

## Don't tell me everything: let me discover it

Writers sometimes introduce a character by listing qualities: “Amara was clever, impatient, friendly and brave.” The reader receives information, but there is no scene to remember. A more engaging approach lets a character's choices reveal those qualities.

Compare: “Amara was impatient” with “Amara tapped her pencil against the desk while the printer crept through its final page. ‘Come on,’ she whispered.” The second version shows impatience through action and dialogue. We can picture it.

This technique is often called **showing rather than telling**. Telling is not always wrong; stories need efficient summaries too. The skill is knowing when a significant moment deserves a scene.

## Build a character around a want and an obstacle

A character becomes interesting when they **want** something and something makes it difficult. The want might be simple: returning a lost object, winning a competition or being included in a group. The obstacle might come from the environment, another person or the character's own hesitation.

Let's invent **Tess**, who wants to return a library book before closing time. Her obstacle is a sudden downpour and a younger sibling who refuses to hurry. Tess values keeping promises, but she is also easily frustrated. These traits can pull in different directions, which gives us something to write about.

A character who is “good at everything” and never struggles offers few opportunities for meaningful decisions. A believable character can have strengths and limitations at the same time.

## Turn a description into a scene

Start with a telling sentence: “Tess cared about her brother, but she was impatient.”

Now show both qualities:

> Tess checked the library clock through the window. Five minutes. Her brother was still crouched beside a puddle, trying to rescue a beetle from the water.  
> “We have to go,” she said, sharper than she meant to.  
> He looked up, holding out one dripping hand.  
> Tess glanced at the library doors. Then she knelt, pulled a leaf from the gutter and helped him lift the beetle onto the grass.  
> “Now,” she said, “we run.”

We learn that Tess is impatient from the clock-checking and sharp voice. We also see care when she helps her brother despite the deadline. The writer has not labelled either trait inside the scene; the actions carry the meaning.

## Dialogue needs a purpose

Dialogue can reveal how characters respond to one another. It should not be included simply to fill space. “Hello,” “Hello,” “How are you?” may be realistic, but it may not advance a short scene. A useful line can show a desire, a conflict or a change in attitude.

Punctuation helps the reader know who is speaking. In Australian English, either consistent single or double quotation marks may be used; for this lesson, use **double quotation marks**.

> “We have to go,” Tess said.

The comma belongs inside the quotation marks because the speech continues into a reporting clause. When the spoken sentence ends without a reporting clause, use its closing punctuation inside the quotation marks:

> “Now we run.”

Begin a new paragraph when the speaker changes. This helps readers follow a conversation without constant “she said” labels.

## Guided revision

**Example A:** “Ben was nervous.” **Show it:** “Ben read the first line of his speech three times, then folded the corner of his cue card until it softened.” The action suggests nerves without stating them.

**Example B:** “Leah was generous.” **Show it:** “Leah slid half her lunch toward the student who had forgotten theirs.” This is observable behaviour from which readers may infer generosity.

**Example C:** “Ravi was determined.” **Show it:** “The kite fell for the fourth time. Ravi straightened its bent frame and tried a different knot.” The repeated attempt shows persistence.

**Example D:** “Jules was angry.” **Show it carefully:** “Jules pressed both palms against the table and spoke through clenched teeth.” This gives clues but does not require every angry character to act in the same way.

## Plan a short character scene

Choose one of these fictional starting situations: **a student finds a lost key; a sibling breaks a model; a child must speak up when a friend is excluded; a young inventor's machine fails during a demonstration.** Your character needs one clear goal, one obstacle, one strength and one limitation. These elements should influence the events rather than appear only in a planning list.

**Worked plan:** Character: Noor. Goal: return a lost key. Strength: careful observation. Limitation: hesitates to approach strangers. Obstacle: the key's owner is leaving on a bus. Decision: Noor calls out despite feeling nervous. This creates a scene where the character's action demonstrates growth without making Noor suddenly fearless.

## Draft the scene

Write **180–250 words**. Open close to the moment when the problem begins. Use at least **two specific actions** that reveal personality and **two lines of purposeful dialogue**. Show the character making a choice that matters. You may tell the reader some background, but avoid replacing the entire scene with a list of traits.

**Model opening:**

> Noor spotted the brass key beneath the bus-stop bench just as the driver started the engine. A woman at the front of the queue patted her pockets and looked sharply toward the footpath.  
> Noor picked up the key. She knew she should call out. Her voice, however, seemed to have vanished.  
> “Excuse me,” she tried. The bus doors hissed.  
> She took a breath. “Did someone lose a key?”

The key details do two jobs: they move the action forward and reveal Noor's hesitation and effort.

## Improve a first draft

Read this fictional draft: “Noor was shy. She found a key. She gave it back. She was proud.” It contains a beginning, middle and end, but almost no scene. Revise by adding **what Noor notices**, **what makes the task difficult**, **what she says** and **what choice she makes**. Avoid adding random adjectives. Details should serve the character and the problem.

A strong revision might show Noor gripping the key, hearing the bus engine and choosing to speak more loudly. These details reveal nervousness and determination.

## Independent editing tasks

**1.** Replace “Owen was disappointed” with a sentence showing the feeling. **Model:** “Owen stared at the empty noticeboard, then folded the entry form into a square.”  
**2.** Add punctuation: Mia said I found it. **Answer:** Mia said, “I found it.”  
**3.** Explain why a character needs an obstacle. **Model:** It creates a decision that can reveal qualities through action.  
**4.** Identify the stronger sentence: “Ava was brave” or “Ava stepped forward even though her hands were shaking.” **The second**, because action provides evidence of courage despite fear.

## Finished writing rubric — 10 points

**Clear character goal and obstacle (2); personality revealed through actions (2); purposeful dialogue (2); coherent sequence and decision (2); sentence clarity and dialogue punctuation (2).**

**Targeted feedback:** If the draft only tells traits, identify one sentence to convert into a scene. If dialogue has no effect on the events, ask what each line reveals or changes. If the obstacle disappears without a choice, strengthen the decision point.
