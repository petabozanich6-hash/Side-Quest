# Country is more than a place on a map

**Year 5 · HSIE · Week 1 Lesson 1 · Investigate · 60 minutes.** Preserve existing outcome metadata. No reflections.

## Begin with a question

When someone says “country”, you might picture Australia as a nation, or the countryside beyond a city. In many Aboriginal Australian contexts, **Country** has a deeper meaning. It can include land, waters, skies, plants, animals, ancestors, stories, relationships, responsibilities and belonging. Country is not simply a piece of property on a map.

Aboriginal peoples are not one single cultural group. Across Australia there are many distinct Aboriginal nations, languages and cultural traditions. Torres Strait Islander peoples also have distinct identities and relationships with their islands and sea Country. The word **Country** is widely used, but meanings, protocols and ways of speaking about it can differ between communities. We should not pretend one short explanation speaks for everyone.

Think about a river. On an ordinary map, it may appear as a blue line. For people with long cultural and family relationships to that place, the river may also connect knowledge of seasonal changes, food sources, travel, stories, ceremony and responsibilities for caring for it. Those relationships cannot be understood from the blue line alone.

## How people learn from Country

Knowledge of Country can be shared through Elders, family, community, language, stories, observation and practice. Some knowledge is public; some is culturally restricted and should not be copied or shared without permission. Learning respectfully means using reliable, appropriately shared sources and not inventing a Dreaming story or pretending to speak on behalf of a community.

Long-term observation of local environments can produce detailed knowledge. For example, changes in flowering, animal movement, rainfall and water levels may indicate seasonal patterns. Different Aboriginal communities have developed their own ways of recognising seasons, which may not match a simple four-season European calendar.

This is not a claim that every community uses the same calendar. The correct question is: **Which community's knowledge is being described, and where does it apply?**

## Understanding custodianship and responsibility

The idea of **caring for Country** involves responsibilities to places and living systems. In different contexts this may include managing access, protecting culturally important sites, maintaining healthy habitats, passing on knowledge and participating in land and water management. Contemporary Aboriginal people continue these practices and relationships in urban, regional and remote places. Living in a city does not make someone's connection to Country disappear.

A common misconception is that First Nations cultures belong only to the past. They do not. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples are living communities whose cultures, languages, knowledge and relationships with Country continue today.

## Read a source critically

Consider these two fictional statements:

**Source A:** “The creek was used long ago. Today it is just a waterway.”

**Source B:** “The creek is a living part of the local environment. People may have continuing cultural relationships and responsibilities connected with it.”

Source A makes a sweeping claim without evidence and assumes that cultural connections ended in the past. Source B is more careful: it recognises that continuing connections **may** exist, without inventing the specific traditions of a community.

Neither statement tells us which Traditional Owners are connected to this particular creek. To find that out, we would need a trustworthy local source, such as information published by the relevant Aboriginal organisation or Local Aboriginal Land Council. We must not guess a nation's name from a map or invent a local protocol.

## Investigate a fictional landscape

Imagine a valley with a river, woodland, wetlands and a rocky ridge. This is a fictional case study; it is **not** presented as a real Aboriginal cultural landscape.

The river changes depth across the year. The wetlands support waterbirds and frogs. The woodland contains flowering plants, and the ridge provides a broad view of the valley.

**Question 1:** What could someone learn by observing this place across several seasons? **Model:** When water levels rise or fall, which plants flower at different times, and where animals move.

**Question 2:** Why is it incomplete to describe the river only as a source of drinking water? **Model:** Rivers may also have ecological, historical, social and cultural importance, and may be connected to responsibilities and relationships.

**Question 3:** What would you need before making claims about a specific community's stories or sacred sites? **Model:** Reliable community-authorised information and attention to whether knowledge is appropriate to share.

## Comparing maps and lived knowledge

A physical map can show the river's course, the ridge's height and the wetland's location. It is useful evidence, but it does not show every relationship people have with those places. Oral histories, community publications and firsthand accounts can provide different kinds of information. Sources should be identified and interpreted in context rather than treated as interchangeable.

**Practice:** A map labels a wetland but contains no cultural information. Can you conclude that the wetland has no cultural significance? **No.** Absence of information on that map is not evidence of absence of cultural connection.

**Practice:** A tourist brochure says “All Aboriginal people follow the same six seasons.” Is this accurate? **No.** Seasonal knowledge differs between regions and communities. We would need a named, reliable source for any particular calendar.

## Independent source-analysis task

Read this **fictional practice text**, not a quotation from a Traditional Owner:

> “Over many years, people living near a coastal wetland notice that some birds return at particular times and that water levels change after heavy rain. Community members work together to protect nesting areas. Children learn to observe the wetland and respect the places where birds breed.”

**A.** Identify two environmental observations. **Birds return at particular times; water levels change after heavy rain.**

**B.** Identify one responsibility described. **Protecting nesting areas.**

**C.** Explain why this text alone cannot establish the cultural practices of a named Aboriginal community. **It is fictional and does not identify a community-authorised source.**

**D.** Write two respectful questions you could ask when researching a real local place. **Models:** “Which Traditional Owners are connected to this area?” and “What publicly shared resources explain caring for Country here?”

## Final check

**1 (2).** Explain why Country can mean more than physical land. **Model:** It can include waters, living things, stories, ancestors, relationships and responsibilities.

**2 (2).** Why should we avoid saying “all Aboriginal communities do this”? **Model:** Communities are distinct and practices vary.

**3 (2).** Give one example of knowledge developed through long-term environmental observation. **Model:** Recognising seasonal changes through flowering or animal movement.

**4 (2).** Why might a map be insufficient to explain cultural connections? **Model:** It can show locations but not all relationships, knowledge or responsibilities.

**5 (2).** What should you do before using a community's cultural story in a project? **Model:** Check that it is shared by an appropriate, reliable source and that its use is permitted.
