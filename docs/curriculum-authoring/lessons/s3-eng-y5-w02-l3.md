# Word choices that bring a character to life

**Year 5 · English · Week 2 Lesson 3 · Language and spelling · 60 minutes.** Preserve existing metadata. No reflections.

## A tiny change can transform a sentence

Compare “Mara walked into the room” with “Mara crept into the room.” The second sentence contains no extra description of Mara's feelings, yet **crept** suggests she may be trying not to attract attention. Now compare “Mara marched into the room.” The action feels different again: perhaps determined, angry or confident. A carefully chosen **verb** can reveal character without a long explanation.

Words do not always have one fixed emotional meaning. “Marched” may suggest confidence in one context and anger in another. Readers combine the word with the surrounding events. That is why choosing vocabulary involves meaning **and** context.

## Verbs carry action and attitude

A **verb** often describes an action or state. Writers can choose precise verbs instead of using a general verb followed by several adverbs. “She hurried” is usually more direct than “she walked very quickly”. But an unusual verb is not automatically better. “She ambulated rapidly” sounds unnatural in most children's stories. Choose words that fit the narrator's voice and the situation.

Consider this scene: “Jon looked at the broken trophy.” It tells us where his attention goes. “Jon stared at the broken trophy” suggests longer, more intense attention. “Jon glanced at the broken trophy” suggests a brief look. These differences matter when the writer wants readers to infer whether Jon is shocked, worried or trying to hide his reaction.

### Guided choice

A character has heard a noise in an empty house. Which is most effective: **strolled**, **crept** or **skipped** toward the doorway? **Crept** most naturally suggests caution. However, **skipped** could work deliberately if the author wanted to show an unusually fearless or playful character. The best answer depends on the effect the writer intends.

## Adjectives should add information

An **adjective** describes a noun. “A bag” becomes “a battered leather bag”, which suggests age and use. Yet piling up adjectives can slow the story: “the very large, enormous, huge, gigantic bag” repeats essentially the same idea. Precise details work better than a heap of near-synonyms.

Character description is strongest when a detail matters to the scene. A frayed schoolbag strap that snaps during a hurried escape may become part of the action. Eye colour may not matter unless the story gives it a purpose. Writers do not need to describe every visible feature.

## Connotation: what a word suggests

Two words can have similar dictionary meanings but create different impressions. **Confident** often has a positive association; **arrogant** suggests excessive self-importance. **Curious** suggests interest; **nosy** suggests unwanted interference. These associations are called **connotations**.

Imagine two narrators describing the same character: “Leah asked another question because she was curious” and “Leah asked another question because she was nosy.” The action is similar, but the narrator's attitude changes. This can help reveal not only a character but also the narrator's viewpoint.

### Practise meaning in context

**1.** Choose a verb for someone moving carefully across a creaking floor: **tiptoed** is suitable because it suggests quiet, deliberate steps.

**2.** Replace “the very old and extremely ancient book” with a precise description. **Model:** “the cracked, leather-bound book”. It offers observable detail rather than repeated age words.

**3.** Explain the difference between “determined” and “stubborn”. **Model:** Both suggest persistence, but stubborn can imply refusing to reconsider even when there are good reasons.

**4.** “The child demanded an answer” versus “The child requested an answer”: what changes? **Demanded** sounds more forceful; **requested** sounds more polite or neutral.

## Word families and spelling patterns

Writers also need to recognise how words change when we add suffixes. Take **hope**. Add **-ful** to make **hopeful**: the final silent e is dropped before the suffix. Add **-less** to make **hopeless**, again dropping the final e. The word family helps us connect meaning as well as spelling.

Consider **decide → decision**. The spelling changes more noticeably, so it is useful to learn the pair together rather than assume every suffix is added without alteration. **Courage → courageous** keeps the link in meaning but changes the ending. These words are useful for describing characters' choices.

Look at **try → tried**. When a verb ends in a consonant followed by y, we usually change y to i before adding **-ed**. Compare **play → played**, where a vowel comes before y, so the y stays. Spelling patterns are tools, not substitutes for checking unfamiliar words.

### Worked spelling decisions

**A.** Write the past tense of **carry**. **Carried**, because consonant + y changes to i before -ed.

**B.** Write the past tense of **enjoy**. **Enjoyed**, because vowel + y usually keeps the y.

**C.** Complete “The character acted with great ___” using **courage**. **Courage** is the noun; **courageous** would describe the character.

**D.** Which is correct: **hopefull** or **hopeful**? **Hopeful**, with one final l in -ful.

## Apply vocabulary to a scene

Read the plain version:

> Sam went down the hallway. Sam looked at the door. Sam was worried. Sam opened it.

We can improve it without adding unnecessary length:

> Sam edged down the hallway and paused beside the door. A faint scraping sound came from the other side. Sam gripped the handle, waited, then turned it.

The revised version uses **edged**, **paused** and **gripped** to show cautious movement. It does not announce “Sam was worried”, but the actions provide clues. The sound adds a reason for the hesitation.

**Your task:** Rewrite this passage in **60–90 words** to suggest either excitement or suspicion: “Alex found a parcel on the doorstep. Alex picked it up and went inside.” Choose precise verbs, one or two useful adjectives and at least one detail that supports the mood.

**Excitement model:** “Alex spotted the parcel before the front gate had clicked shut. The bright label carried the name of the competition Alex had entered weeks ago. Alex scooped it up, hurried inside and tore the tape away with trembling fingers.” The actions and detail support excitement.

**Suspicion model:** “Alex noticed a plain parcel beside the doorstep. There was no return address. Alex turned it over, checked the street and carried it inside without opening it.” The same event feels different because the details and verbs change.

## Independent tasks

**1.** Replace **went** with a verb that suggests reluctance. **Model:** trudged.  
**2.** Replace **said** with a verb that suggests speaking quietly. **Model:** whispered. Use dialogue context carefully; not every speech tag needs replacing.  
**3.** Explain how **confident** differs from **arrogant**. **Model:** confident suggests belief in ability; arrogant suggests exaggerated superiority.  
**4.** Spell the past tense of **hurry**. **Hurried**.  
**5.** Spell the past tense of **stay**. **Stayed**.  
**6.** Complete: “The explorer was ___ despite the danger” using the adjective form of **courage**. **Courageous**.  
**7.** Correct “The hopefull child carryed the box.” **“The hopeful child carried the box.”**  
**8.** Explain why “the enormous gigantic massive bag” is less effective than a specific description. **The adjectives repeat size rather than adding distinct information.**

## Final assessment

**Question 1 (2).** Explain how “crept” reveals more than “walked”. **It suggests careful, possibly secretive movement.**  
**Question 2 (2).** Explain a difference in connotation between “curious” and “nosy”. **Curious is generally positive or neutral; nosy suggests intrusive interest.**  
**Question 3 (2).** Spell **try** and **play** in the past tense and explain the difference. **Tried / played; consonant + y changes to i, vowel + y retains y.**
