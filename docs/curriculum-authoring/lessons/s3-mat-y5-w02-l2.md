# Is the answer sensible before you calculate?

**Year 5 · Mathematics · Week 2 Lesson 2 · Strategies · Approximately 60 minutes.** Keep existing outcomes and identity. No reflections.

## The shopping-list problem

A school is buying equipment. Three items cost **$298**, **$403** and **$197**. Before adding carefully, you might notice that these are close to **$300**, **$400** and **$200**. That makes an estimated total of **$900**. The exact sum is **$898**, so the estimate is extremely useful.

Estimating does not mean guessing without thinking. It means choosing simpler nearby numbers and calculating with them to judge the likely size of a result. An estimate can help plan a budget, detect a calculator mistake or decide whether there is enough time or space for something.

## Why different estimates can both be useful

Round each price to the nearest hundred and you get $300 + $400 + $200 = **$900**. Round to the nearest ten and you get $300 + $400 + $200 again in this case. But with other values, the choice matters.

Consider **347 + 268**. Rounding to the nearest hundred gives 300 + 300 = **600**. Rounding to the nearest ten gives 350 + 270 = **620**. The exact answer is **615**. Both estimates communicate the approximate size, but 620 is closer. Greater precision can be useful, although it may require more mental effort.

An estimate is not automatically better just because it uses more digits. Its usefulness depends on the decision you are making. If you only need to know whether a total is around six hundred or six thousand, rounding to hundreds is enough.

## Rounding can push an estimate above or below

A common misconception is that rounding always makes numbers larger. It does not. We round some values up and others down. For example, 248 rounds to 200 to the nearest hundred, while 276 rounds to 300. Their estimated sum is 500; the exact sum is 524. The estimate is below the exact value because the combined effects of rounding happen to reduce it.

Look at **449 + 449**. Rounding both to the nearest hundred gives 400 + 400 = **800**, whereas the exact sum is **898**. That is still a rough estimate, but it is not especially close. Rounding to the nearest ten gives 450 + 450 = **900**, much nearer. When the answer needs to be more precise, choose an appropriate place value.

## Estimate before using written methods

Imagine calculating **6,842 − 2,915**. Round to the nearest thousand: 7,000 − 3,000 = **4,000**. The exact difference is **3,927**. If a calculator displays 39,270, the estimate immediately reveals that something has gone wrong. The result should be near four thousand, not forty thousand.

Estimation is especially useful for checking multiplication. **49 × 21** is close to **50 × 20 = 1,000**. The exact product is **1,029**. If someone reports 10,290, their answer is about ten times too large.

For division, **1,198 ÷ 6** is close to **1,200 ÷ 6 = 200**. The exact answer is approximately 199.7. This gives a reasonable expectation before carrying out division.

## Application: a community garden

A fictional community garden needs **387 litres** of water for one bed, **412 litres** for another and **194 litres** for a third. Its tank holds **1,000 litres**. Estimate the total first: 400 + 400 + 200 = **1,000 litres**. That estimate suggests the tank might be just enough, but it cannot prove it.

Now calculate exactly: 387 + 412 = 799; 799 + 194 = **993 litres**. The tank is enough, with **7 litres** remaining. This example shows why estimating is useful for planning but exact calculation is necessary when a decision depends on a small margin.

**Question:** Would it be safe to claim the tank has 100 litres left based on the estimate? **No.** The estimate only shows the total is close to 1,000, and the exact remaining amount is 7 litres.
