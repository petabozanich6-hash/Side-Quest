# s3-mat-y6-w01-l3 — Comparing and ordering large numbers

**Year 6 · Stage 3 · Mathematics · Week 1, Lesson 3 · Unit: Large numbers and estimation · Role: Problem solving**

**Existing lesson title:** Comparing and ordering large numbers. **Attached outcome codes:** Retain exactly as stored in the application; the original migration supplies an empty array. No code has been invented here.

**Lesson goal:** Compare, order and justify relationships between large whole numbers using place value, including close values and numbers containing zeros.

**Student success criteria:** I can use >, < and = correctly; compare from the greatest place value; order three or more large numbers; justify a comparison in words; and diagnose a common ordering mistake.

**Estimated learning time:** 60 minutes, with a break if needed. **Materials:** paper/digital working space; optional place-value chart. **No videos.**

## 1. Opening mystery: which expedition wins? (5 minutes)

Two explorers report the distance travelled by their imaginary research probes:

- **Probe A:** 6,408,250 kilometres
- **Probe B:** 6,480,205 kilometres

**Pip:** “Probe A has a 2 in the hundreds column and Probe B has a 2 in the hundreds column too. I think they tied!”

**Juno:** “Matching one digit is not enough. We must compare the largest places first.”

**Opening response:** Which probe travelled farther? Write one sentence explaining how you decided. Do not calculate a subtraction yet.

**Reveal:** Probe B travelled farther. Both numbers have 6 millions and 4 hundred-thousands. At the **ten-thousands** column, Probe A has 0 and Probe B has 8. Since 80,000 is greater than 0, Probe B is larger; no smaller place can reverse that decision.

**Why this matters:** A number may contain many digits, but comparison is not a hunt for whichever digit looks biggest. Position determines value.

## 2. Teach: compare the greatest place first (10 minutes)

We compare whole numbers by asking, in order:

1. Does one number have more digits? For positive whole numbers without leading zeros, the one with more digits is larger: 12,000,000 > 9,999,999.
2. If they have the same number of digits, compare the **leftmost** digits.
3. If those match, move one place right until you find the **first difference**.
4. The number with the larger digit at that first different place is the larger number.
5. If every digit matches, the numbers are equal.

**Comparison symbols:**

- `>` means **greater than**: 8,000,001 > 7,999,999.
- `<` means **less than**: 4,205,100 < 4,250,100.
- `=` means **equal to**: 5,030,000 = 5,000,000 + 30,000.

**Memory aid:** Read the statement aloud. “4,205,100 is less than 4,250,100.” The open side of < or > faces the larger number, but always check the *words*, not just the shape.

### Worked model 1 — same digit count, different first digit

Compare **8,095,010** and **7,999,999**.

Both have seven digits. Compare millions: 8 > 7. Therefore **8,095,010 > 7,999,999**. Stop: smaller places cannot outweigh the difference in millions.

### Worked model 2 — the first difference is hidden

Compare **3,406,080** and **3,460,008**.

| Place | Number A | Number B | What it tells us |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Millions | 3 | 3 | Tie |
| Hundred-thousands | 4 | 4 | Tie |
| Ten-thousands | 0 | 6 | **First difference: B wins** |
| Thousands | 6 | 0 | Do not restart the comparison here |
| Hundreds | 0 | 0 | Not needed |
| Tens | 8 | 0 | Not needed |
| Ones | 0 | 8 | Not needed |

So **3,406,080 < 3,460,008**.

**Important:** A has more thousands and tens, but B already has 60,000 in the ten-thousands column where A has zero. Those smaller differences cannot overturn the comparison.

### Worked model 3 — equal value, different representation

Compare **4,070,200** and **4,000,000 + 70,000 + 200**.

Rebuild the expanded form: 4,070,200. So **4,070,200 = 4,000,000 + 70,000 + 200**.

Different appearances do not always mean different values.

### A crucial distinction

The number **5,090,000** is greater than **5,009,000** because at ten-thousands the first has 9 and the second has 0. Writing commas helps us read numbers; commas are **not** place-value digits.

## 3. Teach: ordering a collection (8 minutes)

To arrange numbers **ascending**, put them from **smallest to largest**. To arrange them **descending**, put them from **largest to smallest**.

Consider these library visitor totals:

- North: **2,405,600**
- East: **2,450,060**
- West: **2,405,060**
- South: **2,540,006**

**Worked ordering method:**

All begin with 2 million. North, East and West have 4 hundred-thousands; South has 5 hundred-thousands, so **South is largest**.

Among the three beginning 2,4..., East has 5 ten-thousands, while North and West have 0 ten-thousands. **East comes next**.

Compare North and West: they tie through thousands (2,405,...). North has 6 hundreds, West has 0 hundreds. **North > West**.

**Ascending:** West 2,405,060; North 2,405,600; East 2,450,060; South 2,540,006.

**Descending:** South; East; North; West.

**Check:** Reading left to right in the ascending list, each number should be greater than the previous one.

## 4. Guided practice — think, choose, explain (12 minutes)

**G1. Compare:** 9,010,005 ___ 9,001,500. Choose <, > or =.

**Hint 1:** The millions are equal. Look at the hundred-thousands, then ten-thousands. **Hint 2:** 1 ten-thousand is greater than 0 ten-thousands.

**Answer:** **9,010,005 > 9,001,500**. **Feedback for <:** The 1 in the first number means 10,000; the 1 in the second number means 1,000. The same digit has a different value.

**G2. First difference:** Compare 4,672,105 and 4,671,999. At which place do they first differ?

**Answer:** **Thousands**: 2 thousands versus 1 thousand. Thus **4,672,105 > 4,671,999**. **Feedback if hundreds selected:** You went too far right. Stop at the *first* different column.

**G3. Different lengths:** Which is larger, 10,000,000 or 9,999,999? Explain without adding.

**Answer:** **10,000,000**. It has eight digits, while 9,999,999 has seven. For positive whole numbers, more digits means a larger value. **Feedback:** A string of nines does not become larger than the next power of ten.

**G4. Order ascending:** 6,070,500; 6,007,500; 6,700,050.

**Answer:** **6,007,500; 6,070,500; 6,700,050**. **Hint:** All have 6 millions. Compare hundred-thousands, then ten-thousands.

**G5. Equal or unequal?** 3,050,040 ___ 3,000,000 + 50,000 + 40.

**Answer:** **=**. **Feedback for > or <:** Expand the right-hand side into the same place-value columns as the left.

**G6. Explain a mistake:** Pip says 5,809,004 < 5,790,999 because 790,999 looks bigger than 809,004. What is wrong?

**Model response:** Both have 5 millions. At hundred-thousands, 5,809,004 has 8 while 5,790,999 has 7. So **5,809,004 > 5,790,999**. We compare place by place from the left, not by the appearance of the trailing group.

**Feedback for an incomplete explanation:** Identify the exact first different column and what its digits mean.

## 5. Independent practice — prove it (12 minutes)

**I1.** Insert <, > or =: 7,030,040 ___ 7,003,400.  
**Answer:** >. At ten-thousands: 3 > 0.

**I2.** Insert <, > or =: 12,000,009 ___ 11,999,999.  
**Answer:** >. At millions: 2 > 1 after the equal ten-millions digit.

**I3.** Insert <, > or =: 6,400,070 ___ 6,000,000 + 400,000 + 70.  
**Answer:** =.

**I4.** Order ascending: 8,080,008; 8,800,080; 8,008,800; 8,080,080.  
**Answer:** 8,008,800; 8,080,008; 8,080,080; 8,800,080.

**I5.** Order descending: 3,905,010; 3,950,001; 3,590,100; 3,905,100.  
**Answer:** 3,950,001; 3,905,100; 3,905,010; 3,590,100.

**I6.** Which is larger: 24,099,999 or 24,100,000? Name the first different place.  
**Answer:** **24,100,000**. At hundred-thousands: 1 > 0.

**I7.** Find a whole number strictly between 5,060,000 and 5,070,000.  
**Example answer:** 5,065,000. Accept **any integer from 5,060,001 through 5,069,999**. **Feedback:** Both endpoints are excluded because “strictly between” means greater than the first and less than the second.

**I8.** Is this reasoning valid? “4,999,999 is greater than 5,000,000 because it has six nines.” Explain.  
**Answer:** No. 5,000,000 has 5 millions and 4,999,999 has only 4 millions; 5,000,000 is larger.

**I9.** Three numbers all begin with 7,30: 7,304,600; 7,300,640; 7,304,060. Which is the middle value? Explain.  
**Answer:** **7,304,060**. Ascending: 7,300,640; 7,304,060; 7,304,600. The first difference between the two 7,304,... numbers occurs in the hundreds column: 0 < 6.

**I10. Challenge:** Use each of the digits 0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9 exactly once to make the **largest** six-digit number less than 800,000. Explain.  
**Answer:** **795,320**. The largest allowable first digit is 7; arrange remaining digits in descending order. **Common error:** 975,320 exceeds 800,000.

### Feedback and marking

Machine-markable questions: I1–I6 and I9. I7 accepts the numeric interval, not a single fixed answer. I8 and I10 require an explanation rubric (human review or carefully bounded checks).

For an incorrect comparison, respond with the **first different place** and what it represents. For an incorrect ordered list, identify the first adjacent pair out of order and invite a retry. Never respond with “wrong” alone.

## 6. Transfer task — the science archive (7 minutes)

Four imaginary observatories record how many signals they processed:

| Observatory | Signals |
|---|---:|
| Aurora | 14,205,090 |
| Beacon | 14,250,009 |
| Comet | 14,205,900 |
| Delta | 14,025,990 |

**A.** Order the observatories from fewest to most signals.  
**Answer:** **Delta; Aurora; Comet; Beacon**.

**B.** Which two observatories have the closest counts? Show a numerical reason.  
**Answer:** **Aurora and Comet**. Difference: 14,205,900 − 14,205,090 = **810**. Other pairwise gaps are larger. **Note:** Comparing close values can be reasoned by place value, then checked by subtraction.

**C.** A news headline says, “Beacon processed more than 15 million signals.” Is it accurate?  
**Answer:** **No**. Beacon processed 14,250,009, which is less than 15,000,000. **Model explanation:** At millions, 14 million is less than 15 million.

**D.** Write a two-sentence correction to the headline that includes one valid comparison.  
**Example:** “Beacon processed 14,250,009 signals. That was more than Aurora's 14,205,090 signals, but fewer than 15 million.”

**Rubric (8 points):** A 2 points (full ordering); B 2 (correct pair and valid difference); C 2 (judgement and explanation); D 2 (accurate claim and clear comparison).

## 7. Exit ticket — independent evidence (6 minutes)

Complete without hints; explanations matter.

**E1 (1 point):** 6,409,008 ___ 6,490,008.  
**Answer:** <.

**E2 (2 points):** Order ascending: 9,090,090; 9,009,900; 9,900,009.  
**Answer:** 9,009,900; 9,090,090; 9,900,009. One point for the correct smallest value, one for the complete order.

**E3 (2 points):** Explain why 10,000,001 > 9,999,999 without subtracting.  
**Model:** The first number has eight digits and the second seven. For positive whole numbers, the eight-digit number is greater. One point for correct claim; one for a place-value reason.

**E4 (2 points):** 4,070,500 and 4,007,500: identify the first different column and state which is greater.  
**Answer:** Ten-thousands; 4,070,500 is greater. One point for column, one for comparison.

**E5 (2 points):** A learner orders 3,450,020; 3,405,200; 3,540,002 from smallest to largest. Correct the list and explain the mistake.  
**Answer:** 3,405,200; 3,450,020; 3,540,002. The first two were reversed; compare ten-thousands (0 versus 5 after matching millions and hundred-thousands). One point for correct order, one for reason.

**Score guidance (9 points):** 8–9: proceed; 6–7: retry the missed comparison type; 0–5: revisit the place-value comparison table with support. These are teaching decisions, not standardised scores.

**Reteach for E1/E4:** Compare 7,006,500 and 7,060,500, marking the ten-thousands column.  
**Reteach for E2/E5:** Sort 2,080,100; 2,008,100; 2,800,100 and explain each first difference.  
**Reteach for E3:** Compare 9,999,999 and 10,000,000 using digit count.

## 8. Reflection and preview (2 minutes)

Finish: “When comparing large numbers, I look at ______ first because ______.”

**Model:** “I look at the greatest place value first because the first difference there decides which number is larger.”

**Juno:** “You now know how to compare a pair and sort a whole collection. In our next lesson we'll practise these skills until they feel more automatic.”
