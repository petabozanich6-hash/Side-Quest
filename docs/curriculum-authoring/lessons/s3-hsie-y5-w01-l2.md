# A place has more than one story

**Year 5 · HSIE · Week 1 Lesson 2 · Apply and respond · 60 minutes.** Preserve existing outcome metadata. No reflections.

## The problem with a very short sign

Imagine a council installing a sign beside a wetland. Its first draft reads:

> “This wetland contains water, birds and reeds. It is useful for walking.”

The statement is not necessarily false, but it tells only part of the story. It describes some physical features and one recreational use. It says nothing about the wetland's ecological relationships, history or possible continuing cultural significance.

Your task is to create a more informative, respectful public information panel for a **fictional wetland**. The aim is not to invent Aboriginal stories or cultural knowledge. It is to learn how to distinguish what you know from what you would need to research with appropriate sources.

## What makes a trustworthy information panel?

A good public sign makes claims that can be supported. If you can see reeds and waterbirds in an observation record, you can say they are present. If a named organisation has published approved information about the cultural significance of a real place, you may be able to summarise that information accurately with attribution. But if you do not have such a source, you must not write “the local people believe...” and fill in a story from imagination.

This distinction is important because First Nations peoples have distinct identities, histories and knowledge systems. Being respectful is not just using polite words. It includes **accuracy, attribution, permission and avoiding generalisation**.

We can practise these skills using a fictional wetland, without pretending to speak for a real community.

## The fictional Willowbank Wetland

Here is all the information available for our invented site:

> Willowbank Wetland is a shallow freshwater area connected to a creek. Reeds grow around its edges. Waterbirds nest there during part of the year. After heavy rain, the water level rises. A walking path passes one side of the wetland. Litter has sometimes been found near the path. The council wants visitors to understand why the area needs care. The fictional source pack contains **no information identifying Traditional Owners or community-authorised cultural knowledge**.

This source pack is deliberately limited. It gives us ecological observations and a management concern, but no authority to make claims about specific Aboriginal traditions.

## Model: improving the sign step by step

First, describe the physical place accurately: “Willowbank Wetland is a freshwater habitat connected to a creek.” This sentence is supported by the source pack.

Next, explain an ecological relationship: “Reeds provide cover around the wetland's edges, and waterbirds use the area for nesting during part of the year.” The pack confirms reeds and nesting birds; saying reeds provide cover is a reasonable general ecological explanation, though detailed species claims would need more evidence.

Then identify a responsibility: “Please keep litter out of the wetland and stay on the designated path.” This follows from the reported litter and the aim of protecting habitat.

Finally, consider cultural information. We **cannot** name Traditional Owners from the fictional pack. A responsible draft can include an editorial note, not a fabricated claim: “Before publishing information about Aboriginal connections to this place, the council should consult the relevant Traditional Owner organisations and use appropriately authorised information.”

That note belongs in the council's planning document, not necessarily on the final visitor sign. It shows what must happen before the sign is complete.

## Compare strong and weak wording

**Weak:** “All Aboriginal people used wetlands in exactly the same way.” This generalises across distinct communities and lacks evidence.

**Stronger:** “The cultural history and continuing connections of a real wetland should be researched through appropriate local Aboriginal sources.” This does not invent a practice.

**Weak:** “No cultural information appears in our pack, so there is no cultural significance.” This confuses missing evidence with evidence that something does not exist.

**Stronger:** “Our pack does not provide enough information to describe specific cultural connections.” This states the actual limit.

## Guided writing

**1.** Rewrite “The wetland is just a swamp.” **Model:** “The wetland is a freshwater habitat that supports reeds and nesting waterbirds.” The improved sentence names features rather than dismissing the place.

**2.** Rewrite “People should be careful here.” **Model:** “Visitors should take litter home and remain on the walking path to reduce disturbance.” The instruction is specific and tied to the site.

**3.** A classmate writes “The Traditional Owners of Willowbank perform a particular ceremony here every spring.” Can this be included? **No.** Willowbank is fictional and the source pack contains no authorised cultural information. The claim is invented.

**4.** What could the council do before writing cultural content about a real site? **Model:** Identify and contact the appropriate Traditional Owner organisations, consult reliable community-published resources, and follow guidance about what may be shared.

## Create your own information panel

Write **130–180 words** for visitors to fictional Willowbank Wetland. Your panel must include an accurate description of the habitat, one ecological relationship, two specific actions visitors can take, and a suitable title. It must **not** invent cultural information.

Then write a separate **editor's note of 60–90 words** explaining what additional research or consultation would be needed before a real council could responsibly include information about First Nations connections.

### Complete example panel

**Willowbank Wetland: A Place for Wildlife**

Willowbank Wetland is a shallow freshwater habitat connected to a creek. Its water level rises after heavy rain, and reeds grow around the edges. Waterbirds use the wetland for nesting during part of the year, so protecting the area helps provide a safer place for wildlife. Visitors can enjoy the wetland from the walking path without entering sensitive areas. Please stay on the designated path and take all litter home. Plastic and other rubbish can harm animals and damage habitats. The wetland is more than a pleasant view: it is a living environment where water, plants and animals interact. Caring for it helps protect these relationships for the future.

**Example editor's note:** “The available fictional information does not identify Traditional Owners or provide authorised cultural material. For a real site, the council should identify the appropriate local Aboriginal organisations and seek guidance about what information can be shared publicly. The panel should use accurate attribution and avoid presenting one community's knowledge as if it applies everywhere.”

## Evaluate the work

**Assessment rubric (10 points):** Habitat description accurate (2); ecological relationship explained (2); two specific visitor actions (2); cultural claims avoided and limits recognised (2); clear, readable language and title (2).

**Common feedback:** If the panel lists features without explaining relationships, ask how water, plants and birds connect. If the note says only “ask someone”, ask **who** should be consulted and **why**. If cultural content is invented, remove it and replace it with a documented research need.

## Final check

**A (2).** Is a missing cultural reference in a map proof that a place has no cultural importance? **No; the map may not contain that information.**

**B (2).** Why is a named, authorised source preferable to a general statement about “all Aboriginal people”? **It supports accuracy and respects distinct communities.**

**C (2).** Give two site-care actions appropriate to Willowbank. **Stay on the path; remove litter.**

**D (2).** Identify one claim in the fictional source pack that can be stated confidently. **Water levels rise after heavy rain**, or another directly supplied fact.
