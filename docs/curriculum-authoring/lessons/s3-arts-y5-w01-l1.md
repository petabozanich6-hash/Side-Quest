# How artists make flat paper look three-dimensional

**Year 5 · Creative Arts · Week 1 Lesson 1 · Explore and learn · Approximately 60 minutes.** Preserve existing outcome metadata. No reflections.

## The mystery of the round apple

An apple drawn as a simple circle may look like a symbol for an apple rather than an object with weight and shape. Artists can make it appear more solid by carefully observing its **outline**, the **light** falling on it and the **shadows** it creates.

You do not need expensive equipment to explore these ideas. A pencil, paper and an ordinary object such as a cup, spoon or fruit are enough. If you do not have those materials, use the supplied imaginary object descriptions and draw from them.

Today you will learn how to control lines and tone rather than simply “draw neatly”. Drawing is a way of looking closely and making deliberate choices.

## Begin by looking for shapes

A complicated object becomes easier to draw when we notice its basic forms. A mug may have an oval opening, a roughly cylindrical body and a curved handle. These are starting points, not perfect mathematical shapes. Real objects have irregularities, and a drawing can become more convincing when you notice them.

Imagine placing a mug on a table. From directly above, its opening looks close to a circle. From a normal seated viewing angle, the opening appears as an **ellipse**—a flattened oval—because you are seeing the circle at an angle. Drawing a perfect circle at the top of every mug often makes the mug look as though you are looking straight down at it.

Start with light lines. They are easier to adjust when you discover that the handle is wider or the base narrower than you first thought.

## Line can describe more than an edge

An **outline** marks the visible boundary of a shape. A **contour line** follows an edge or change of form. Lines can be thick, thin, light, dark, straight, curved, smooth or broken. Each quality changes how the drawing feels.

Try making three marks with the same pencil: one light line using little pressure, one medium line and one dark line using greater pressure. Do not press so hard that the paper tears. The aim is to learn control, not force.

Now draw a curved line that begins lightly, becomes darker in the middle and fades again. You have changed the **line weight**. Artists may use this to emphasise some edges while allowing others to remain soft.

## Tone makes form visible

**Tone** means how light or dark an area appears. On a rounded object, the side facing the light is often brighter than the side turned away. The change from light to dark helps us see the object as a form rather than a flat outline.

Imagine a ball on a table with a lamp to its upper left. The upper-left surface receives more direct light. The opposite side is generally darker. A **cast shadow** appears on the table where the ball blocks light. The cast shadow is different from the darker area **on the ball itself**.

We can show tone using **shading**. One technique is **hatching**: a series of lines placed close together. Lines spaced widely create a lighter area; lines closer together create a darker area. **Cross-hatching** adds a second set of lines crossing the first. The aim is not to cover everything with identical scribbles. You choose where darker and lighter marks belong.

## Worked drawing: a cylinder

A cylinder is a useful practice shape because many everyday objects resemble one.

First, lightly draw an ellipse for the top. Beneath it, draw two nearly vertical sides. Join their lower ends with a shallow curved line to suggest the base. Keep the first marks light because the proportions may need adjusting.

Next, choose where the light comes from. Let's place it on the **left**. Leave a lighter band on the left side of the cylinder. Add gentle hatching toward the centre, then closer hatching toward the right side. Do not make every part equally dark: the gradual change creates a sense of roundness.

Finally, add a cast shadow on the table extending away from the light, toward the right. A cast shadow anchors the object so it does not appear to float.

**Common mistake:** Drawing a dark outline all the way around and then filling the entire cylinder with one grey tone. This makes it difficult to see where the light is coming from. Instead, vary the tone deliberately.

## Practise three essential skills

**Exercise 1 — line control (8 minutes).** Draw five straight lines from light to dark and five curved lines from light to dark. **Success looks like:** visible differences in pressure and smooth intentional curves. If the lines are all equally dark, slow down and reduce pressure for the first marks.

**Exercise 2 — a five-step tone scale (8 minutes).** Draw five equal boxes. Leave the first white. Shade the fifth as dark as your pencil comfortably allows. Fill the three middle boxes so they become gradually darker. **Success looks like:** five distinguishable values. If two boxes look the same, adjust the density of marks rather than pressing harder everywhere.

**Exercise 3 — cylinder study (10 minutes).** Draw the cylinder described above with a light side, darker side and cast shadow. **Success looks like:** a recognisable ellipse, consistent light direction and at least three visible tones.

## Independent artwork: an everyday object

Choose a mug, fruit or small container. Place it somewhere with a clear light source, or use this fictional still-life description: **a plain round orange sits on a table; light comes from the upper left; the lower-right side is darker and a shadow falls to the right**.

Draw the object in a space at least as large as your hand. Begin with the largest shapes. Add the important contours, then use shading to show form. Aim for a light area, a middle tone and a dark area. Add the cast shadow where appropriate.

Do not judge the drawing by whether it looks photographic. The learning goal is whether your choices of line and tone help another viewer understand the object's form.

## Read and improve a drawing

A fictional student draws a mug with a perfectly circular opening, two straight sides, dark shading on the left and a cast shadow also extending to the left. The student says the light comes from the left.

What could be improved?

**Model answer:** At a normal seated viewing angle, the mug opening would usually look more like an ellipse than a circle. If light comes from the left, the left-facing surface should generally be lighter and the cast shadow should extend away from the light, toward the right. The artist should check the real object because reflections and multiple light sources can change the appearance.

## Final skills check

**1 (2).** What is tone? **The lightness or darkness of an area.**

**2 (2).** How does hatching create a darker area? **By placing lines closer together or layering them, increasing the density of marks.**

**3 (2).** What is a cast shadow? **A shadow on another surface where an object blocks light.**

**4 (2).** Why is an ellipse often used for the opening of a mug? **A circular opening seen at an angle appears oval.**

**5 (2).** In your finished artwork, identify where the lightest and darkest tones are and whether the cast shadow matches the light direction. **Mark from visible evidence in the artwork, not from personal reflection.**

**Assessment:** 2 points each for line control, varied tone, coherent light direction, recognisable form and considered shadow placement. Give specific feedback such as “The right side needs a darker middle tone” rather than “Draw better.”
