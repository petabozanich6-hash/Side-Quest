# Make a character heard: voice, expression and clear handwriting

**Year 5 · English · Week 2 Lesson 4 · Speaking and handwriting · 60 minutes.** Preserve attached outcomes. No reflections.

## Words on a page are not the whole performance

Imagine reading the line “I didn't say you took it.” You could stress **I**, **say**, **you** or **took**, and each version would suggest a different meaning. Spoken language carries information through **pace**, **volume**, **pauses**, **intonation** and **emphasis**. When reading a character's dialogue aloud, those choices can reveal fear, excitement, sarcasm or uncertainty.

However, speaking dramatically is not the same as shouting. An effective reader helps an audience understand the words. If every line is delivered at maximum volume, important differences disappear. If every sentence is rushed, listeners have little time to follow the action.

Today you will practise delivering a short character scene and presenting a neatly handwritten excerpt. These are connected skills: both require attention to how a reader or listener receives a message.

## How punctuation guides a reader's voice

Read these three sentences:

> “You're here.”  
> “You're here?”  
> “You're here!”

The words are identical, but the punctuation suggests different ways to deliver them. A full stop usually suggests a statement. A question mark signals a question, often with an appropriate change in intonation. An exclamation mark can signal strong feeling, but it does not automatically mean the line should be screamed.

A comma can indicate a small pause. A full stop usually creates a more definite boundary between ideas. When you read aloud, punctuation is a guide to meaning, not a rule that every comma requires an identical-length pause.

## Worked performance: the lost map

Read this original mini-scene:

> The map was missing from the table.  
> “It was here a minute ago,” said Ada.  
> Finn crouched beside the chair. “Did it fall?”  
> “No.” Ada looked toward the open window. “I think someone moved it.”  
> Finn stood slowly. “Then we'd better find out who.”

Ada's first line may sound surprised or frustrated. Finn's question is genuinely searching for an explanation. The pause after “No” gives Ada's next thought more weight. Finn's final line might be quiet and determined rather than loud. A performer chooses an interpretation and makes it understandable.

### Try the same line two ways

Say “I think someone moved it” once as an uncertain possibility and once as a confident accusation. For uncertainty, you might speak more slowly and emphasise **think**. For confidence, you might stress **someone** or **moved**. Neither reading is automatically correct; the surrounding story would help decide which fits.

## Audience awareness

Speaking to one friend across a desk is different from speaking to a group across a room. The listener must be able to hear your words, but you should not strain your voice. Face the audience when practical, pronounce word endings clearly and pause at natural sentence boundaries. If you use notes, glance at them rather than burying your face in the page.

A speaker may need accommodations such as seated delivery, text-to-speech or an alternative recorded format. The learning goal is clear communication and interpretation, not a single required physical posture.

## Handwriting communicates too

Imagine receiving two copies of a character's letter. One has crowded words and uneven spacing that makes “we are leaving” difficult to distinguish from “wear e leaving”. The other uses clear letter shapes, consistent spacing and readable lines. The second helps the reader concentrate on the message.

Legible handwriting does not require every person to use identical letter shapes. It requires that letters are recognisable and that words and lines are spaced clearly enough to read. A comfortable grip and posture can help, but students should use whatever approved writing supports they need.

### Letter formation and spacing

Write the sentence **“Ada looked toward the open window.”** slowly. Notice the letters that rise above the main body of lowercase writing, such as **d**, **l** and **t**, and those that descend below the line, such as **g** and **p** in other words. Keep letters within a word close enough to look connected as a unit, and leave a visible space between words.

If the letters lean at slightly different angles, that is less important than whether another person can read the sentence. First aim for clarity; speed can develop later.

## Guided speaking practice

**1.** Read “Did it fall?” as a genuine question. **Success:** the delivery communicates uncertainty and gives the listener a clear question.

**2.** Read “Then we'd better find out who” with determination but without shouting. **Success:** the emphasis and pace suggest resolve.

**3.** Read the entire scene, pausing between the narrator's words and the dialogue. **Success:** listeners can tell when the speaker changes.

**4.** Explain why “No.” may be followed by a longer pause in Ada's dialogue. **Model:** The pause suggests she is considering a more serious possibility before speaking.

## Independent performance task

Choose **Ada** or **Finn** and prepare a **45–75 second** reading of the scene, including narrator lines. Mark two places where you will pause and underline two words you intend to emphasise. Rehearse once at a comfortable pace. Then record or perform the passage for an adult or partner.

**Model performance notes:** Pause after “The map was missing from the table”; emphasise **here** in Ada's first line; pause after “No”; emphasise **who** in Finn's final line. Other interpretations are acceptable if supported by the scene.

**Assessment:** Speech is understandable (2), pace and pauses support meaning (2), emphasis fits character and context (2), punctuation is interpreted sensibly (2), speaker changes are clear (2). Do not penalise accent, disability or an approved communication accommodation.

## Independent handwriting task

Copy the five lines of the mini-scene onto lined paper, or complete an accessible equivalent. Keep the punctuation, capitals and paragraph breaks accurate. If writing by hand, aim for consistent spacing and recognisable letters. If an accessible typing alternative is used, focus on accurate transcription and readable formatting.

**Model checklist:** “The map was missing from the table.” begins with a capital and ends with a full stop. Dialogue begins and ends with quotation marks. New speakers begin new paragraphs. The contractions **didn't** and **we'd** retain their apostrophes.

**Editing challenge:** Correct this transcription:  
`the map was missing from the table "did it fall" said finn`

**Answer:** `The map was missing from the table. “Did it fall?” said Finn.` The capital letters identify the start of the sentence and Finn's name; the question mark belongs inside the quotation marks.

## Final check

**1 (2).** Explain one way emphasis can change meaning. **Stressing different words can change what the listener thinks is important or implied.**  
**2 (2).** What does a question mark usually tell a reader? **That the sentence is a question and should be delivered accordingly.**  
**3 (2).** Why should a performer avoid rushing every line? **Listeners need time to follow meaning and speaker changes.**  
**4 (2).** Name two features of legible handwriting. **Recognisable letter forms and clear spacing**, or other relevant features.  
**5 (2).** Correct the punctuation of `ada said i found it`. **Ada said, “I found it.”**

**Feedback:** If words are unclear, practise at a slower pace with comfortable volume. If expression is flat, choose one meaningful word to emphasise rather than exaggerating every line. If transcription is hard to read, enlarge spacing between words and check punctuation before increasing speed.
