# The still-life studio: making light visible

**Year 5 · Creative Arts · Week 1 Lesson 2 · Create and perform · 60 minutes.** Retain existing curriculum metadata. No reflections.

## A drawing is a series of decisions

Place a cup on a table and look at it from where you are sitting. You might recognise the cup immediately, but drawing it is harder than naming it. You have to decide where its edges begin and end, how wide its opening appears, where the light falls and how its shadow touches the table. The aim today is to create a finished still-life drawing using line, shape and tone, rather than relying on a dark outline alone.

A **still life** is an artwork showing arranged objects that do not move while they are being drawn. Artists use still lifes to study form, proportion, surface and light. You can work from a real mug, fruit or box, or use the supplied drawing reference. You need a pencil, paper and an eraser if one is available. An eraser is helpful but not essential: light construction lines can remain part of an expressive drawing.

## First, see the space around the object

Imagine a mug with a handle on the right. Most beginners begin by drawing the handle because it is distinctive. That can make the rest of the mug too small. Instead, look at the whole object. How tall is it compared with its width? Does the handle extend about a quarter of the mug's width, or nearly half? The empty space inside the handle has a shape too. Artists call these empty surrounding areas **negative space**. Observing them can help you position difficult parts accurately.

Draw a light rectangle that would fit around the mug, including its handle. This is a **bounding box**, a temporary guide. Inside it, place the mug's top and bottom. From a normal seated viewpoint, a circular rim appears as an ellipse. The ellipse is wider than it is tall. If you draw it as a pointed oval, the rim may look pinched; try a smooth curve instead.

## Build the form before adding darkness

Use gentle pencil pressure to mark the two sides of the mug. They may be nearly parallel or taper slightly. Draw the base as a shallow curve. Add the handle by drawing its outside edge first, then the opening inside. Pause to compare proportions: is the handle attached near the top and lower down the body? Does the opening look large enough for fingers?

Only after the structure looks convincing should you begin shading. Choose a clear light direction. Suppose the light comes from the upper left. Leave the left-facing area relatively light. Add a middle tone toward the centre and darker tone on the right. This change in tone makes the cylindrical body look rounded.

A **cast shadow** is the darker shape on the table where the mug blocks light. With the light at upper left, the shadow generally extends toward the lower right. The shadow's exact edge depends on the light source; a large diffuse light creates softer edges than a small bright lamp. You do not need to invent a sharp black triangle if the actual shadow is soft.

## Worked example: correcting a flat-looking cup

A student draws a mug with an accurate outline, then shades the entire body the same medium grey. It still looks flat. Why? Because uniform shading does not show the surface turning away from the light. The student can improve the drawing by preserving a lighter area, gradually darkening one side and adding a cast shadow that agrees with the light direction.

Now imagine the student adds very dark hatching to every edge. The outline becomes heavy and distracts from the shape. A better choice is to vary **line weight**: some edges can be darker, while edges facing strong light can be faint. Drawing is not about making every mark equally bold.

## Practise before the final drawing

**Exercise 1: Proportion.** Draw a rectangle twice as tall as it is wide. Place an ellipse near the top and a shallow curve near the bottom. Check that the sides connect smoothly. **Model criterion:** The ellipse is symmetrical enough to suggest a round opening seen at an angle; it is not a pointed diamond.

**Exercise 2: Tone.** Create five adjacent boxes, moving from white to very dark. Use hatching or controlled pencil pressure. **Model criterion:** A viewer can distinguish all five values without labels. If two middle values merge, increase the difference gradually.

**Exercise 3: Shadow.** Sketch a simple ball with light coming from the upper left. Shade the ball's lower-right side and draw a cast shadow on the surface to the right. **Model criterion:** The darkest area on the ball and the cast shadow are not mistaken for the same thing.

## Your finished artwork

Arrange one or two everyday objects, or use this described reference: **a plain cylindrical mug stands to the left of a round apple on a table; light comes from the upper left; both cast shadows to the right; the mug is taller than the apple.**

Draw the whole arrangement, leaving space around the objects. Work through four stages: light construction, corrected contours, tonal shading and cast shadows. Keep at least three visibly different tones. Spend more time looking at proportions than decorating the background. A believable apple need not be perfectly circular; its slight irregularity can make it feel more natural.

If working digitally, use a pencil-like tool that allows lighter and darker strokes. If a student cannot draw by hand, they may annotate a provided outline with tone and light direction; the assessed skill remains understanding visual form.

## A curator's assessment

Imagine two drawings. Drawing A has intricate patterns on the mug but no clear light source. Drawing B has fewer details but convincing proportions, a light-to-dark transition and shadows that fall consistently. For today's objective, **Drawing B demonstrates the target skills more clearly**. Decoration is not wrong, but it does not replace observation of form.

### Finished artwork rubric — 10 points

**Proportion and placement (2):** objects fit comfortably and show considered size relationships. **Contour and line control (2):** shapes are recognisable, with intentional marks. **Tonal range (2):** at least three meaningful values. **Consistent lighting (2):** lighter and darker sides match the chosen light direction. **Cast shadows (2):** shadows anchor objects to the surface and generally fall away from the light.

### Check your understanding

**1.** Why might a mug opening be drawn as an ellipse? **Because a circular opening viewed at an angle appears flattened.**  
**2.** What is negative space? **The empty space around or within objects, such as the opening of a handle.**  
**3.** Why can uniform shading make a cylinder look flat? **It fails to show the change in light across a curved surface.**  
**4.** Where would a cast shadow usually extend if light comes from the upper left? **Away from the light, generally toward the lower right.**
