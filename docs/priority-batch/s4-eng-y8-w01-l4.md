## Teaching 1: Plan the listener’s route through the incident

**An oral telling unfolds once for a listener.**
Introduce person, setting and goal before the problem, response and outcome. A listener may not be able to reread a previous sentence, so clear connections matter.

**Select a perspective deliberately.**
Read the Sam version below. Its focus is preparing the exhibition and dealing with a question about the sign. Keep that focus if retelling this version; do not quietly turn Kai’s accusation into a confirmed event.

**Choose essential events rather than every sentence.**
Arrival and directions establish the goal, the missing sign creates the problem, checking the gap leads to discovery, and securing the corners leads to readiness. Preserve those connections in your own wording.

Write a short event map before planning delivery. A dramatic voice cannot compensate for a missing cause or an unexplained outcome.

READ THE COMPLETE ORIGINAL SAM VERSION
BEFORE THE OPENING
Sam reached the courtyard at five past nine carrying tape for the direction signs. The exhibition would open at ten, and visitors needed to find the garden gate. Sam fixed an arrow near the entrance, then looked towards the display tables.
Kai stood beside a model with a phone in hand. A few minutes later, Kai put the phone down and looked at the empty space beside the board.
“Where did you put the sign?” Kai asked.
Sam followed the glance towards the roll of tape. “I haven’t moved your sign. Have you looked behind the board?”
Kai bent down and drew out the blue sign. The fan faced the table; its draught could explain the paper’s movement, although Sam had not seen it fall.
Sam held the board while Kai replaced the sign. “We can tape the corners.”
“That was my plan,” Kai said.
Sam chose not to argue about whose idea it was. The visitors would soon arrive, and the sign needed to stay visible. At ten, the corners were secure and both displays were ready.

I DO:
Beginning: Sam prepares directions before the exhibition.
Problem: Kai questions Sam about the missing sign.
Response: Sam suggests checking the gap and helps secure the sign.
Outcome: both displays are ready at opening.

CHECK:

## Teaching 2: Create cues that prompt complete ideas

**Cue notes are brief reminders.**
Use a short phrase per event, not a full paragraph to read. A cue such as gate directions carries more meaning than gate alone. Choose a format accessible to you.

**Explain from the cue, then move on.**
Glance at one note and communicate its event as a complete idea. Looking back is allowed. The cue supports memory but the listener needs the relationship, not just a list of nouns.

**Keep viewpoint in the cues.**
A note such as Kai’s accusation can prompt a reported claim. A note such as Sam took sign would present an unsupported event. The notes should preserve the selected account’s limits.

Test the cue by looking away and explaining. If it loses an action or encourages an inaccurate claim, revise the wording before rehearsal.

I DO:
Cue: “Gate directions before opening.”
Telling: “Sam arrived early to put up directions so visitors could find the courtyard.”
Cue: “Question → gap check.”
Telling: “Kai asked about the missing sign, and Sam suggested looking behind the board.”

YOU TRY:
Turn “corners secured, opening” into a connected outcome.

CHECK:
Explain that they secured the sign so it stayed visible when the exhibition opened. Keep the action and result, not only two nouns.

Notice: A useful cue prompts an accurate action and relationship.

**Question:** Which cue preserves the account’s knowledge limit?

1. Sam definitely caused every problem
2. Kai’s private thoughts proved
3. Question about sign → check gap
4. All events invented by the listener

Reviewer answer: Question about sign → check gap

Why: The cue records the question and response without treating the accusation as a verified action.

### Help with this step

1. Look at what the cue would make you claim.
2. Keep a reported interpretation separate from an observed event.
3. Choose a brief action phrase that prompts an accurate full idea.

**Pace, pause and emphasis guide attention.**
Pace is speed, a pause separates thoughts, and emphasis makes a selected word or phrase stand out. Choose them for the meaning you want a listener to follow.

**Clarify the viewpoint shift and discovery.**
Give the opening enough time to introduce Sam’s purpose. A brief pause before the gap check can mark a change from accusation to action. Keep connected phrases together instead of pausing between every word.

**Avoid using performance to invent certainty.**
A forceful reading of an accusation can make it sound proven. Preserve its status as a question or claim through accurate wording; delivery should not replace evidence.

Adapt to the listener and your usual communication support. Clarity does not require an accent, theatrical character voice, audience, recording or upload.

I DO:
I pause after reporting Kai’s question, then give the suggested gap check a steady pace. This helps the listener follow the response without treating the accusation as the ending.

YOU TRY:
Tell the discovery event twice. Change one pace or pause choice, then explain whether it makes the connection clearer.

CHECK:
Keep the wording accurate and related words connected. If the event is already clear, preserve the useful choice instead of exaggerating every word.

Notice: A delivery choice needs a reason tied to meaning, not only a claim that it sounds dramatic.

## Teaching 4: Rehearse, reflect and label the evidence

**Rehearsal tests a plan.**
Communicate the whole telling once. Check event accuracy first, then clarity and delivery. A smooth performance can still make an unsupported claim; a correct plan does not by itself demonstrate actual speaking.

**Make one specific improvement.**
Restore a missing action, clarify a reference or give the discovery enough time. Practise again and explain what changed and why. If the first attempt worked, describe the check that confirmed it.

**Separate evidence types.**
Cues and a written delivery plan show preparation. An observed or recorded telling can show actual delivery. Solo practice allows reflection on what you noticed, but it is not proof that a real listener understood.

Use your normal communication method and identify it accurately. This lesson can be completed independently; a partner or recording is optional.

I DO:
First draft cue: “Sam moved sign.”
Repair: “Kai asks; sign found in gap.”
Reason: the revised cue avoids turning an accusation into a reported fact.
Rehearsal change: I slow the discovery and explain how securing the corners completes the goal.

YOU TRY:
Check one cue or delivery choice, practise again and explain the improvement with its evidence type.

CHECK:
Name an actual change and purpose. Label solo, observed, recorded or written practice rather than overstating what the evidence demonstrates.

Notice: Do not label a written plan as an observed performance.

**Question:** What does a written cue and delivery plan show by itself?

1. Preparation and intended choices
2. An audience’s actual response
3. A recorded oral performance
4. Proof of another person’s listening

Reviewer answer: Preparation and intended choices

Why: The plan records preparation; actual delivery and interaction need separate observed or recorded evidence.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the distinction between preparation and performance.
2. Choose one actual accuracy or delivery improvement from your practice.
3. Explain the change and label the kind of evidence you really produced.
