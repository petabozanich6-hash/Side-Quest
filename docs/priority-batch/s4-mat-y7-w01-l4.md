## Teaching 1: Define integers and the reference zero

**An integer is a whole-number value with a sign when needed.**
The integers include negative whole numbers, zero and positive whole numbers: for example, negative three, zero and four. Fractions such as one half are not integers. The word whole in this explanation concerns the size of the unit, not a rule that the sign must be positive.

**Zero is a reference point.**
A signed value describes a position relative to zero. On a temperature scale, a negative reading is below zero degrees Celsius. In a floor model, negative levels can be below the ground reference. Choose and state the reference before interpreting the sign.

**Positive and negative are directions from the reference.**
A plus sign can be written for a positive value, but it is often omitted. A minus sign attached to a numeral marks a negative value. Zero is neither positive nor negative.

Do not assume that negative means physically impossible or that it always means bad. Its meaning depends on the chosen reference and context.

I DO:
I use ground level as zero. A position two levels below it is −2; three levels above it is +3, usually written 3. A ground-level position is 0.
Temperature example: −4 °C is four degrees below zero, not a count of negative objects.

YOU TRY:
With sea level defined as zero, describe a point five metres below it.

CHECK:
The signed height is −5 m. State the reference because a different zero would change the description.

## Teaching 2: Separate value from distance

**Absolute value measures distance from zero.**
Distance is non-negative. The notation |−3| means the distance of negative three from zero, which is three. Similarly, |3| is three. The bars here are absolute-value notation, not brackets telling you to change every sign in an expression.

**Opposites have equal distance and opposite sides.**
Three and negative three are opposites. Their number-line points mirror each other across zero. Zero is its own opposite.

**Distance does not decide ordinary order.**
Negative five is farther from zero than negative two, but it lies farther left and is a smaller value. Ask whether the task concerns signed value, distance or an opposite before answering.

Use the number line when the notation is unfamiliar. First identify the point, then count its distance to zero.

I DO:
Value: −5. Distance from zero: |−5| = 5. Opposite: 5.
For 2, the distance is 2 and the opposite is −2.

YOU TRY:
Find the distance and opposite for −4. Give the two answers separate labels.

CHECK:
Distance: 4. Opposite: 4. These happen to have the same numeral in this example, but they are answers to different questions. For positive four, distance stays four while its opposite is negative four.

Notice: Absolute value is distance. It does not mean that negative values are larger in ordinary order.

**Question:** What does |−5| describe?

1. A signed value smaller than −5
2. A distance of five units from zero
3. A rule that −5 is greater than 2
4. A fractional interval

Reviewer answer: A distance of five units from zero

Why: Absolute value gives the non-negative distance from zero, which is five units for −5.

### Help with this step

1. Find the signed point before thinking about distance.
2. Separate the labels value, distance and opposite.
3. Count to zero for distance; reflect across zero for the opposite.

**Greater values lie farther right.**
This rule includes negative integers, zero and positive integers. On the line, negative two lies right of negative five, so negative two is greater. The numeral five being larger than two does not reverse their signed positions.

**Compare across zero first.**
A positive integer is greater than zero; zero is greater than a negative integer. If the two values lie on opposite sides, their positions already determine the comparison.

**Compare two negatives carefully.**
The one closer to zero lies farther right and is greater. The one with a larger distance from zero lies farther left and is smaller. That statement concerns two negative values, not all possible numbers.

Locate both points or sketch the relevant interval before recording a sign. Explain position as evidence.

I DO:
−2 > −5 because −2 lies to the right of −5.
−5 < 1 because −5 is left of zero and 1 is right of zero.

YOU TRY:
Compare −4 and −1 on a number line.

CHECK:
−4 < −1. Negative one is nearer zero and farther right, so it is the greater signed value.

Notice: A larger absolute value does not mean a larger signed value.

**Question:** Which statement is true?

1. −5 > −2
2. −2 > −5
3. −2 = −5
4. Every negative integer exceeds zero

Reviewer answer: −2 > −5

Why: Negative two lies farther right than negative five on the number line, so it is greater.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the two points relative to zero.
2. Locate the value farther right before choosing a symbol.
3. Use number-line position as the reason; keep distance from zero separate.

## Teaching 4: Treat addition as a signed change

**The starting value and added change have different jobs.**
In negative three plus five, negative three is the start and positive five is the change. Begin at the starting point, then apply the added value. Do not always begin at zero.

**An added positive value moves right.**
An added negative value moves left. The numeral gives the distance. This movement model explains addition; it is not yet a rule for subtracting negatives.

**Count equal intervals.**
From negative three, five steps right pass through negative two, negative one, zero, one and two. Count movements rather than including the starting tick as an extra step.

Record the equation and movement in words so the result can be checked. A negative starting value can lead to a positive result when the rightward change is large enough.

I DO:
−3 + 5 = 2. Start at −3 and move five units right.
2 + (−4) = −2. Start at 2 and move four units left.

YOU TRY:
Start at −2 and add 3. Count the movements.

CHECK:
−2 + 3 = 1. You pass through −1, 0 and 1.

Notice: Use the added value for direction. The starting sign does not decide every movement.

**Question:** What is −3 + 5?

1. -8
2. 2
3. -2
4. 8

Reviewer answer: 2

Why: Adding positive five moves five units right from negative three to two.

### Help with this step

1. Find the starting point and added change separately.
2. Use the added sign to decide the movement direction.
3. Count equal intervals from the start and record the landing value.

## Teaching 5: Balance opposite-direction changes

**Opposite contributions can cancel in pairs.**
One positive unit and one negative unit sum to zero. Pair as many opposite units as possible; the unpaired contribution determines the result.

**Compare distances when signs differ.**
For negative seven plus three, three pairs cancel and four negative units remain. The result is negative four. For negative three plus seven, four positive units remain instead.

**Equal opposite values give zero.**
Negative five plus five equals zero. Zero is a valid result, not a missing answer. This also provides a useful check for opposites.

Say which contribution remains before writing the sign. Subtracting the distances without explaining the remaining direction can produce the correct size with the wrong sign.

I DO:
−7 + 3 = −4: three positive units cancel three of the seven negative units.
−3 + 7 = 4: three negative units cancel three positive units.

YOU TRY:
Find −6 + 4 using cancellation or a line movement.

CHECK:
−2. Four opposite pairs cancel and two negative units remain.

Notice: The remaining direction gives the sign. Merely taking the difference of magnitudes is incomplete.

**Question:** What is −7 + 3?

1. -4
2. 4
3. -10
4. 10

Reviewer answer: -4

Why: Three opposite pairs cancel, leaving four negative units.

### Help with this step

1. Compare the opposite-direction contributions.
2. Pair equal positive and negative units until one direction remains.
3. Use the unpaired direction for the sign and its distance for the size.

## Put it all together

I DO: A complete integer fluency with checks response.
Use a one-unit number line with zero as reference. Values: −6, 0, 4 and −2.
Distances: 6, 0, 4, 2. Ascending: −6, −2, 0, 4. Sums: −6 + 4 = −2 and −2 + (−6) = −8. The larger distance of −6 does not make it greater than −2.

WHY THE METHOD WORKS
Position represents signed value; distance from zero answers a different question. For addition, the first value is the starting position and the second is a signed change. Keep these roles separate whenever they occur in the task.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Calling −6 greater than −2 because six exceeds two confuses distance with signed value. Locate the points and use the one farther right as the greater value.

CHECK
Rebuild the requested representation and read the conclusion in the supplied context. Use only the methods taught in this lesson, not an operation rule borrowed from a different topic.
