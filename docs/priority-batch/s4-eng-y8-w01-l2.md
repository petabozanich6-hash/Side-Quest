## Teaching 1: Separate shared events from private interpretations

**Perspective is the position from which a story is perceived.**
A different narrator can notice different details and attach different meanings to the same events. Changing perspective does not require changing every shared event.

**Make an event record first.**
Read The Last Sign below. Record what is directly reported: Sam has tape, Kai asks about the missing sign, the sign is behind the board, and they secure it. Separately label Kai’s unsupported accusation as an interpretation.

**Distinguish fact constraints from creative choices.**
In a perspective rewrite, you may invent plausible thoughts for Sam because you are writing original fiction. Those invented thoughts are not evidence that the source proved Sam felt that way.

Keep the agreed sequence stable when the task asks for the same incident. The creative change is the account’s access and emphasis, not an unexplained replacement of the central event.

READ THE ORIGINAL SCENE
THE LAST SIGN
I was the first one ready for the courtyard exhibition. I am sure of that. My phone said 9:12 when I put it down, and the opening was not until ten. The others were still moving tables.
The blue sign beside our display had vanished. Sam was standing near the garden gate with a roll of tape. That made the answer obvious: Sam must have taken it. I had watched our table all morning, so there was no other explanation.
“Where did you put the sign?” I asked.
Sam glanced towards the gate. “I came through there at five past nine. I taped up the directions first.”
“That is not what I asked.”
Sam set the tape on a chair. “I haven’t moved your sign. Have you looked behind the board?”
I knew it could not be behind the board. I had put it neatly on the table. Still, I bent down because Sam was waiting. A blue edge showed in the narrow gap. I pulled out the sign. The fan on the windowsill was pointing straight at the display.
Sam held the board while I placed the sign back. “We can tape the corners,” Sam said.
I said that was what I had planned to do anyway. Then I noticed the time again. Between writing messages and checking a game score, I had spent several minutes looking at my phone with my back to the table. Perhaps all morning was not quite the right description.
At ten, the exhibition opened. The sign stayed in place. I told the first visitor we had solved a small problem together, which sounded better than explaining how certain I had been.

I DO:
Shared event: the blue sign is found behind the board.
Kai’s interpretation: Sam must have taken it.
Creative choice for a Sam rewrite: Sam wants visitors to find the gate. This motive is plausible fiction, not a verified fact about an unseen real person.

YOU TRY:
Label the fan’s role carefully: what is seen, and what is inferred?

CHECK:
The fan faces the display. Its draught is a plausible explanation for the paper’s movement, but neither narrator reports watching the sign fall.

## Teaching 2: Choose a viewpoint and control access

**Viewpoint controls what the narration can know.**
A first-person Sam uses I to report Sam’s experience. A third-person limited Sam uses the name or pronouns while staying with Sam’s perceptions and thoughts. Neither automatically knows Kai’s private thoughts.

**Choose one lens for the scene.**
If using third-person limited, describe what Sam sees and hears, and choose plausible interior thought for Sam. Avoid suddenly stating what Kai secretly thinks without marking a different narrative choice.

**Report behaviour before inferring motive.**
Sam can see Kai holding a phone. Sam cannot directly observe the contents of Kai’s mind. Use a cautious interpretation or leave the action to speak for itself.

A consistent lens helps a reader know which information is available. Precision matters more than adding I to every sentence.

I DO:
Third-person limited: “Sam saw Kai put the phone down and look at the empty space.”
Within that lens, I can invent Sam’s thought about getting ready. I avoid “Kai secretly wanted to blame someone” as an unmarked fact about Kai’s mind.

YOU TRY:
Rewrite one observed action through Sam’s lens without claiming Kai’s private motive.

CHECK:
“Kai asked where the sign was” preserves something Sam hears. An interpretation can be added cautiously, but it should not become unexplained mind-reading.

Notice: Changing pronouns alone does not create a controlled perspective. Check access to information.

**Question:** Which sentence fits third-person limited Sam?

1. Sam knew every secret thought in Kai’s mind without explanation
2. Sam heard Kai ask where the sign was
3. The author becomes the sign
4. All characters share one hidden thought

Reviewer answer: Sam heard Kai ask where the sign was

Why: Sam can hear the spoken question. The lens does not automatically provide direct access to Kai’s private thoughts.

### Help with this step

1. Identify whose perceptions anchor this version.
2. Keep observed actions separate from another character’s private thoughts.
3. Write what the focal character can see or hear before adding a qualified interpretation.

**Voice is the impression created by the telling.**
Word choice, sentence patterns and selected details can suggest attitude and priorities. A practical account may attend to signs, visitors and the next action; a defensive account may emphasise certainty and personal credit.

**Choose a coherent priority.**
For Sam, one creative choice is focusing on preparing the entrance. Select details about arrows and securing corners to support that priority. You do not need to make Sam flawless or turn Kai into a villain.

**Show a thought through a decision.**
Sam can decide not to argue about credit because visitors will arrive soon. An action and brief thought can establish voice more precisely than a label such as nice.

Keep language consistent with the chosen lens and purpose. A new perspective should reveal a different emphasis while remaining part of the same incident.

I DO:
“Sam chose not to argue about whose idea it was. The visitors would soon arrive, and the sign needed to stay visible.”
This invented thought foregrounds practical preparation. It is a narrative choice, not proof that Sam always behaves that way.

YOU TRY:
Choose one detail Sam might prioritise in the courtyard and explain how it supports the voice.

CHECK:
An arrow near the entrance can support a concern with helping visitors find the route. Keep the priority linked to an action.

Notice: A different perspective can complicate the incident without reversing every judgement into its opposite.

## Teaching 4: Revise for continuity and explain the effect

**Compare the rewrite with the event record.**
Check the arrival time stated by Sam, the question, discovery and secured corners. If you change an event intentionally, it becomes an adaptation rather than the same-incident rewrite requested here.

**Check access sentence by sentence.**
Ask how Sam knows each stated fact. A perception, spoken line or chosen interior thought should fit the viewpoint. Remove unmarked access to another mind.

**Explain the perspective’s effect.**
Name a detail or wording choice and what it makes the reader notice. For example, preparing directions may shift attention from blame to a shared task.

Show one before-and-after revision with a reason. That records your craft decision more clearly than saying you made it sound better.

I DO:
Before: “Sam knew Kai was embarrassed.”
After: “Kai looked at the sign and said it had been the plan all along.”
Reason: the revision reports visible or audible behaviour instead of unexplained access to Kai’s private feeling.

YOU TRY:
Choose a sentence in your draft and test how the focal character could know it.

CHECK:
If the knowledge is not available through the chosen lens, revise it into observation, qualified inference or your own focal character’s thought.

Notice: An effect explanation links a craft choice with a reader’s focus. It is not only a plot summary.

**Question:** Which revision improves viewpoint control?

1. Replace unobserved private thought with observable behaviour
2. Give Sam knowledge of every future event
3. Remove the incident’s shared outcome
4. Add a new secret as if the source confirmed it

Reviewer answer: Replace unobserved private thought with observable behaviour

Why: Observable behaviour fits the lens and avoids unexplained knowledge of another character’s mind.

### Help with this step

1. Compare each sentence with the chosen viewpoint’s access.
2. Find an unsupported claim about another character’s private thought.
3. Replace it with behaviour, a qualified inference or a clearly chosen thought of the focal character.

COMPLETE THIRD-PERSON LIMITED SAM MODEL
BEFORE THE OPENING
Sam reached the courtyard at five past nine carrying tape for the direction signs. The exhibition would open at ten, and visitors needed to find the garden gate. Sam fixed an arrow near the entrance, then looked towards the display tables.
Kai stood beside a model with a phone in hand. A few minutes later, Kai put the phone down and looked at the empty space beside the board.
“Where did you put the sign?” Kai asked.
Sam followed the glance towards the roll of tape. “I haven’t moved your sign. Have you looked behind the board?”
Kai bent down and drew out the blue sign. The fan faced the table; its draught could explain the paper’s movement, although Sam had not seen it fall.
Sam held the board while Kai replaced the sign. “We can tape the corners.”
“That was my plan,” Kai said.
Sam chose not to argue about whose idea it was. The visitors would soon arrive, and the sign needed to stay visible. At ten, the corners were secure and both displays were ready.

WHY IT WORKS
The model keeps the recognisable events while selecting Sam’s observations and a practical preparation priority. The interior choice not to argue is newly created fiction, not evidence that the source established Sam’s private motive.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“Sam knew Kai secretly felt ashamed” gives unmarked access to Kai’s mind. Replace it with visible behaviour or dialogue, or use a clearly qualified interpretation.

REVISION EXPLANATION
I foregrounded directions and securing corners so the incident centres on preparation rather than the narrator’s certainty about blame.
