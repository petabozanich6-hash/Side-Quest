## Teaching 1: Separate narrator, character and author

**The narrator is the voice telling the story.**
The author creates the text; the narrator presents its events. In a first-person narrative, I may be a character inside those events. Do not assume every statement by that narrator is the author’s own view.

**Perspective limits information.**
A character narrator reports what they notice, remember or choose to tell. They cannot automatically know another character’s private thoughts. A statement about someone else’s motive may be an inference rather than observed evidence.

**Read the complete story below first.**
Track what Kai reports doing and what Kai claims to know. The first-person voice gives direct access to Kai’s account, but it does not guarantee that the account is complete or accurate.

Start with the distinction between voice and event. Later steps will evaluate particular claims rather than treating an entire narrator as simply truthful or false.

READ THE ORIGINAL STORY
THE LAST SIGN
I was the first one ready for the courtyard exhibition. I am sure of that. My phone said 9:12 when I put it down, and the opening was not until ten. The others were still moving tables.
The blue sign beside our display had vanished. Sam was standing near the garden gate with a roll of tape. That made the answer obvious: Sam must have taken it. I had watched our table all morning, so there was no other explanation.
“Where did you put the sign?” I asked.
Sam glanced towards the gate. “I came through there at five past nine. I taped up the directions first.”
“That is not what I asked.”
Sam set the tape on a chair. “I haven’t moved your sign. Have you looked behind the board?”
I knew it could not be behind the board. I had put it neatly on the table. Still, I bent down because Sam was waiting. A blue edge showed in the narrow gap. I pulled out the sign. The fan on the windowsill was pointing straight at the display.
Sam held the board while I placed the sign back. “We can tape the corners,” Sam said.
I said that was what I had planned to do anyway. Then I noticed the time again. Between writing messages and checking a game score, I had spent several minutes looking at my phone with my back to the table. Perhaps all morning was not quite the right description.
At ten, the exhibition opened. The sign stayed in place. I told the first visitor we had solved a small problem together, which sounded better than explaining how certain I had been.

I DO:
Kai is the first-person character narrator. The claim that Sam took the sign is Kai’s interpretation, not an independently confirmed event.

YOU TRY:
Find a sentence in which Kai claims knowledge of someone else’s action.

CHECK:
“Sam must have taken it” attributes an action without direct observation. The text later gives reasons to question that certainty.

1. An independent report outside the story
2. Sam in a confirmed confession
3. The first-person narrator Kai

### Help with this step

1. Look for the voice using I in the story.
2. Separate an observed action from a claim about another person’s motive or behaviour.
3. Identify who makes the claim before treating it as a verified event.

## Teaching 2: Compare a claim with contrary evidence

**Reliability concerns how far an account can be trusted.**
An unreliable narrator may misremember, misunderstand, conceal information or present a biased interpretation. This lesson evaluates reliability within a fictional text; it does not diagnose a person.

**Find an actual gap or contradiction.**
Kai claims to have watched the table all morning. Later Kai reports several minutes looking at a phone with their back to the table. Those two statements do not fully fit. The later admission weakens the claim of continuous observation.

**Check a specific claim, not every sentence at once.**
The sign really is missing from its expected place. That reported event can be accurate even while the accusation about Sam is unsupported. Reliability can vary across an account.

Use a two-column note: claim and evidence that complicates it. Explain the connection before applying the label unreliable.

I DO:
Claim: “I had watched our table all morning.”
Contrary evidence: several minutes on the phone with a back turned to the table.
Reason: Kai did not continuously observe what happened, so the certainty about Sam’s action is not justified.

YOU TRY:
Compare the claim of being first ready with Sam’s stated arrival time. What can you cautiously question?

CHECK:
Sam says five past nine, while Kai looks at the phone at 9:12. These details complicate Kai’s confident first-ready claim, but arrival and readiness are not identical. Do not claim the times alone prove every part of the opening false.

Notice: A useful judgement is specific and proportionate. One questionable claim does not make every reported event false.

**Question:** Which detail directly weakens Kai’s claim of watching continuously?

1. The sign is blue
2. Kai later admits looking at a phone with a back turned
3. The exhibition opens at ten
4. The courtyard has a gate

Reviewer answer: Kai later admits looking at a phone with a back turned

Why: The admission contradicts uninterrupted observation. The other details do not establish that gap.

### Help with this step

1. Read the claim and later admission side by side.
2. Ask what the later detail prevents Kai from knowing with certainty.
3. Explain that precise limit instead of declaring every event false.

## Teaching 3: Infer bias without inventing certainty

Learner pause: the final line pauses before continuing.

**Bias is a leaning that shapes interpretation.**
Kai interprets Sam’s nearby tape as proof of removal and resists a question that offers another explanation. The account may protect Kai’s image as prepared and competent.

**Use language as evidence.**
Words such as obvious, no other explanation and I knew signal strong certainty. Compare that certainty with the limited observation. The mismatch is more useful than simply saying Kai sounds unpleasant.

**Qualify an inference.**
Say the wording suggests defensiveness or a wish to appear prepared. That is an interpretation supported by the text, not access to a mind outside the narrated words. Alternative supported readings may be possible.

Notice the ending’s effect. Kai gives visitors a smoother account of solving the problem together. This may show selective presentation, while the admission about the phone also shows some self-correction.

I DO:
The phrase “there was no other explanation” makes Kai’s accusation sound certain. The phone admission shows a gap in observation, suggesting overconfidence rather than proof against Sam.

YOU TRY:
Explain what “which sounded better” suggests about the account Kai gives visitors.

CHECK:
It suggests Kai selects a version that presents the incident more favourably. The inference concerns the wording, not a claim that every part of the final statement is false.

Notice: Support an interpretation with words and explain its scope. Avoid turning one incident into a claim about an entire personality.

1. Look for wording that presents Kai’s certainty or self-image.

## Teaching 4: Write a claim, evidence and explanation

**An analysis explains how the account shapes meaning.**
State a focused claim about one aspect of reliability. Support it with a short quotation or accurate paraphrase, then explain what that detail changes for a reader.

**Use paired evidence when judging a contradiction.**
A certainty claim alone might not establish unreliability. Pair it with the later admission that limits the narrator’s knowledge. The contrast gives your reasoning a foundation.

**Acknowledge limits and self-correction.**
Kai’s uncertainty near the end complicates a simple label. You can argue that the accusation is unreliable while acknowledging the account reveals the narrator’s own mistake.

Read the paragraph back. It should distinguish event, interpretation and qualification, rather than retelling the plot without explaining reliability.

I DO:
“Kai’s accusation is unreliable because the confidence exceeds the observation. Kai claims to have watched all morning, but later admits facing away while using a phone. This gap means the nearby tape does not prove Sam moved the sign. The later self-correction also prevents a judgement that every part of the account is knowingly false.”

YOU TRY:
Give a focused claim about the opening, paired with a detail that complicates it.

CHECK:
Use the time details cautiously and distinguish arriving from being ready. The strongest claim describes overconfidence rather than claiming more than the evidence proves.

Notice: Evidence needs an explained relationship. Quoting two lines without connecting them is not yet analysis.

**Question:** What strengthens a reliability analysis?

1. A specific claim, paired evidence and an explained limit
2. A judgement based only on liking the character
3. A retelling with no link to reliability
4. An invented confession

Reviewer answer: A specific claim, paired evidence and an explained limit

Why: A focused claim supported by connected evidence makes the judgement checkable and proportionate.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the paragraph’s claim and paired evidence.
2. Explain what the contradiction changes about the narrator’s knowledge.
3. Include a qualification when the text also shows self-correction.

MODEL ANALYSIS
Kai’s accusation is unreliable because its certainty exceeds the narrator’s observation. Kai claims to have watched the table all morning, yet later admits spending several minutes on a phone with a back turned. The contrast creates a gap in what Kai could know about the sign. Sam’s tape and nearby position therefore do not prove removal. The sign is found behind the board, and the fan offers a plausible alternative cause without either character reporting the fall. The account also reveals Kai’s self-correction, so it would be too broad to label every event a deliberate lie. The ending suggests selective presentation when Kai says the cooperative version sounded better.

WHY IT WORKS
The claim is specific, the evidence is paired and each inference has a stated limit.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“Kai lies about everything” exceeds the text. Revise to a judgement about the unsupported accusation and explain the contradictory observation claim.
