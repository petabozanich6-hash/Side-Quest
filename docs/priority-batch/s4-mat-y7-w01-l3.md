## Teaching 1: Treat addition as a signed change

**The starting value and added change have different jobs.**
In negative three plus five, negative three is the start and positive five is the change. Begin at the starting point, then apply the added value. Do not always begin at zero.

**An added positive value moves right.**
An added negative value moves left. The numeral gives the distance. This movement model explains addition; it is not yet a rule for subtracting negatives.

**Count equal intervals.**
From negative three, five steps right pass through negative two, negative one, zero, one and two. Count movements rather than including the starting tick as an extra step.

Record the equation and movement in words so the result can be checked. A negative starting value can lead to a positive result when the rightward change is large enough.

I DO:
−3 + 5 = 2. Start at −3 and move five units right.
2 + (−4) = −2. Start at 2 and move four units left.

YOU TRY:
Start at −2 and add 3. Count the movements.

CHECK:
−2 + 3 = 1. You pass through −1, 0 and 1.

Diagram: A number line from -6 to 6, with equal one-unit intervals. -3 is highlighted. Values increase to the right.

Position and distance from zero answer different questions.

Notice: Use the added value for direction. The starting sign does not decide every movement.

**Question:** What is −3 + 5?

1. -8
2. 2
3. -2
4. 8

Reviewer answer: 2

Why: Adding positive five moves five units right from negative three to two.

### Help with this step

1. Find the starting point and added change separately.
2. Use the added sign to decide the movement direction.
3. Count equal intervals from the start and record the landing value.

## Teaching 2: Combine changes with the same sign

**Same-direction changes accumulate.**
Two positive values add to a positive total. Two negative values add to a negative total because both contributions point left. Add their distances, then retain the shared direction.

**Explain before using a shortcut.**
Negative two plus negative four represents two leftward units and four more leftward units. The total is six leftward units, recorded as negative six. It does not become positive merely because there are two minus signs.

**Bracket an added negative when helpful.**
Write −2 + (−4) to make the added value clear. The plus sign is the operation; the minus sign in the brackets belongs to the negative value.

Check with a number-line movement from the first addend. A second leftward move cannot land to the right of the starting point.

I DO:
−2 + (−4) = −6. Combine the two leftward contributions.
3 + 4 = 7. Combine the two rightward contributions.

YOU TRY:
Find −3 + (−2), then check the direction of the result.

CHECK:
−5. Starting at −3 and adding a negative two moves farther left.

Notice: Two negative addends do not cancel. Rules for multiplication or subtraction would answer a different operation.

**Question:** What is −2 + (−4)?

1. 6
2. -2
3. -6
4. 2

Reviewer answer: -6

Why: Both addends are negative, so their leftward distances combine to negative six.

### Help with this step

1. Look at whether both contributions point the same way.
2. Combine the distances while keeping their shared direction.
3. Verify by moving from the first value in the direction of the second.

**Opposite contributions can cancel in pairs.**
One positive unit and one negative unit sum to zero. Pair as many opposite units as possible; the unpaired contribution determines the result.

**Compare distances when signs differ.**
For negative seven plus three, three pairs cancel and four negative units remain. The result is negative four. For negative three plus seven, four positive units remain instead.

**Equal opposite values give zero.**
Negative five plus five equals zero. Zero is a valid result, not a missing answer. This also provides a useful check for opposites.

Say which contribution remains before writing the sign. Subtracting the distances without explaining the remaining direction can produce the correct size with the wrong sign.

I DO:
−7 + 3 = −4: three positive units cancel three of the seven negative units.
−3 + 7 = 4: three negative units cancel three positive units.

YOU TRY:
Find −6 + 4 using cancellation or a line movement.

CHECK:
−2. Four opposite pairs cancel and two negative units remain.

Notice: The remaining direction gives the sign. Merely taking the difference of magnitudes is incomplete.

**Question:** What is −7 + 3?

1. -4
2. 4
3. -10
4. 10

Reviewer answer: -4

Why: Three opposite pairs cancel, leaving four negative units.

### Help with this step

1. Compare the opposite-direction contributions.
2. Pair equal positive and negative units until one direction remains.
3. Use the unpaired direction for the sign and its distance for the size.

## Teaching 4: Model and check a contextual change

**Write the starting reading first.**
If a temperature starts at negative four degrees Celsius and rises six degrees, model −4 + 6. A rise is a positive change on this scale; the negative start remains part of the equation.

**Attach units to the conclusion.**
The sum is two, so the final reading is two degrees Celsius. Do not report six as the final reading: six describes the change, not the endpoint.

**Check through the movement.**
From negative four to zero takes four of the six upward units. Two more take the reading to positive two. This checks the sign and distance without relying on an unexplained slogan.

If a change is downward, use an added negative value. Keep reference, starting value and change consistent throughout the problem.

I DO:
Start −4 °C; rise 6 °C.
Equation: −4 + 6 = 2.
Conclusion: the final temperature is 2 °C.

YOU TRY:
Start 3 °C and fall 5 °C. Write the change as an added value.

CHECK:
3 + (−5) = −2, so the final reading is −2 °C.

Notice: Separate the start, change and endpoint. They are not interchangeable labels.

**Question:** A reading starts at −4 °C and rises 6 °C. What is the final reading?

1. −10 °C
2. 6 °C
3. −2 °C
4. 2 °C

Reviewer answer: 2 °C

Why: The positive change crosses zero and finishes two degrees above it.

### Help with this step

1. Label the starting measurement and signed change.
2. Decide whether the change moves the reading up or down the same scale.
3. Write the addition equation and check the path across the reference.

## Put it all together

WHY THE METHOD WORKS
Position represents signed value; distance from zero answers a different question. For addition, the first value is the starting position and the second is a signed change. Keep these roles separate whenever they occur in the task.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
Deleting a negative sign changes the value. Keep it attached and interpret it using the stated zero before continuing.
