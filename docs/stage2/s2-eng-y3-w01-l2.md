# Year 3: Build a clear sentence

Stage 2 · Year 3 · English · Week 1 · Lesson 2 · 60 minutes

Seed key: `s2-eng-y3-w01-l2`. Status: unreviewed. Original content. Outcomes: EN2-CWT-01. These are contributions to the outcomes, not full coverage.

## Accept the quest

Become a Sentence Builder! Turn an idea into clear words, then check that your reader knows what you mean.

**Learning intention:** We are learning to write clear statements with a complete idea, a capital letter and suitable ending punctuation.

- I can say a complete idea before writing it.
- I can show who or what my statement is about.
- I can start with a capital and finish with a full stop.
- I can add a useful detail and reread my sentence.

**Materials:** Everything you need to read is included in this lesson; Optional: paper and a pencil

### Help with this step

1. Read the mission and notice that you will begin by saying an idea.
2. Choose a simple person and object you can picture; the lesson will help you connect them.
3. Start with the first teaching step. You can practise orally before using the text box.

## Teaching 1: Say one complete idea

**What does a sentence do?**
A sentence helps a reader follow an idea. In this lesson we practise statements: sentences that give information. Say your idea aloud before writing. This helps you notice whether a piece is missing.

**Give the reader enough information.**
For these statements, tell who or what you mean and what happens or what it is like. “The bag is wet” describes the bag. “Ava carried the bag” tells an action. Both give a complete idea.

**Compare a piece with a thought.**
“The wet bag” names something, but leaves the reader asking what about it. “The wet bag dripped” finishes the thought. A fragment is an unfinished piece of a sentence.

Do not worry about writing a long sentence. First make one short idea clear. Other kinds of sentences can work differently; today we are building these information statements.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Idea piece: The red kite.
I ask, “What about the kite?” I add what happened: “The red kite fell.” Now the reader can follow the event.

Another complete idea: “The kite is torn.” This describes it instead of telling an action.

YOU TRY:
“Ben opened...” What is missing? Say a possible ending before reading on.

CHECK:
“Ben opened the gate” tells us what he opened. “Ben opened” leaves this particular intended action unfinished. Choose an ending that makes sense, rather than adding an unrelated word.

**Notice:** Say the whole idea once. If it leaves an important question unanswered, add the missing information before checking the letters.

**Question:** Which statement gives a complete idea?

1. The blue bag
2. Ava carried the bag
3. Beside the bag
4. The very wet

**Reviewer answer:** Ava carried the bag

**Why:** Ava carried the bag tells who acted and what she carried. The other choices are pieces that do not give a complete statement.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a named thing and a complete thought.
2. Ask what about the person or object needs to be told.
3. Say the whole idea. Add an action or description if an important part is missing.

## Teaching 2: Show who or what, then what happens

**Find the two useful parts.**
In “Ben held the string”, Ben tells us who. Held the string tells us what he did. You can use those two questions to build an action statement.

**A statement can describe too.**
“The string is long” tells what the statement is about and what it is like. Not every sentence describes a movement. Words such as is can connect something with its description.

**Keep your words together.**
Choose words that make sense as one idea. “The string carried Ben” has the same words as another sentence, but changes who does what. Read for meaning, not just whether you used a set of words.

**Try a simple frame.**
“Who or what? ____.” “What happens or what is it like? ____.” Join your answers and say the whole statement. Use the frame as help, not as extra words to copy into every sentence.

### Show me an example

I DO:
Who? Ava.
What happened? Picked up the kite.
Statement: “Ava picked up the kite.”

What? The kite.
What is it like? Torn.
Statement: “The kite is torn.” I need is to make this description read as a complete statement.

YOU TRY:
Use Ben and held the gate to build one statement. Then describe the gate as open.

CHECK:
“Ben held the gate.” “The gate is open.” These are different ideas: one tells an action and the other describes something.

**Teaching diagram:** Two statement patterns: Ava picked up the kite tells who and what happened; the kite is torn tells what and what it is like.

*Use the questions to check meaning.*

**Notice:** Do not choose words only because they fit in a box. Say the finished idea and check who is doing the action.

**Question:** In “Ben held the string”, which words tell what Ben did?

1. Ben
2. The name Ben
3. Held the string
4. String only

**Reviewer answer:** Held the string

**Why:** Held the string tells the action and its object. Ben identifies who; string alone names a thing without the action.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at who or what and the second part of the statement.
2. Separate the person from what happens before joining the words.
3. Use the two questions to test your idea, then reread the word order.

## Teaching 3: Mark the beginning and ending

**Help the reader see the sentence.**
Start a sentence with a capital letter. Finish these information statements with a full stop. A full stop marks where the idea ends, not where you happen to reach the edge of the page.

**Names also have capitals.**
In “Ben helped Ava”, both names begin with capitals even when a name is not the first word. Ordinary words in the middle do not need capitals simply because they matter.

**Choose an ending for the job.**
A question mark ends a question, such as “Where is the kite?” An exclamation mark can show strong feeling. For our ordinary statements, use a full stop.

**Check meaning as well as marks.**
Adding a capital and full stop to “The blue bag” does not supply its missing idea. Make the thought complete first, then mark its beginning and ending.

**Original episode:** A thought needs more than marks

**Pip:** I wrote: the red kite. I added a capital and a full stop. Is the idea finished?

**Juno:** Ask what about the kite. Add what happens or what it is like.

**Pip:** Now I can say: The red kite is torn. The reader gets a whole idea.

**Juno:** Pause and try. Finish an idea first, then check its beginning and ending. **[Pause to try; press Play to continue.]**

### Show me an example

I DO:
Draft: ava held the gate
I change ava to Ava and add a full stop: “Ava held the gate.”

COMPARE:
“The blue bag.” has marks but still does not tell us what about the bag in this task.
“The blue bag dripped.” has both a complete thought and the marks.

YOU TRY:
Fix “ben helped ava” as an information statement.

CHECK:
“Ben helped Ava.” Both names have capitals and the complete statement ends with a full stop. No capital is needed on helped.

**Notice:** Do not use punctuation to hide a missing thought. A sentence check needs both clear meaning and correct marks.

**Question:** Which version has suitable capitals and a full stop?

1. ben helped Ava.
2. Ben helped ava
3. Ben Helped Ava
4. Ben helped Ava.

**Reviewer answer:** Ben helped Ava.

**Why:** Ben and Ava are names with capitals, helped stays lower case and the statement ends with a full stop.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at meaning checks and sentence-mark checks.
2. Find the name or first word, then locate the end of the complete idea.
3. Check the beginning and ending separately, keeping ordinary middle words lower case when appropriate.

## Teaching 4: Add one helpful detail and reread

**A useful detail answers a question.**
Once your statement is clear, you can add where, when or how. “Ava carried the kite” becomes “Ava carried the kite to the bench.” The added words tell where she carried it.

**Keep one idea easy to follow.**
You do not have to squeeze every event into one sentence. “Ava carried the kite Ben shut the gate” joins two complete statements without showing their boundary. For now, split them into two sentences.

**Reread your own writing.**
Read aloud or quietly. Ask: Does it make sense? Are words missing? Did I start with a capital? Did I finish the statement? Change one problem, then read again.

**Choose detail, not just length.**
A longer sentence is useful when the added words help a reader picture or understand the idea. Leave out unrelated information.

### Show me an example

I DO:
First statement: “Ben held the gate.”
Useful detail: “Ben held the gate for Ava.” This explains who benefited.

COMMON MISTAKE:
“Ben held the gate Ava carried the kite.” I can hear two thoughts. I fix the boundary: “Ben held the gate. Ava carried the kite.”

YOU TRY:
Add a where detail to “Ava put the kite down.” Then read your sentence once to check it.

CHECK:
One possible sentence is “Ava put the kite down on the bench.” Other sensible places are fine. Keep the original action and make the added detail useful.

**Notice:** If you can hear two separate ideas, try a full stop between them. Add one useful detail at a time.

**Question:** Which detail tells where in “Ava placed the kite on the bench”?

1. On the bench
2. Ava
3. Placed
4. The name Ava

**Reviewer answer:** On the bench

**Why:** On the bench tells the place where Ava put the kite. The other words identify the person or action.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the useful-detail questions.
2. Ask whether the new words tell a place, time or manner.
3. Read the original action and added words together. Keep the detail only if it helps the reader.

## Put it all together

I DO: Build and check three statements.
1. Idea: Ava and a kite. I decide what happened: “Ava carried the kite.”
2. Add a useful where detail: “Ava carried the kite to the bench.”
3. A second idea: “Ben held the gate for her.”
4. A description: “The kite was wet.”

MY FINISHED MINI-PASSAGE
Ava carried the kite to the bench. Ben held the gate for her. The kite was wet.

WHY IT WORKS
Each statement gives a complete idea. Names begin with capitals. Each sentence has an end mark. To the bench helps the reader picture the place. For her is clear here because Ava has already been named.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“The wet kite.” is a piece. I make it a statement: “The kite was wet.” A capital and full stop alone cannot add the missing information.

REREAD
I check the words, marks and meaning before calling the passage finished.

**Teaching diagram:** A writing method: say an idea, write it, check the complete thought and sentence marks, then reread the detail.

*Check meaning before decorating the sentence.*

### Help with this step

1. Read the finished mini-passage and then the steps that built it.
2. Check one complete thought before moving to the next sentence.
3. Point to a useful detail and explain what it adds to the idea.

## Try it with help

WE DO: Build two statements about a paper boat.
1. Who or what? Mila. What happened? Made a paper boat. Say the joined idea before comparing: “Mila made a paper boat.”
2. Add a where detail from your own idea. One possibility: “Mila made a paper boat at the table.”
3. Describe the boat as small. Try before comparing: “The boat is small.” The word is connects the boat with its description.
4. Fix “mila put the boat down” as a statement. Compare: “Mila put the boat down.” Check the name and full stop.
5. Reread both of your own sentences. If words are missing, fix the idea before adding more detail.
You can practise orally first. Your sensible details do not need to match the suggestions.

### Help with this step

1. Try the first person-and-action statement before comparing its suggestion.
2. Say the idea aloud, then add one sensible place detail.
3. Use “Mila ____” as a beginning and finish the action in your own words.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Write a clear mini-passage (about 20 minutes).
Choose a fictional kite, paper boat or garden task.
1. Say a statement about someone doing something. Write it in the big box or on paper.
2. Write a second statement that describes an object or gives another clear action.
3. Add one useful where, when or how detail to one statement.
4. Write a third statement to finish the small event.
5. Read all three. Check complete ideas, names and sentence capitals, full stops, and word order. Improve one sentence.
6. Submit the three sentences and explain your improvement. If you used paper, type the passage or a short summary plus the sentence you improved. No photo is required.
Use “My first sentence said ____. I changed it to ____ because ____.” to explain your edit.

**Reflect:** Which check helped you improve a sentence, and how?

### Help with this step

1. Choose one small fictional event and say its first idea.
2. Begin “My character ____.” Add the action before writing the next statement.
3. A partial opening shape is “At the table, ____ made ____.” Fill the gaps with your own idea and check it.

2. Beside the kite
3. The kite is wet
4. Wet the

**Reviewer answer:** The kite is wet

**Why:** The kite is wet names the thing and tells what it is like. The other choices are unfinished pieces in this task.

2. Made a boat
3. Boat only
4. A full stop

**Reviewer answer:** Mila

**Why:** Mila identifies who made the boat. Made a boat tells her action.

3. Mila made a boat
4. Mila made a boat.

**Reviewer answer:** Mila made a boat.

**Why:** The sentence starts with a capital on the name, has sensible lower-case words and ends with a full stop.

2. A full stop
3. A comma only
4. No mark

**Reviewer answer:** A full stop

**Why:** A full stop marks the end of the ordinary statements we are practising.

**Question:** Why does “The blue bag.” still need work as a statement here?

1. It is too short to exist
2. Blue cannot describe a bag
3. It does not tell what about the bag
4. Every sentence needs ten words

**Reviewer answer:** It does not tell what about the bag

**Why:** It names the bag but leaves the intended thought unfinished. Length alone is not the problem.

1. Beside the bench
2. Ben
3. Waited
4. The letter B

**Reviewer answer:** Beside the bench

**Why:** Beside the bench gives the place. Ben names who and waited gives the action.

2. Add an unrelated colour
