# Year 4: Follow a story through time

Become a Story Tracker! Follow Nia’s envelope mission and discover how a storyteller can change the order without changing the events.

Learning intention: We are learning to follow story events, recognise a flashback and explain how an opening affects a reader.

Duration: 60 minutes.

Success criteria:
- I can identify a character’s goal and obstacle.
- I can put events in the order they happened.
- I can recognise a signalled flashback.
- I can explain an opening using a detail from the text.

## Accept the quest

### Help with this step

1. Read Nia’s mission and the goal about following story time.
2. You will compare two tellings of the envelope events before making your own timeline.
3. Begin with Nia’s goal; later the time clues will help you separate happened order from told order.

## Teaching 1: Track the goal, obstacle and response

**A narrative tells connected events.**
A character often wants something: this is a goal. An obstacle gets in the way. The character’s response leads to a consequence, or what happens next. The ending tells the outcome. These questions help you follow many stories, although not every story uses exactly the same pattern.

**Read for actions and reasons.**
Read the whole story below once. Then ask: What does Nia want? What gets in her way? What does she do about it? Notice how the events connect rather than only collecting objects mentioned in the text.

**Check the ending against the goal.**
Nia wants to return an invitation before her brother leaves. Giving him the envelope answers that goal. The courtyard gate is a place in the story; naming it alone would not explain the outcome.

Use the text as evidence. If a feeling or reason is not stated, use the actions to make a careful suggestion instead of treating a guess as a fact.

READ THE COMPLETE ORIGINAL STORY
THE BLUE ENVELOPE
Nia stood outside the library meeting room with a blue envelope. The doors would close for the reading club in five minutes. She wanted to return the invitation before her brother left without it.
Earlier that morning, Nia had found the envelope under their kitchen table. Her brother had put it there while searching for a pencil. She had noticed his name and decided to bring it to the library.
Now she checked the meeting-room door. A notice said the club had moved to the courtyard. Nia followed the signs past the shelves. She found her brother beside the courtyard gate and handed him the envelope. He read the invitation, then held the gate while they went in together.

I DO:
Nia’s goal is returning the invitation in time. The empty meeting-room location creates a problem, so she reads the notice and follows the signs. Her response leads to finding her brother.

YOU TRY:
Which action shows that Nia deals with the changed location? Find the words before reading on.

CHECK:
She follows the signs past the shelves. That action connects the notice to reaching the courtyard; it is more useful evidence than simply naming the envelope.

Notice: A story map records what changes and why. A list of objects does not show those connections.

**Question:** Which note explains Nia’s response to the changed location?

1. She has a blue envelope
2. She follows the signs to the courtyard
3. The library has shelves
4. The invitation has a name

Reviewer answer: She follows the signs to the courtyard

Why: Following the signs is the action she takes after learning the meeting has moved. The other notes name details without explaining her response.

### Help with this step

1. Reread what Nia wants before naming the obstacle.
2. Separate the problem from the action she takes about it.
3. Use goal → obstacle → response → outcome to organise the events.

## Teaching 2: Separate event order from telling order

**Two orders can be different.**
Event order is the order things happened in the story world. Telling order is the order the writer presents them. A story can begin later, then explain an earlier event.

**Use a timeline to rebuild event order.**
Find the earliest event. Ask what must happen before the next one: Nia must find the envelope before she can carry it to the library. The club notice comes before following the signs. Number the events in that order.

**Time words are clues.**
Earlier that morning takes us back before the opening outside the room. Now brings us to the opening’s time again. The paragraph’s place on the page does not prove that its event happened after the previous paragraph.

If you are unsure, reread the transition and use the character’s actions to check. Do not reorder events merely to match the order you first read them.

I DO:
Telling order: at the meeting room → kitchen memory → courtyard search.
Event order: find envelope in kitchen → bring it to library → read notice → find brother.
The kitchen event is told second but happened first.

YOU TRY:
Which comes first in event order: carrying the envelope or finding it? Explain how you know.

CHECK:
Finding comes first because Nia needs the envelope before she can bring it. You used a connection between actions, as well as the time clue.

Diagram: Event order moves from finding the envelope to bringing it, reading the notice and finding the brother. Telling order starts at the library, goes back to the kitchen and returns.

Label the two orders before numbering.

Notice: Keep two labels: happened and told. This avoids confusing the first paragraph with the earliest event.

**Question:** Which event happened earliest, even though it is told later?

1. Nia hands over the envelope
2. The brother holds the courtyard gate
3. Nia finds the envelope under the kitchen table
4. Nia reads the library notice

Reviewer answer: Nia finds the envelope under the kitchen table

Why: The morning kitchen event happened before the library visit. Earlier that morning signals that move back in time.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the difference between happened and told.
2. Find the earliest event using the morning clue.
3. Ask what Nia must do before she can carry the envelope; use that connection to start the timeline.

## Teaching 3: Spot a flashback and return

### In-site episode: Two orders, one envelope

Pip: The story starts at the library. Did the kitchen event happen later just because I read it second?
Juno: Check Earlier that morning. It moves back to when Nia found the envelope. That happened before she carried it.
Pip: So I need one order for the events and another for the telling. Now signals the return to the library.
Juno: Pause here. Trace the discovery, journey and search in event order. Then notice the question created by the library opening.

Learner pause: the final line pauses before continuing.

**A flashback shows an earlier event.**
When a story moves back from its current moment to something earlier, that part is a flashback. Here it explains how Nia got the envelope. It adds information the opening has not yet supplied.

**Look for the move and the return.**
Mark the words Earlier that morning. Then find Now. Those signals help a reader know when the story leaves the library moment and when it returns. A memory can be longer in other stories; this one is one paragraph.

**A flashback still belongs to the story.**
It is not a new ending or a random extra event. The kitchen action explains why the invitation matters and why Nia is carrying it. The writer connects the earlier event to the present goal.

**Check meaning, not just one word.**
An ordinary sentence containing yesterday is not always a full flashback. Ask whether the telling actually moves to an earlier event and then follows that event. Use the whole passage.

I DO:
Opening moment: Nia at the library room.
Move back: “Earlier that morning...” begins the kitchen event.
Return: “Now she checked...” takes us back to the library action.
The earlier paragraph explains the envelope’s purpose.

YOU TRY:
Find the return signal. Say what action follows it.

CHECK:
Now signals the return; checking the meeting-room door follows. Naming both the signal and action shows you followed the transition.

Notice: A flashback changes the telling order. It does not change which event happened first in the story world.

**Question:** What is the main job of the kitchen flashback here?

1. To invent a second unrelated story
2. To remove the library ending
3. To prove all stories need a memory
4. To explain how Nia found the invitation she is carrying

Reviewer answer: To explain how Nia found the invitation she is carrying

Why: The earlier event supplies the background needed to understand her library goal. A flashback is a choice, not a rule for every story.

### Help with this step

1. Find the paragraph that leaves the library moment.
2. Mark a move-back clue and a return clue separately.
3. Explain where the reader goes in each paragraph before naming the structure.

## Teaching 4: Compare two openings with evidence

**A writer chooses where to begin.**
The time-order version below begins with the discovery. It helps the reader follow the events from their start. The flashback version begins near the club’s closing time; the reader first meets a goal with time pressure.

**Explain an effect carefully.**
Use three parts: the choice, a detail and what it may make a reader notice or wonder. “It is exciting” is too vague by itself. The five-minute detail explains why the library opening can create urgency.

**There is more than one useful structure.**
One opening is not automatically better. A chronological opening may be clearer for one purpose; a later opening may create a question before explaining background. Compare the effects instead of declaring that every story must use a flashback.

Reread both endings too. These versions keep the same main events and outcome. Changing the telling order does not require inventing a new event.

READ THE TIME-ORDER VERSION
THE BLUE ENVELOPE: TIME ORDER
That morning, Nia found a blue envelope under the kitchen table. Her brother had left his invitation there while searching for a pencil. She decided to bring it to the library.
Later, Nia stood outside the meeting room. The reading-club doors would close in five minutes. She wanted to return the invitation before her brother left without it. A notice said the club had moved to the courtyard.
Nia followed the signs past the shelves. She found her brother beside the courtyard gate and handed him the envelope. He read the invitation, then held the gate while they went in together.

I DO:
The flashback version begins at the meeting room. The detail “five minutes” may make a reader wonder whether Nia will find her brother in time. The time-order version explains the envelope’s discovery first, so the reader has that background before the library visit.

YOU TRY:
Explain what the time-order opening helps the reader understand first.

CHECK:
It explains how Nia found the invitation and why she brings it. An answer using that discovery is more precise than saying the opening is simply good.

Notice: Support an effect with a specific detail. Use may when describing a possible reader response.

**Question:** Which explanation links an opening detail to its effect?

1. The five-minute deadline may make a reader wonder whether Nia will arrive in time
2. The story is good because it is good
3. Every narrative must begin at a library
4. A flashback always changes the ending

Reviewer answer: The five-minute deadline may make a reader wonder whether Nia will arrive in time

Why: The deadline is evidence for a possible feeling of urgency. The other choices repeat a judgement or make an unsupported rule.

### Help with this step

1. Reread the opening sentence and deadline.
2. Choose one detail that could create a question for a reader.
3. Link the writer’s opening choice to a detail and a possible reader response.

## Put it all together

I DO: Build a complete reading response using the original story.
THE BLUE ENVELOPE
Nia stood outside the library meeting room with a blue envelope. The doors would close for the reading club in five minutes. She wanted to return the invitation before her brother left without it.
Earlier that morning, Nia had found the envelope under their kitchen table. Her brother had put it there while searching for a pencil. She had noticed his name and decided to bring it to the library.
Now she checked the meeting-room door. A notice said the club had moved to the courtyard. Nia followed the signs past the shelves. She found her brother beside the courtyard gate and handed him the envelope. He read the invitation, then held the gate while they went in together.

MY EVENT MAP
Goal: return the invitation before her brother leaves.
Obstacle: the meeting has moved from the expected room.
Response: read the notice and follow the signs.
Outcome: hand over the envelope and enter with her brother.

MY TIMELINE
1. Nia finds the envelope in the kitchen.
2. She brings it to the library.
3. She reads the changed-location notice.
4. She finds her brother and gives him the envelope.

MY STRUCTURE EXPLANATION
The writer begins at the library, then uses a flashback beginning Earlier that morning to explain the invitation. Now returns to the library. The opening’s five-minute deadline may make a reader wonder whether Nia will complete her goal in time. The chronological version gives the discovery first, so the reader knows the background sooner.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“Nia found the envelope after arriving at the library because that paragraph is second.” I fix this by checking Earlier that morning and rebuilding event order. Paragraph order and event order are different here.
