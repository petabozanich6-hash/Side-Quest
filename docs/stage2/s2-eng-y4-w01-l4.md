# Year 4: Tell a story your listener can follow

Become a Storyteller! Turn a short story into clear cue notes, rehearse it and make one purposeful improvement to your telling.

Learning intention: We are learning to select connected events, tell them clearly from cue notes and explain a rehearsal improvement.

Duration: 60 minutes.

Success criteria:
- I can introduce a character, place and goal.
- I can keep the important events connected.
- I can use cue notes instead of reading every word.
- I can choose a helpful pace, pause or emphasis.
- I can explain an improvement and describe my evidence honestly.

## Teaching 1: Choose the events your listener needs

**Storytelling helps someone follow an event.**
A listener cannot always look back at a page. Introduce the person, situation and goal, then explain the problem, response and outcome. These parts give your telling a route.

**Read and understand before preparing.**
Read the complete original story below. Eli wants a visible finish sign for his game. The wind makes the first arrangement fail. His stand and book change the result. Notice the cause-and-response connection, not only the order of objects.

**Keep important events; reduce small details.**
You do not have to memorise every sentence. A retelling keeps the story’s meaning in your own words. You can shorten a description while preserving the problem and solution. Do not remove the failed first attempt if it explains why Eli tries a different approach.

**Introduce before using pronouns.**
Start with Eli rather than an unexplained he. After the listener knows who he is, he can refer to him clearly. If another person enters, make references clear again.

READ THE COMPLETE ORIGINAL STORY
THE WINDY SIGN
Eli was preparing a treasure-hunt game in the courtyard. He wanted everyone to find the finish beside the garden bed. He wrote FINISH on a card and placed it on a chair.
Before the game began, a gust blew the card face down. Eli could still see the chair, but the players would not know what it marked. He tried standing the card up again. It fell a second time.
Eli looked at the materials on the table. He folded a cardboard stand, slid the card into it and placed a book across the stand’s base. When the next gust came, the word stayed visible. Eli checked the route, then called the players to begin. At the finish, they followed the sign and found the final clue.

I DO:
Beginning: Eli prepares a game and needs a visible finish sign.
Problem: wind blows the card face down; standing it up again fails.
Response: he makes a stand and weights its base.
Outcome: the sign stays visible and the players find the finish.

YOU TRY:
Which event explains why simply standing the card up is not enough?

CHECK:
It falls a second time. Keeping that failed attempt helps a listener understand why Eli changes his plan.

Notice: An object list is not a telling. Include what happens and why the character responds.

**Question:** Which note preserves the story’s main problem?

1. Wind makes the finish card fall face down
2. A list of all colours
3. Only the word courtyard
4. An invented broken bicycle

Reviewer answer: Wind makes the finish card fall face down

Why: The fallen card explains why the players would not recognise the finish and why Eli needs another arrangement.

### Help with this step

1. Reread the goal and the failed first arrangement.
2. Choose actions that explain why the character changes plans.
3. Make a person-and-goal note, then problem, response and outcome notes.

## Teaching 2: Turn event notes into spoken ideas

**A cue is a reminder.**
Cue notes use a few useful words to prompt a fuller idea. “Wind: card falls twice” can remind you to explain the failed arrangement. “Card” alone may be too vague; a full paragraph may tempt you to read every word.

**Use one event per line or box.**
Put the beginning first, then the problem, response and outcome. Leave enough space to find each note at a glance. You can use paper, the printable cue sheet or your usual accessible note format.

**Practise the look-and-tell routine.**
Glance at one cue, then explain that event in a complete idea. Look back for the next cue when needed. You can choose different wording each time if the meaning stays accurate. Looking at a note is useful, not a failure.

**Connect events for the listener.**
Words such as but, so and finally can show the relationship between events. Use them where they help; do not add finally before the problem just because it sounds like a storytelling word.

I DO:
Cue: “Wind: card falls twice.”
Spoken idea: “The wind blew the sign face down. Eli stood it up, but it fell again.”
Cue: “Stand + book.”
Spoken idea: “So Eli made a cardboard stand and put a book across its base.”
The notes prompt the meaning rather than every exact word.

YOU TRY:
Turn “End: visible sign, players finish” into one or two spoken sentences.

CHECK:
One possibility: “The sign stayed visible in the wind. The players followed it to the finish.” Keep the outcome even if your words differ.

Diagram: Two cues prompt complete explanations: wind and two falls explain failure; stand and book explain the response.

Glance → explain → next cue.

Notice: A cue should be short enough to scan and specific enough to remind you of the event.

**Question:** What is the job of a cue note?

1. To replace every story event with one vague word
2. To remind you of an event you will explain
3. To require memorising the original punctuation
4. To change the outcome randomly

Reviewer answer: To remind you of an event you will explain

Why: A cue prompts a fuller spoken idea. It supports memory without requiring a full written script.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at a cue becoming a complete idea.
2. Choose a few words naming the event and its important action.
3. Glance at one note, explain that event, then move to the next note.

## Teaching 3: Choose delivery that supports meaning

### In-site episode: A note becomes a telling

Pip: My cue says stand and book. If I say only those words, will the listener understand the solution?
Juno: Explain the action: Eli makes a stand and puts a book across its base. The cue reminds you; it is not the whole telling.
Pip: I can slow that explanation and keep the important word groups connected.
Juno: Then rehearse the story again. Check both event accuracy and delivery. If you practise alone, describe it as solo practice.
Pip: Pause and turn one short cue into a full idea. Try it twice and explain one useful change.

Learner pause: the final line pauses before continuing.

**Delivery is how you communicate the telling.**
Pace is your speed. A pause is a brief stop. Emphasis makes a chosen word or phrase stand out. Use these choices to help a listener follow an important event, not to show off an acting voice.

**Keep related words together.**
Say “the finish sign” as a connected group. A pause between every word breaks the thought apart. A brief pause after explaining the second failure can give the listener time before the new solution.

**Give the response enough time.**
If you rush “stand and book”, the listener may miss how the solution works. Slow that event a little and clearly say the book weights the base. Speaking loudly does not replace explaining the action.

**Try a choice, then compare.**
Tell one part twice. First use your ordinary delivery; then change one pace, pause or emphasis choice. Ask whether the event becomes easier to understand. Keep the change if it helps. No special accent, dramatic voice or recording is required.

I DO:
I say: “It fell again. [brief pause] So Eli tried a different plan.”
The pause marks the move from repeated failure to a new response. I keep “tried a different plan” connected.
I give “a book across the base” a steady pace so the listener understands how the stand stays put.

YOU TRY:
Tell the response event twice. On the second attempt, slow only the explanation of the book’s place.

CHECK:
Could you hear the connection between the book and the stand? If not, clarify the words before adding more emphasis. Volume alone will not supply the missing relationship.

Notice: Make one purposeful change at a time. You can communicate clearly in your own voice and with your usual support.

**Question:** Which delivery choice supports understanding the solution?

1. Shout every word without explaining
2. Pause inside every small word group
3. Give the stand-and-book explanation a steady pace
4. Invent a louder unrelated event

Reviewer answer: Give the stand-and-book explanation a steady pace

Why: A steady explanation helps the listener follow how the materials solve the problem. The other changes disrupt or replace the meaning.

### Help with this step

1. Look at the pause and steady-pace example.
2. Choose one important event and keep its related words together.
3. Try that event twice, changing one delivery choice to help the meaning.

## Teaching 4: Rehearse, review and improve honestly

**Rehearsal is practice with a purpose.**
Tell the whole story once from your cues. Notice a specific problem: perhaps you skipped the second fall, used an unclear pronoun or rushed the solution. Pick one thing to improve rather than trying to judge everything at once.

**Review meaning and delivery separately.**
Ask: Did I keep the main events accurate? Then ask: Were the words clear and the important event easy to follow? A fluent telling can still miss a key event, and an accurate plan does not guarantee clear delivery.

**Try again and explain the change.**
“I added the failed second attempt so the different solution makes sense” describes a content improvement. “I slowed the solution so the book’s position was clear” describes a delivery improvement. Give a reason, not only “it was better”.

**Describe what was actually produced.**
You can practise independently without a listener or recording. A written cue plan demonstrates preparation. An observed or recorded telling can demonstrate actual delivery. If no one heard it, label your evidence as solo practice and describe what you noticed yourself. Use your usual communication method if speaking is inaccessible.

I DO:
First attempt: I jump from “the sign fell” to “players began”. The solution disappears.
Revision: I add the stand-and-book cue and explain the action.
Second attempt: I slow that explanation and keep “across the base” together.
Reflection: “I restored the solution event and gave it more time so the ending made sense.”

YOU TRY:
Choose one part of your own practice to improve. Tell it again, then describe the difference.

CHECK:
Name an actual event or delivery choice and its purpose. If your first attempt was clear, describe the check that confirmed it instead of inventing an error.

Notice: Written preparation and observed speaking show different skills. Keep the evidence description accurate.

**Question:** Which reflection explains a purposeful improvement?

1. It was better because it was better
2. My notes prove a performance nobody heard
3. I changed every event without checking
4. I restored the solution event so the outcome made sense

Reviewer answer: I restored the solution event so the outcome made sense

Why: The reflection names the change and explains how it helps the listener follow the story. The other statements lack reasoning or misdescribe evidence.

### Help with this step

1. Look back at the separate accuracy and delivery checks.
2. Name one skipped event, unclear reference or rushed explanation.
3. Try a repair, then explain what changed and why; label the evidence you actually made.

## Put it all together

I DO: Prepare and rehearse a complete telling.
THE WINDY SIGN
Eli was preparing a treasure-hunt game in the courtyard. He wanted everyone to find the finish beside the garden bed. He wrote FINISH on a card and placed it on a chair.
Before the game began, a gust blew the card face down. Eli could still see the chair, but the players would not know what it marked. He tried standing the card up again. It fell a second time.
Eli looked at the materials on the table. He folded a cardboard stand, slid the card into it and placed a book across the stand’s base. When the next gust came, the word stayed visible. Eli checked the route, then called the players to begin. At the finish, they followed the sign and found the final clue.

MY FOUR CUES
Eli: finish sign for game
Wind: card falls twice
Response: stand + book across base
Outcome: sign visible, players finish

MY CONNECTED TELLING
“Eli was preparing a treasure hunt and needed a visible finish sign. The wind blew the card face down. He stood it up, but it fell again. So Eli made a cardboard stand and put a book across its base. This time the sign stayed visible. The players followed it to the finish and found the last clue.”

MY DELIVERY PLAN
I introduce Eli before using he. I pause briefly after the second fall, then give the new solution enough time. I keep a book across its base connected so the listener hears how it helps.

COMMON MISTAKE AND FIX
“Eli had a card and a chair and a book” lists objects but loses the response. I replace the list with what he does and how the sign stays visible.

MY REHEARSAL NOTE
On my first solo practice I skipped the second fall. I added it back so the reason for changing plans was clear. I would need an actual observed or recorded telling to claim evidence of delivery beyond my own report.

## Do the task

YOU DO: Storytelling and rehearsal record (about 20–25 minutes).
Use The Windy Sign, included above.
1. Read the complete story. Prepare four short cue notes: beginning, problem, response, outcome. The printable is optional; ordinary paper or typed notes work too.
2. Plan one pace, pause or emphasis choice that supports meaning. Explain its purpose in a short note.
3. Tell the story from the cues in your own words. Keep the important events and use your usual communication support. A listener or recording is optional.
4. Check accuracy and clarity. Choose one useful change and tell it again. If a written communication format is your usual method, practise communicating the connected story in that format and label it.
5. In the big box include your four cues, retelling wording or summary, and the change with its reason. State whether practice was solo, observed, recorded or in a written format.
6. Keep any paper cue sheet for review; an upload is not required.
Check: introduced character and goal; accurate problem and response; connected outcome; helpful cue notes; purposeful delivery plan; explained improvement; honest evidence label.

Reflect: What did your second practice make clearer, and what evidence do you have of that change?
